The evening air brushed the workshop roof with a mild damp coolness that did not rattle the boards, only softened them, and Una stepped through the open doorway while the last pale light slid across the floorboards in long quiet stripes. Una smelled the wood and the faint warmth still rising from the little hearth in the corner, and they wiped their palms on their sweater before looking around. The tools hung neat on a low peg within easy reach of small hands, and the air inside felt gently warmer than the air outside. Una walked farther in and noticed how the light from the window made the metal handles gleam without glare. They stood still for a moment and let the calm of the room settle on their shoulders like a soft shawl.

Papa was already there, kneeling near the hearth where a pair of boots stood upright on a clean cloth, thoroughly dried and no longer muddy or left in a heap by the door. Una saw the careful way the laces had been loosened so the leather could breathe, and they ran one finger along the smooth side of a boot that now felt warm instead of cold and wet. “You dried them all the way,” Una said, voice quiet in the still room. Papa looked up and smiled without hurry. The orange glow from the small fire painted the wall in slow moving shapes, and Una felt the gentle heat on their cheeks as they crouched closer to look. They thought a quick thank-you would feel too thin for something done so kindly without being asked.

Una glanced at the low peg where hammers and brushes waited in their places, then at the wide bench that ran along one wall. They wanted to do one useful thing Papa had not asked for, something that would fit the room the way the dried boots already did. Una pulled a soft cloth from a hook and began wiping a thin film of dust from the bench surface, moving slowly so no scrapes sounded. Under their fingertips they felt a grain of the bench, the fine raised lines of the wood that ran straight and true, and the texture made the work feel steady and real. The cloth picked up the dust in quiet gray swirls, and Una kept going until the whole top looked clean and ready. Papa watched but did not interrupt, and the hearth kept breathing its mild warmth into the space between them.

Outside the open window a faint breeze carried the green scent of tomato vines from the pots Papa kept against the workshop wall, and Una paused to breathe it in before folding the cloth neatly. They set the cloth aside and noticed a small wooden box of spare pegs that had tipped on its side earlier in the day. Una righted the box and sorted the pegs by size so each one lay straight, an extra care no one had requested. “I saw the boots standing dry by the fire,” Una said plainly, looking at Papa. “You took care of them before I even came in.” Papa nodded once, and the soft crackle of a tiny stick settling in the hearth filled the short quiet that followed. Una felt glad the words had come out clear and simple.

A thin bright sound drifted in then, the single clear ring of a cat's collar bell from the yard path, and Una listened while their hands rested on the edge of the bench. The cat did not come inside, only passed by, leaving the workshop still peaceful. Una turned back to the tools on the low peg and checked that each handle hung straight, adjusting one brush that had slipped a little crooked. They did it carefully so the metal did not clink against wood. Papa rose and stretched, the floorboards giving a low friendly creak under his boots. “That looks better already,” Papa said, and Una felt the calm approval settle like warm light across their chest. They kept their movements small and sure, matching the quiet of the room.

Una decided the pegs and the dusting were not quite enough, so they fetched the small broom from its corner and swept the fine shavings that had gathered near the hearth stones into a neat pile, then brushed them into the tin pan Papa used for scraps. The bristles made a soft whispering sound against the floor, and Una worked until every stray curl of wood was gone. They set the pan back in its place and stood the broom upright again. The air near the floor felt cooler than the air higher up, a gentle difference that made Una notice how the fire held its circle of warmth close. “I wanted to help the way you helped with the boots,” Una said, brushing a bit of dust from their sleeve. Papa rested a hand lightly on Una’s shoulder for a moment, and the weight of it felt steady and kind.

They both sat for a little while on the low stools near the bench, and Una rested their elbows on the clean wood while they listened past the near ticking of the small shelf clock, listening instead for the far clock in the house that chimed only the hours. The distant soft tone came faintly through the walls, one deep note that seemed to slow the whole evening. Una breathed out and watched the hearth light flicker without haste. Papa picked up a small block of scrap wood and turned it in his hands, smoothing a rough edge with a scrap of sandpaper so it would not splinter later. The sandpaper made a gentle hiss, and Una watched the pale dust fall onto the cloth below. No one rushed, and the workshop held them both in its quiet circle of tools and warmth and finished boots.

Una stood again and checked the boots once more, making sure the cloth underneath was still dry and tidy, then they folded an extra clean rag and laid it nearby in case Papa needed it tomorrow. They spoke one more time, plain as before. “I noticed you dried them carefully and stood them up,” Una said. Papa answered, “I saw you tidy the bench and the pegs and the floor.” The two sentences sat together in the air like matching pieces, and Una felt a quiet fullness that needed no more words. The light outside had slipped lower, turning the window glass a soft blue, and inside the hearth kept its orange steady. Una rubbed their hands together once and felt the last bit of warmth from the fire on their knuckles.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Una and Papa put the last tools back on the low peg, and the room smelled faintly still of wood and the earlier tomato vines while the cat's distant bell no longer rang. Una touched the dry boots one final time, feeling the leather smooth and ready for morning, then they walked with Papa toward the door where the cooler night air waited without hurry. The far clock chimed again, gentle and far, and Una matched their steps to that slow sound as they left the workshop settled and cared for behind them.
