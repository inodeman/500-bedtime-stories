The warm smell of toasted sesame drifted from the little paper pouch Miss Dalca had opened, and Zola’s hands were already busy folding the soft corners of the blanket so it lay smooth across the ferry bench. Cool evening air brushed Zola’s cheeks while the deck boards felt steady under their shoes. Greta sat close, watching the way Zola patted each fold flat. Through the open window of the tiny cabin beside them, a pale glass of lemon water sat sweating on the sill, beads of moisture catching the low gold light. Miss Dalca wore her cotton apron, and Zola felt the worn fabric when they leaned against her for a moment. “It smells like the bakery near the dock,” Zola said quietly. Greta nodded and tucked one corner of the blanket under her knee so it would not slide.

The ferry rocked in a gentle rhythm, and the water outside made soft lapping sounds against the hull. Zola kept smoothing the blanket until every wrinkle settled, then looked again at the glass on the sill. Cold drops ran down its clear sides and made a small dark ring on the wood. Miss Dalca had poured it for whoever felt thirsty first, yet Zola’s eyes moved from Greta to the single glass and back again. The light through the cabin window turned the lemon slices into tiny moons. Zola’s fingers rested on the folded edge of the blanket, feeling its familiar thickness. “Only one glass is waiting there,” Zola said, voice soft as the breeze. Greta tilted her head, listening, while Miss Dalca stood nearby without hurry.

Zola thought about how the cold drink would taste after the warm sesame snack, and how the fairness of one glass for only one person did not sit right in their chest. The bench held all three of them easily, the blanket a quiet square of comfort under their legs. From somewhere on the dock they had left, a gate latch clicked once in the distance, a small metal sound that faded into the water. Zola rose carefully, crossed the short space to the sill, and lifted the sweating glass with both hands so nothing spilled. The glass felt slick and cool against their palms. Miss Dalca watched but did not reach out. Greta stayed on the bench, knees drawn up, eyes bright with curiosity.

Back at the bench, Zola set the glass down on the flat middle of the blanket where the light could still touch it. “We can make smaller even shares,” Zola told Greta, and the words felt calm and sure. Miss Dalca’s cotton apron brushed Zola’s shoulder as she leaned to see, the cloth soft and slightly flour-dusted from earlier. Zola found the two little tin cups Miss Dalca kept in the picnic basket and wiped their rims with a clean corner of napkin. The lemon water caught the fading sun and threw pale yellow sparks across the blanket weave. Greta held out both hands, ready, and the ferry horn stayed quiet so the only sounds were water and breath.

Zola poured slowly, matching the levels until each tin cup held the same clear amount, lemon wheels floating like twin boats. A thin cool film of water remained on Zola’s fingers, and they wiped it on the napkin before offering the first cup to Greta. “This one is yours, and this one is mine,” Zola said. Greta took the cup with both palms and smiled without speaking yet. Miss Dalca settled onto the far end of the bench, the blanket shifting just enough to stay neat. The glass on the sill now stood empty except for a few bright drops, and the air still carried a faint toasted sesame warmth from the open pouch. Everything felt even and unhurried.

Greta sipped and made a small happy sound at the sharp sweet cold of the lemon. Zola tasted theirs next and felt the chill travel down in a pleasant line. Miss Dalca rested her hands in the lap of her cotton apron and simply nodded once, letting the choice stand without any speech at all. The ferry glided forward, and the light on the water turned from gold to soft blue. Zola tucked the empty glass back toward the sill so it would not tip, then smoothed the blanket again where a corner had lifted. Greta scooted closer so their shoulders almost touched. The bench held them like a quiet boat of its own.

Miss Dalca offered the last of the sesame pieces, and Zola and Greta each took one, the crunch gentle between their teeth. The folded blanket stayed warm from their sitting, a steady texture under knees and palms. Zola watched the empty glass on the sill catch the last pink of sky and felt glad the shares had been made small and equal. “Thank you for waiting with me,” Zola said to Greta. Greta answered, “I liked the even cups best.” Miss Dalca’s smile was quiet as the water. No one needed more words. The ferry moved on, steady and slow, while the cabin window framed the sill like a picture.

Later the sky deepened, and a small lantern was lit inside the cabin so the glass on the sill held a gentle circle of light. Zola set the two tin cups side by side on the blanket to dry, then helped Greta shake the crumbs into the paper pouch. The air cooled another degree, pleasant against bare arms. Miss Dalca folded her cotton apron edges neatly across her knees. Zola’s hands found the blanket corners once more and made certain they lay square. Everything that needed sharing had already been shared. The water outside whispered against the ferry in the same calm pattern it had kept all evening.

Zola leaned back against the bench rail and felt the day settle into quiet. Greta’s breathing slowed to match the gentle rock of the boat. Miss Dalca kept watch without fuss, the lantern glow touching the sill and the empty glass alike. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Zola closed their eyes for a moment and heard only the water and the far hush of evening, the blanket warm beneath them, the last trace of lemon cool on their tongue, and the sure knowledge that both cups had been filled the same.
