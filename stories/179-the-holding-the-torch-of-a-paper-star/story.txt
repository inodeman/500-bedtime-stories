Nana Ruth sat on the low porch step with a stack of pale paper already half folded in her lap, her fingers pressing neat creases while the last warm light of evening settled like honey on the boards. Dori stood close enough to feel the gentle heat rising from the wood and smell the faint sweetness of pear skins drifting from the bowl Nana Ruth had set aside. “We will walk when these stars are ready,” Nana Ruth said in her quiet voice, and Dori watched the paper take shape under those careful hands. The air held a soft glow that made every edge of the porch look kind. Dori touched the finished star Nana Ruth handed them and felt how light it was, almost like holding a piece of moonlight.

They stepped onto the evening lane together, the paper star resting in Dori’s palms, and the path stretched ahead under lanterns hung from low branches. Milo waited near the first fig tree, swinging his own small lantern so that golden circles danced across the dust. A breeze moved through the leaves overhead, and Dori heard the wind in the fig leaves like a slow soft rustle that seemed to walk beside them. Nana Ruth kept an easy pace, never hurrying, and the cool evening air brushed Dori’s cheeks while the paper star warmed slightly against their skin. “Look how the lights make a river,” Milo whispered, and Dori nodded, staying near Nana Ruth’s side where the ground felt steady under their shoes.

Farther along the lane the neighbors had gathered in a loose gentle circle, each person ready to lift something bright when their turn came. Dori’s fingers tightened a little on the paper star, and they felt the shy feeling rise like a small cool cloud inside their chest. The lantern light painted soft orange shapes on the stones, and Dori could hear quiet laughter mixed with the far-off call of a bird settling for night. Nana Ruth did not rush them forward. She simply stood close, her shawl smelling faintly of clean cotton, and let Dori take in the scene at their own speed. Dori noticed how the stars and lanterns glowed without any need to shout.

When the circle grew quieter, it became clear that each child would hold up a paper star so the light could catch it for a moment. Dori’s turn drew nearer, and they pressed their free hand into their pocket where a warm coin rested against their palm, smooth and familiar. The shy feeling stayed, yet Dori remembered Nana Ruth’s calm face and the way the evening air still felt friendly on their arms. Milo gave a small encouraging smile from across the circle, and the lantern flames made tiny bright sparks that looked almost like they were dancing in place. Dori breathed in slowly and decided they could try the smallest brave version without needing to be loud or big.

Dori lifted the paper star just high enough for the nearest lantern light to touch its points, and in a voice that could be heard they said, “Here is my star.” The words came out clear and steady though not large, and the paper caught a soft golden shine that made the star seem to float for a heartbeat. Nana Ruth’s eyes crinkled with quiet gladness, and she did not ask for anything more. The circle stayed peaceful, and someone farther along the lane answered with a gentle “Thank you, Dori,” so the sound of their own name floated back warm as the light. Dori felt the cool evening settle around their shoulders like a soft blanket while the star glowed in their raised hands.

Nana Ruth leaned close and spoke only for Dori. “That was enough, and it was lovely,” she said, her tone kind and free of any push for a bigger show. Dori lowered the star carefully and felt the paper’s thin edges against their fingertips, still warm from the lantern glow. Milo stepped nearer and offered a quiet high-five that made a soft pap sound in the air. The lane smelled clean and leafy, and the light from all the hanging lanterns pooled on the ground in calm puddles of gold. Dori walked on with Nana Ruth and Milo, the shy feeling smaller now, replaced by the easy comfort of simply being there among the lights.

They continued a little farther where the lane curved past more fig trees and low fences, and Dori kept the paper star close without needing to hide it. The temperature of the air had cooled just enough to make the lantern warmth feel even more welcome on their face. Nana Ruth pointed out a moth that fluttered near one of the lights, its wings soft as dust in the glow. “The stars look like they are keeping each other company,” Dori said, surprised by how easy the words felt this time. Milo answered, “They do,” and the three of them stood still for a moment listening to the quiet hum of the evening settling deeper around the path.

On the way home the lane grew quieter still, and Dori held Nana Ruth’s hand while the paper star rested safely in their other palm. The last of the hanging lanterns threw long gentle shadows that moved slowly across the stones like friendly shapes. Dori could feel the smooth wood of Nana Ruth’s porch step again under their feet when they finally reached the house, and inside the rooms smelled faintly of the pear skins left from earlier. They straightened the rug corner with a toe so it lay flat and neat, a small calm motion that matched the soft quiet of the house. Nana Ruth set a single lantern near the window where it could watch the dark outside, and Dori placed their paper star on the table beside it so both could rest.

Dori sat on the cushion by the window with Nana Ruth close by, the room holding a gentle hush broken only by the distant settling of the house. The paper star caught a little of the indoor light and seemed to smile with its five bright points. Milo had gone home with a wave, and now only the softest evening sounds remained. Dori felt the last of the outdoor coolness leave their cheeks while the indoor air wrapped them in quiet warmth. Nana Ruth hummed a tiny tune without words, and Dori leaned against her side, eyes growing heavy in the kindest way. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
