The smell of warm bread drifted through the open cottage door while Greta’s busy hands already sorted the small glass jars along the wide windowsill, turning each one so the labels faced outward in the soft afternoon light. Warm air brushed their wrists as they slid a tray of pale drying wool a little farther from the edge, careful not to tip anything. Greta paused only long enough to breathe in that cozy kitchen scent again before reaching for another jar of buttons and pins. Outside the window the garden waited quietly, and inside the sill glowed with all its jars, trays, and soft hanging wool. Greta liked the gentle clink of glass against wood and kept their fingers moving in slow, neat circles until every jar stood straight.

Greta’s eyes found the deep indigo bowl near the middle of the sill, where a plump skein of wool rested half-hidden under a clean cloth, and they felt a quick wish to lift the cover and check the color. The bowl sat among the trays like a quiet island of blue, catching a thin stripe of sunlight that made the ceramic shine. Greta leaned closer and saw only the edge of the cloth, still damp in places from the dye bath earlier. They remembered the ferryman had said it needed more time, yet curiosity tugged at their sleeves. Greta’s fingertips hovered just above the cloth while the room stayed still and warm around them.

The ferryman stood a few steps behind Greta, his coat smelling faintly of river water, and he watched the windowsill with calm eyes. “The wool is still drinking its blue,” he said in a low voice that matched the hush of the cottage. Greta turned and saw him smile. “I know you want to look, Greta,” he added, “but the color finishes best when no one peeks too soon.” Greta nodded slowly, feeling the soft afternoon air on their cheeks. The ferryman rested one hand on the back of a wooden chair and waited without hurry, as if the whole house moved at the same gentle pace as the river he knew so well.

Greta drew their hand back and spoke softly. “I will leave the cloth right where it is,” they said. The ferryman answered, “That is a kind choice for the wool.” Greta felt the cool ceramic rim of a nearby empty jar under their fingers as they steadied themselves, the smooth glaze surprising and pleasant against their skin. They pressed the cloth more firmly over the indigo bowl so no light could slip inside, then stepped away one quiet pace. The windowsill still held its jars and trays and drying wool, all peaceful in the golden light that slanted across the floorboards.

Instead of lifting the cover, Greta chose a quiet task that fit the room. They gathered three empty trays from the far end of the sill and carried them one by one to the low shelf near the sink, stacking them with soft scrapes of wood. Greta wiped each tray with a dry cloth until the surface felt clean under their palms, then returned to the windowsill and aligned the remaining jars once more. The ferryman watched without speaking, and Greta felt glad for the small useful work that kept their hands busy while the skein rested. Warm light from the west window touched Greta’s hair as they worked, and the cottage stayed filled with the easy sounds of settling wood and distant birds.

After a while Greta sat on the low stool beside the sill and listened. Outside, the wind in the fig leaves made a slow rustling that rose and fell like quiet breathing. Greta tilted their head and let the sound settle into the room along with the fading bread scent. The ferryman settled into the chair and said, “The river teaches the same patience the dye does.” Greta answered, “I can wait with it.” Their voice stayed soft, and they rested both hands in their lap while the fig leaves whispered on. Time stretched gently; the light on the trays shifted from gold to softer amber, and Greta watched dust motes drift without needing to hurry anything along.

Greta rose again and checked the other drying wool with careful fingertips, feeling how some strands had grown springy and light. They moved a single tray an inch closer to the fresher air near the open window, then stepped back so the indigo bowl remained undisturbed. The ferryman stood and joined them at the sill for a moment. “You have given it exactly what it needs,” he said. Greta smiled and replied, “It will tell us when it is ready.” They both stayed quiet after that, simply sharing the warm room and the view of the garden path that led down toward the water. Greta’s shoulders felt loose and easy, and the temperature of the air cooled just enough to make the indoors feel cozy.

Later the light grew pink along the edges of the jars, and Greta noticed the cloth over the indigo bowl no longer looked damp. They waited one more stretch of time, counting slow breaths while the ferryman trimmed the wick of a small lantern on the side table. Greta’s hands folded the extra cloths into neat squares and set them beside the trays. Everything on the windowsill seemed to rest together—the jars, the trays, the drying wool, and the covered bowl—as if the whole cottage agreed that waiting could feel as gentle as a held breath. Greta felt the last warmth of the day on their bare feet against the floorboards and knew the skein was nearly finished in its own quiet way.

When the cloth finally lifted under Greta’s careful fingers, the skein shone a deep even blue that caught the evening light like still water. Greta and the ferryman both smiled without needing more words. Greta touched the wool lightly and found it cool and ready, then laid the cloth aside and left the beautiful color resting open in its bowl among the jars and trays. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Greta stayed close to the sill a little longer, breathing the calm air and feeling the day settle into night around the cottage and the patient blue wool.
