"These boats will not come free," said Neighbor Sami, kneeling low beside the wooden street model.  
Pema stopped in the middle of the school hallway and turned toward the gentle voice. Late sunlight drifted through the high windows in soft golden bands that warmed Pema’s cheeks and made the floorboards glow the color of honey. Coat pegs stretched along one wall holding a few empty jackets that hung still as sleeping birds, while wooden stools waited in a loose circle near the gleaming music stands whose metal legs caught little sparks of light. Pema heard the faint creak of a stool leg when a breeze touched it, and the air itself felt mild and settled, smelling faintly of the wax used to shine the long corridor. The paper boats sat wedged deep inside the dry gutter that ran beside the painted curb of the model town, a class project left out so everyone could admire the tiny houses and the empty channel where water had once been pretended. No flood had ever filled it; the gutter stayed dusty and dry after the morning craft time ended, yet the bright folded boats had slipped in and caught their corners on the little wooden ridges. Neighbor Sami tugged one boat with careful fingers, then another, but the dry gutter kept them snug, and the work looked wide for a single pair of hands.

Pema walked closer, shoes whispering over the smooth boards, and watched Neighbor Sami’s elbows rise and fall with quiet effort. The hallway felt roomy and peaceful, the music stands standing like tall silver trees that threw slender shadows across the stools. Pema noticed how the paper boats, some blue and some yellow, sat jammed one behind another as if they had decided to rest together in that narrow dry place. A soft draft moved past Pema’s ankles, cool against bare skin, and carried the distant sound of wind in the fig leaves outside the open window at the far end. Neighbor Sami glanced up with a small tired smile but said nothing more, only reached again for a boat whose sail had folded flat against the gutter wall. Pema felt the warm air of the hallway press gently on their arms and understood at once that the job would go faster with two.

Without waiting for any invitation, Pema knelt on the other side of the model so that the dry gutter lay between them like a tiny quiet riverbed. The wood of the floor pressed smooth and solid under Pema’s knees, and the light from the window painted a bright square right across the paper boats. “I can free the ones on this end,” Pema said in a low voice that matched the calm of the hallway. Neighbor Sami nodded once, still concentrating, and the two of them began to work in easy silence broken only by the soft rustle of paper. Pema’s fingers found the edge of a yellow boat and eased it sideways until the stuck corner loosened with a tiny paper sigh. The air near the model carried a faint sweetness, the smell of pear skins left from someone’s afternoon snack on a nearby stool, and Pema breathed it in while sliding the boat free and setting it carefully on the painted street.

Neighbor Sami lifted a blue boat from the far side and held it up so the light shone through its thin walls. “They look happier already,” Neighbor Sami said, and Pema answered, “We can set them in a neat line when they are all out.” Together they kept going, Pema taking every other boat so the work split itself evenly without any fuss. The dry gutter slowly emptied its treasure, and each freed boat felt light as a leaf in Pema’s palm. Music stands cast their thin cool shadows across Pema’s hands, and the temperature of the hallway stayed gentle, never too warm, never chilly. Pema’s shoulder brushed the edge of a stool once, feeling its worn rounded corner, and then they returned to the next boat whose sail had crumpled against a tiny bridge.

A warm coin rested in Pema’s pocket from lunchtime, and when Pema shifted weight the metal pressed its smooth round face against their leg like a small friendly reminder of ordinary days. Pema slid another boat free and lined it beside the others while Neighbor Sami freed two more. The paper made soft whispering sounds as it left the dry gutter, and the hallway itself seemed to lean in with its quiet wooden walls and rows of pegs. Outside the window the same light kept pouring, steady and kind, and Pema felt the quiet satisfaction of hands working side by side. No one asked for thanks; the boats simply rose one by one into the open air of the model street.

“Almost finished,” Neighbor Sami murmured, and Pema replied, “Just three left on my side.” They moved slower now, careful not to crease any sail, and the last boats came free with patient tugs. Pema set them in a gentle row beside the tiny wooden shops, and the dry gutter lay empty and clean at last, its wooden bottom showing faint pencil marks from the students who had built it. The stools nearby held the faint scent of pear skins still, and the music stands stood tall and unused, their silver tops catching the lowering light. Pema sat back on their heels for a moment, feeling the solid floor and the mild air, while Neighbor Sami gathered the boats into a shallow basket that had waited under one peg.

Niko appeared at the hallway corner carrying a stack of song sheets and paused to watch the neat line of paper boats. “They look ready for a still pond someday,” Niko said softly, then continued past the music stands without lingering. Pema and Neighbor Sami shared a quiet glance that needed no words, the kind that simply says the work is done. Pema stood and brushed a speck of dry dust from their sleeve, feeling the fabric soft against their wrist. The hallway lights had not yet clicked on, so the natural glow from the windows kept everything gentle and gold. Neighbor Sami placed the basket on a low stool where the boats could rest safely overnight.

Pema noticed a rug corner near the coat pegs that had folded over itself during the afternoon footsteps, and they straightened it with one careful toe until the edge lay flat again. The simple motion felt right in the peaceful space, and Neighbor Sami smiled without speaking. All the paper boats now sat free and tidy, no longer stuck in the dry gutter of the model that belonged so naturally among the pegs and music stands. Pema walked a few steps toward the tall window, listening to the last hush of the hallway, and felt how the air cooled just a little as evening came closer. The stools and stands kept their quiet places, and the whole corridor seemed ready for rest.

Pema rested one hand on the cool windowsill and looked out at the soft darkening yard. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Neighbor Sami carried the basket of boats toward the art shelf, and Pema followed a little way before turning for the door, shoes quiet on the boards, ready for the calm walk home under a sky that held the last pale light.
