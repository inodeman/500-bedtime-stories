The ferryman was already settled deep in the library window seat when Sora climbed up beside him, the long cushions cool against their bare legs and the afternoon light pouring warm and buttery across both their laps. He held a thin stack of cream-colored papers and read from them in a low steady voice, practicing each sentence as if the words themselves needed gentle steering. Sora watched his mouth shape the phrases and felt the soft weight of the seat pillows press against their back. Outside the tall glass the river moved slow and silver, and the air inside carried a faint sweet scent of honey from the tea the librarian had left on a nearby table. Sora wanted already to speak of the bright blue feather they had found that morning, yet they stayed quiet and listened.

“The substitute must check the ropes twice before the first crossing,” the ferryman said, tapping the top page with one careful finger, “and must keep the lantern filled even if the sky looks clear.” His voice stayed calm and even, the same way he spoke when the water was smooth. Sora leaned closer so the fabric of his coat brushed their arm and they could hear every soft syllable. The light through the window turned the edges of the papers the color of warm milk. Sora’s own news pressed against their tongue like a bubble wanting to pop, but they swallowed it and let the ferryman continue without a single interruption.

He turned the next page with a rustle that sounded almost like dry leaves and kept going. “If fog comes the substitute will ring the little bell three times so the far bank knows the boat is coming,” he practiced aloud, “and will never hurry the oars when the current shifts.” Sora sat very still, feeling the cool glass of the window against one shoulder and the gentle warmth of the sun on the other. Their bigger news about the feather and the funny way it had spun in the wind still waited inside them, yet they chose to keep it waiting. The ferryman’s words filled the quiet corner of the library like a slow boat itself, and Sora followed every one.

“The passengers will sometimes bring baskets that smell of bread,” the ferryman went on, eyes still on the page, “and the substitute must make room for every basket and every small hand that reaches for the rail.” Sora nodded though he was not looking, and the cushions under them gave a tiny friendly sigh. Far away in the garden a gate latch clicked once with a bright metallic sound that drifted through the open side window, then silence returned. Sora’s fingers traced the seam of the seat cushion while they listened all the way through that careful sentence and the next. The urge to jump in with their own story rose again and fell away like a wave that never quite reached the shore.

He read the following lines even more slowly, almost as if he were teaching the paper itself how to speak. “When the moon is new the substitute will keep extra blankets folded under the bench,” he said, “and will offer them without being asked if anyone shivers.” Sora felt the temperature of the seat change where the sunlight had moved a little higher, leaving a cooler stripe across their knees. They folded their hands in their lap and watched the way the ferryman’s thumb rested on the margin of the page. The bigger news stayed locked behind their teeth. Listening felt like holding a small smooth stone, solid and quiet and worth keeping still for.

The ferryman reached the final paragraph of the instructions and practiced it twice so the rhythm would stay in his mind. “At the end of every day the substitute will write three true sentences about the crossings in the green notebook,” he said, “and will leave the notebook on the same shelf so the next morning begins easy.” He looked up at last and smiled at Sora, the light catching the silver threads in his hair. Sora waited through the last full stop and the soft silence that followed. Only then did they feel the bubble of their own words begin to loosen. The library air stayed gentle and still around them both.

Sora tilted their head and asked the one real question that had grown while they listened. “Does the substitute ever get to choose a new path across the water if the old one feels too familiar?” Their voice was soft so it would not startle the quiet. The ferryman considered the window and the river beyond it, and the scent of honey rose again from the cooling teacup nearby. He rested his free hand on the seat between them, and Sora noticed again how chalky that hand felt, dry and powdery from the morning’s chart work along the dock. The texture stayed under their fingertips for a moment when they brushed it by accident.

“Yes,” the ferryman answered, “the substitute may choose any safe path as long as everyone arrives gently and the boat comes home whole.” He folded the papers once more and slipped them into his coat. Sora breathed out and finally shared the small thing they had decided to keep until the end. “I found a blue feather on the path this morning and it smelled like rain,” they said, holding out their empty palm as if the feather still rested there. The ferryman nodded as though that news mattered just as much as any instruction. Outside a single clock chime sounded from the hall and both of them waited through it without speaking until the last vibration faded.

Sora rested their head against the window frame while the ferryman tucked the instructions safely away. The cushions had grown warmer where their bodies had sat so long, and the late light turned the river outside to pale gold. They felt glad they had waited, glad the bigger news could wait a little longer still. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
