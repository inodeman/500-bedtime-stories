"Will you walk with me when the lanterns start?" Vera asked, their voice gentle as warm evening light spilled gold across the meadow gate where the first games always began.

The grass near the posts felt cool under Vera's shoes, and the air carried a clean brightness after the afternoon wash. Mr. Ade stood a little back from the gate with a quiet smile, holding a basket of small paper lanterns that glowed like tiny moons once the wicks were lit. Uma waited just outside the fun, close enough to hear the happy chatter yet not quite inside the circle, fingers resting on the wooden rail as if the gate itself might decide. Vera watched Uma's shoulders and noticed how still they stayed while other children paired off without looking around. The lantern walk was meant to be done in pairs, everyone knew, so each walker could share the path and the soft glow, yet the pairs had already formed in a rush of giggles and quick steps. Vera's own lantern sat warm in their hands, the paper sides smooth and the little flame steady, and they felt the pull to fix the empty space without making a big show.

Mr. Ade only tilted his head and let the children sort the evening themselves, the way he always did when the meadow gate filled with plans. Vera breathed in and caught the faint smell of lemon soap still clinging to their own wrists from the careful wash before they came outside. They stepped closer to Uma and spoke low so the words belonged only to the two of them. "The walk goes better if we change the pace," Vera said. "We can go side by side from the very first post, and no one needs to call out seats or prizes." Uma's eyes lifted, bright with surprise and something softer, and the wind in the fig leaves rustled once like a quiet answer from the trees beyond the fence. Vera shifted their lantern so the light touched both of them at once, and the shared glow made the gate posts look friendlier than before.

They did not rearrange the whole line with loud announcements. Instead Vera simply slowed the starting steps and opened a real place beside them, wide enough for Uma's shoulder and quiet enough that belonging felt ordinary. "Here," Vera murmured, "we keep the same lantern between us and match our feet to the grass." Uma nodded and took the offered edge of the light, and together they passed under the meadow gate while the other pairs drifted ahead at their own easy speed. Mr. Ade watched from the shade without a single correction, trusting the small change Vera had made. The path beyond the gate smelled of evening earth, and the lanterns bobbed like slow fireflies as the children moved in twos across the open field.

Halfway along the gentle loop Vera felt the floured towel in the picnic basket bump their knee when they paused to adjust the wick, the cloth still dusted soft from the bread Mr. Ade had wrapped earlier. Uma laughed under her breath at the unexpected powdery touch, and Vera laughed too, the sound small and bright in the dusk. "We can rest the lantern on the towel for a moment if the handle grows warm," Vera offered, and they did, setting the glowing paper carefully so the flame stayed safe. The pause let the slower pairs catch the same stretch of path, and suddenly the walk felt less like a race and more like a shared ribbon of light. No one pointed or praised out loud; the new pace simply held, and Uma walked inside it as if the place had always been waiting.

Mr. Ade joined them only long enough to hand around cups of cool water from a jug, the glass sides beaded with droplets that caught the lantern shine. Vera wiped a drop from Uma's cup with the edge of their sleeve and kept the conversation soft. "Tomorrow we can start the games from this gate again," Vera said, "and we will remember to leave room from the first moment." Uma answered, "I like walking when the light is shared like this," and the words settled between them like another quiet step. The meadow stretched wide and kind under the early stars, and every pair of lanterns made its own small island of gold without crowding the others. Vera felt the night air cool against their cheeks and knew the change they had chosen needed no speech beyond the walking itself.

When the loop brought them back toward the gate the children gathered the lanterns into a careful row on the low stone wall. Vera and Uma set theirs down together so the glows touched and made one longer shape. Mr. Ade nodded once, pleased in the silent way that needed no prize list or loud cheer. The last games of the evening were simple ones of gentle tossing and soft counting, and Vera made sure the circle opened wide enough that Uma sat fully inside it from the start. The grass under everyone smelled sweeter now that the lantern walk had finished, and the fig trees beyond the fence held their dark leaves still. Vera's hands felt warm from the shared handle, and the warmth stayed even after the paper lights rested.

Inside the little house by the meadow they washed the cups and set the basket on the sideboard. Vera took a clean cloth and began to wipe the table in slow circles, the wood growing smoother under each pass while Uma stacked the plates with careful clinks. Mr. Ade hummed a tune without words, the sound low as the last light left the sky outside. The room smelled of the finished day and the safe indoor air, and Vera's eyelids grew heavy in the kindest way. They spoke once more before the quiet deepened. "I am glad we walked together," Vera said, and Uma answered with a tired, happy nod that needed no extra sentence.

Later Vera settled onto the low bed near the window while the house settled around them in soft creaks and gentle drafts. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the meadow gate waited for morning games, and inside the room held the memory of paired light, lemon-clean hands, fig-leaf whispers, and the powdery towel that had steadied their pause, all of it folded into the calm that carried Vera toward sleep with Uma's friendship still warm in the quiet dark.
