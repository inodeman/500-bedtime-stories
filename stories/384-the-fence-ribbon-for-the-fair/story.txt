Soft evening air drifted across the harbor dock and brushed the wooden planks with a mild coolness that smelled faintly of salt and distant gardens. Anil stood near the edge where the baskets rose one by one from the waiting boats, their woven sides brushing against the rail as helpful hands passed them upward into the soft gold light of late day. Anil watched the gentle motion of the water and felt the smooth warmth of the railing under their palms while the fair ribbon waited in a neat coil beside a stack of empty crates. The ribbon had been meant to mark a gentle path along the dock so visitors could stroll safely past the busy loading spots without bumping anyone. Anil breathed in the calm air and listened to the quiet lap of waves against the pilings.

Anil called softly to Haru, who was already kneeling near the first post with the free end of the fair ribbon in both hands. “We should stretch it carefully so it stays even all the way,” Anil said, and Haru nodded while the cool breeze lifted a few loose strands of ribbon. The light from the low sun painted pale stripes across the boards and warmed the tops of Anil’s shoes. Nearby, Uncle Idris set down a small tin of dried flowers and smiled without hurrying anyone. Anil could smell the faint chamomile rising from the open tin as Uncle Idris checked the posts that would hold the ribbon. The dock felt steady under Anil’s feet, and the baskets kept rising with soft scrapes and little thumps that never grew loud.

Anil and Haru both knew the fair ribbon would twist or sag if anyone pulled too fast or tried to finish alone. Anil ran one finger along the smooth edge of the ribbon and felt how light it was, almost like a soft leaf. “If we rush the tying, the path will look crooked tomorrow,” Anil told Haru in a quiet voice. Haru agreed at once and suggested they divide the work into two slow jobs so nothing jerked free. The air stayed mild, and a thin shaft of sunlight rested on Anil’s shoulder like a friendly hand. Uncle Idris only watched and waited, ready to help with words rather than hurry.

Anil took charge of guiding the long middle stretch while Haru minded the starting post and the first gentle loop. Together they decided Anil would walk the ribbon forward a little at a time and Haru would keep the free end from tangling. The wooden planks felt dry and slightly ridged under Anil’s socks when they slipped their shoes off to move more quietly. A soft creak came from the nearest boat as another basket rose, and Anil paused so the ribbon would not snag on anyone’s sleeve. The temperature of the air stayed kind, neither hot nor chilly, and Anil felt glad they had chosen the slower way.

Uncle Idris cleared his throat gently and offered only a calm count and the proper name for the knot they would need at each post. “This is called the harbor loop,” he said, “and you hold it for three soft beats before you draw it closed.” Anil repeated the name under their breath and practiced the three quiet counts while Haru watched. The sound of a tin clock came faintly from the little shed at the far end of the dock, ticking in a steady friendly rhythm that matched the slow pace they had chosen. Anil smiled because the clock made the work feel like a peaceful song rather than a race. Light from the open water glittered on the ribbon and made tiny bright dots dance across Anil’s wrists.

Anil stepped forward with the middle of the ribbon held loosely so it never pulled tight too soon. Haru stayed at the first post and shaped the harbor loop exactly as Uncle Idris had named it, counting the three soft beats aloud in a low voice. “One, two, three,” Haru whispered, and Anil answered, “That looks perfect from here.” The texture of a tin lid met Anil’s hand when they steadied themselves against a nearby crate that still held its shiny cover; the metal felt cool and smooth and reassuring. Baskets continued to rise in an easy rhythm, and the air carried the mild scent of wood warmed by the day’s sun. Anil kept their steps small so the ribbon stayed level and bright.

Anil and Haru traded places halfway along the dock so each could rest their arms and keep the work gentle. Anil now shaped the harbor loop at the middle posts while Haru walked the free length forward. “We are doing fine if we stay slow,” Anil said, and Haru replied, “The path already looks kind and clear.” Uncle Idris offered the count again whenever they reached a new post, never taking the ribbon himself, only speaking the name and the three soft beats. Soft shadows lengthened across the planks and cooled the wood just enough that Anil felt comfortable kneeling. The distant tin clock kept its quiet ticking, and Anil matched their breathing to it without thinking.

When the final stretch of ribbon reached the last post, Anil held the end steady and Haru formed the harbor loop with careful fingers. Uncle Idris spoke the count one last time in his warm low voice, and both children drew the knot closed on the third beat. The finished ribbon shone in a long gentle curve that marked the safe walking path beside the places where baskets still rose. Anil brushed a bit of dock dust from their knees and felt the evening air turn softer against their cheeks. Haru gave a quiet laugh of pure relief, and Anil answered with a soft smile that needed no words. Everything had stayed peaceful because they had shared the two slow jobs.

Anil sat for a moment on a low crate beside Haru while Uncle Idris coiled the leftover ribbon into a neat circle. The three of them watched the last baskets of the evening rise without hurry, and Anil felt the solid dock beneath them like a quiet friend. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Anil rested their hands in their lap and listened to the tiny waves, happy that the fair path was ready and that every careful step had been shared.
