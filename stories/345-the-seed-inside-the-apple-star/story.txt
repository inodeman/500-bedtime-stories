"Pilar, come stand by the meadow fence with me," Tala said, their voice calm in the soft evening air. The low sun poured warm honey-colored light across the wooden rails and the tall grass beyond, and Tala felt the last heat of the day still resting on their shoulders. Beside the fence sat a sturdy bucket of water that caught the light like a quiet mirror. Auntie Luz had carried the bucket and a small basket of apples from the house, and now she waited a few steps away while Tala and Pilar explored the edge of the meadow. Tala rested both palms on their knees for a moment, breathing in the open space, and watched a few pale moths drift above the clover. The wooden fence felt solid under one hand when they reached out to steady themselves. Everything around them seemed gentle and ready for looking.

Tala lifted an apple from the basket and held it near the bucket so they could rinse its smooth red skin. Cool water dripped over their fingers and made tiny clear sounds against the metal rim. Pilar leaned in close, and together they turned the apple slowly, noticing its freckles and the way the wet surface gleamed. Auntie Luz had already cut one apple cleanly across the middle earlier at home and wrapped the halves in a scrap of cloth, and now Tala unwrapped them with careful hands. The cut face showed a perfect star of dark seeds nestled in the pale flesh. Tala's eyes widened at the neat pointed shape waiting right there in the middle. The evening air cooled a little against their damp wrists as they held the half apple steady.

A faint click of a gate latch sounded somewhere behind the meadow fence, soft and far, as if a neighbor closed a garden door for the night. Tala kept their attention on the star of seeds and felt a quick wish rise to pocket the pretty half or poke one seed with a fingertip just to see it move. Instead they stayed still and spoke quietly to Pilar. "See the star inside," Tala said. "We can look without touching the seeds at all." Pilar nodded, and both children bent their heads closer while the last warm light painted the apple flesh a soft cream color. The water in the bucket nearby gave off a clean mineral scent that mixed with the grass. Tala's bare toes pressed into the cool earth, grounding them while they studied every point of the seed star.

Tala tilted the apple half so the star caught the fading light and showed Pilar how the five seeds formed their quiet pattern. "If we leave every seed right where it sits, the star stays whole for anyone who finds it later," Tala told them. Pilar breathed softly and kept their hands folded, watching the seeds gleam like tiny polished stones. Auntie Luz stepped nearer, her skirt brushing the fence rails with a gentle swish. The air had grown cooler still, and a pale moth landed for a second on the bucket handle before lifting away. Tala felt proud of the careful way they all shared the view without any hurry. The half apple rested lightly in their open palm, its cut surface cool and smooth.

Auntie Luz leaned in and looked at the seed star with the same quiet attention. "It looks like a secret the apple kept until we cut it open," Auntie Luz said, her tone warm and slow. Tala smiled and held the apple a little higher so the light could reach the center again. Pilar whispered, "I like how the points all meet in the middle." The three of them stood together by the meadow fence while the bucket of water reflected the soft sky. Tala noticed how the wooden rails smelled faintly of sun-warmed timber and how the grass tips brushed their ankles with a feathery touch. No one reached for the seeds. The wish to pocket or poke had already drifted away like the moth.

Tala remembered the scrap of fresh paper Auntie Luz sometimes carried for drawing, and for a moment the clean bright smell of it rose in their mind as if the paper were still in the basket. They set the thought aside and focused on the real apple in their hands. The star of seeds stayed perfect and undisturbed. Pilar shifted closer so their shoulders almost touched, and together the two children traced the outline of the star with their eyes alone. Auntie Luz rested one hand on the top rail of the fence and watched them with a peaceful face. The temperature of the air kept slipping cooler, inviting slower breaths. Tala felt the solid ground under their feet and the quiet company of their friend and their auntie all around the simple discovery.

Near the fence a low border of garden earth rose like a short damp clay wall, cool and slightly rough when Tala brushed it once with the back of their hand. The texture surprised them in a pleasant way, soft yet firm, and they smiled before turning back to the apple. "We can leave the star right here on the fence rail so the evening can keep it," Tala said. Pilar agreed at once, and Auntie Luz nodded. Tala placed the apple half carefully on the flat top of a wide rail where it would stay safe and visible. The seeds still formed their clear star under the open sky. A few fireflies began to blink pale green in the taller grass beyond the fence, adding tiny moving lights to the calm scene.

They stood a little longer while the bucket of water grew still again and the last gold light thinned into blue. Tala felt the quiet satisfaction of having looked closely and then chosen to leave the star exactly as they found it. Pilar gave the apple one more long gaze, and Auntie Luz touched Tala's shoulder lightly in thanks. No one spoke for a whole peaceful minute. The meadow fence held its place between the tended path and the wilder flowers, and the water in the bucket mirrored the first faint stars appearing overhead. Tala's hands hung relaxed at their sides, no longer wishing to pocket or poke anything. The whole small group seemed wrapped in the gentle hush of the hour.

Later that night Tala sat by their bedroom window after the walk home, still thinking of the seed star left shining on the fence rail. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Cool night air slipped through the slightly open sash and carried the faint scent of grass from outside, while the quilt under Tala's legs felt thick and familiar. They remembered the bucket, the careful looking, and the way Auntie Luz and Pilar had shared the wonder without moving a single seed. Tala rested both palms once more on their knees and listened to the house settle into deeper quiet. The apple star stayed behind in the meadow where it belonged, and Tala carried only the memory of its neat points into sleep.
