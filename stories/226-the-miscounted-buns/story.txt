A tiny clink rose from the workbench as a loose brush settled against a jar, and Dahlia heard it right away while the last orange light of day stretched warm across the ceramic studio sill above the quiet plaza. They sat very still on the low stool, feeling the mild heat from the nearby kiln on their bare arms, and watched how the soft glow made the row of unfinished bowls shine like little moons. Outside the wide window the plaza stayed hushed, with only a few late walkers far below. Dahlia breathed in the clean clay scent that always lived in this room and let their shoulders drop. Grandpa Sol moved quietly behind them, sorting glazes, and the peaceful room seemed made for slow evening work.

Dahlia reached for the cloth-covered basket resting on the sill and peeled back the corner. Inside lay the soft buns meant for sharing after they finished painting the last plates, and the warm yeasty smell rose gently. They counted them carefully under their breath, finger hovering over each golden top. One, two, three, four, five. The number stopped short by one, and the missing place felt easy to shrug away because the basket still looked full enough in the dimming light. The wood of the sill felt smooth and cool under their palms as Dahlia paused, wondering whether anyone would notice if they simply tucked the cloth back down.

The thought of hiding the short count sat heavy for a moment, yet Dahlia remembered how the evening had begun with easy laughter and decided the plain truth would feel lighter. They glanced toward Grandpa Sol, who stood near the drying rack with a soft scrap of soapy wool in his hands, the clean soap smell drifting over as he wiped a rim. Dahlia’s own fingers found the leather lace that tied the basket handle, rubbing its worn smooth length while they gathered their words. The studio stayed calm, filled only with the low hum of the cooling kiln and the far-away hush of the plaza below.

“Grandpa Sol,” Dahlia said in a voice just above a whisper, “the bun count was short by one, and I almost shrugged it off so no one would know.” Grandpa Sol turned, his eyes kind in the fading light, and the air between them held a gentle coolness from the open window. He set the soapy wool aside and came to sit on the matching stool, the floorboards creaking softly under his careful steps. Dahlia kept hold of the leather lace, feeling its familiar texture, and waited without worry. Outside, a single leaf skittered across the empty plaza stones, the sound so faint it barely reached the sill.

“Thank you for telling me straight, Dahlia,” Grandpa Sol answered, his tone warm as fresh tea. “We can mend what can be mended together, and there is no hurry at all.” He lifted the cloth fully and they both looked at the five buns nestled in the basket. The kiln’s remaining warmth brushed their cheeks while they talked. Dahlia felt the relief spread through their chest like a quiet blanket. A soft jingle sounded then as the studio cat padded in, its collar bell giving one clear bright note before the cat curled near the warm wall. The pair smiled at the small visitor and returned to the simple task.

Together they decided the short count could be mended by setting one ceramic plate aside for later and sharing the five buns evenly with the leftover glaze cups filled with cool water. Dahlia carefully moved the cups into a neat row, the glass edges catching the last pink light from the plaza sky. Grandpa Sol sliced the first bun with a slow knife that made a soft whisper against the board. “This way everyone still has a taste, and tomorrow we can bake a fresh batch right here,” he said. Dahlia nodded and felt the pleasant coolness of the water glass against their fingers as they passed one cup across.

Faye appeared in the doorway with quiet steps, carrying two more clean cups, and the air stirred just enough to bring a faint chill from the hallway. “We heard the kettle and came to help finish,” Faye said softly, settling on the rug. Dahlia explained the short count in plain words, and Faye simply nodded without any fuss. The three of them sat close on the sill side of the room, the cat’s collar bell giving another tiny ring when the cat shifted. Dahlia broke their share of bun into small pieces, noticing how the soft crumb felt against their tongue and how the sweet taste lingered. Outside the plaza lamps began to glow one by one, steady and kind.

They lingered over the last bites while Grandpa Sol wiped the board clean and Dahlia lined the spoons up by size along the sill edge, the metal cool and smooth under their careful hands. The spoons caught little sparks of light from the street below, and the studio felt settled and safe. Faye hummed a quiet tune that matched the slow evening, and Dahlia joined with a soft hum of their own. The leather lace still hung from the basket, now empty and light. No one rushed, and the cool air from the open window brushed their faces like a friendly hand. Grandpa Sol poured a final drop of water for the cat, who lapped it with tiny sounds.

Dahlia leaned their head against Grandpa Sol’s sleeve and watched the plaza grow quieter still, the last walkers gone and only the soft lamp light remaining. The temperature in the room had eased into a perfect mildness, neither warm nor chill, perfect for resting eyes. “I am glad I spoke the plain truth,” Dahlia murmured. Grandpa Sol rested a gentle hand on their shoulder and answered, “So am I, Dahlia. Everything feels right now.” The cat stretched and the collar bell gave one final delicate ring. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
