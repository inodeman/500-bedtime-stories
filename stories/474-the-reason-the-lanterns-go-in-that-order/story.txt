The first lantern hung lowest, its glass cool and faintly blue in the dim porch light, and the second sat a hand higher with a warm amber pane, and the third rested highest of all with a pale green glow that made the wood behind it look soft. Delia stood close enough to see the tiny scratches on the metal hooks and the way the cords twisted the same careful way each time, and the rain ticked steadily on the porch roof like quiet fingers, and nobody was late for anything. Warm air drifted from the open kitchen door and carried the smell of tomato soup, thick and gentle. Nana Ruth sat in the wide chair with the wicker handle smooth under her palm, and Delia sat on the low bench beside her, knees tucked up, listening to the soft tick of water on the boards.

Nana Ruth touched the lowest lantern with one finger and smiled the slow smile that meant a story had already begun. “These go in this order for a reason,” Nana Ruth said, her voice low enough that the rain almost kept time with it. Delia felt a bubble of bigger news rise in their chest—something about Willa and the paper boats and the way the creek had looked that morning—but Delia pressed their lips together and watched the amber light instead. The porch boards felt cool under Delia’s bare feet, and a thin breeze slipped past the railing and brushed Delia’s cheek like a damp cloth. Far down the street a cat’s collar bell gave one small bright ring and then fell quiet again.

“When I was small,” Nana Ruth said, “my mother hung them just this way so the blue one would greet the night first, and the amber one would hold the middle of the dark, and the green one would keep watch nearest the stars.” Delia wanted to say that Willa had found a leaf shaped like a boat, but Delia only nodded and breathed in the warm soup air again. The rain ticked on, steady and kind, and the lantern glass made soft colored shapes on the floorboards that moved when the breeze moved. Nana Ruth’s sleeve brushed Delia’s arm, and the fabric felt dry and cotton-soft against Delia’s skin.

“The blue one is for coming home,” Nana Ruth went on, “because home looks a little cooler when you first step onto the porch after a long day.” Delia’s toes curled against the cool wood. The bigger news pushed again—Willa had said the leaf-boat floated farther than any of the others—but Delia kept still and watched Nana Ruth’s mouth shape each word. A moth fluttered near the amber glass and then drifted away into the darker yard. Somewhere inside the house a kettle clicked as it cooled, a small ordinary sound that made the porch feel even safer.

“The amber one is for the middle hours,” Nana Ruth said, “when supper is finished and the talk grows quieter and the chairs scrape back just once.” Delia smoothed one wrinkle out of the cloth napkin that lay folded on the bench between them, feeling the faint ridges of the weave under their fingertips. The rain kept its gentle tick-tick on the roof, and the colored lights painted slow circles on Delia’s knees. Nana Ruth’s hand rested again on the wicker handle of the chair, and the woven strands felt firm and familiar under her quiet grip. Delia almost spoke, then waited, cheeks warm from the kitchen air that still smelled faintly of soup.

“The green one stays highest,” Nana Ruth continued, “so that anyone looking up from the path can see that the house is still awake and kind, even when the rest of the street has gone dim.” Delia’s shoulders loosened. The news about Willa and the leaf-boat could wait a little longer. Outside the railing the rain made tiny silver threads that caught the porch light and then disappeared into the grass. Delia’s hands rested open on their lap, palms cool where the air touched them, and the three lanterns glowed in their careful row like three quiet friends who already knew the ending of the tale.

Nana Ruth’s voice grew even softer, almost matching the rain. “That is why we never switch them,” she said. “Blue first for the step onto the boards, amber next for the long sit-down, green last for the look toward sleep. If they hung any other way, the porch would feel different, and we would notice without quite knowing why.” Delia watched the green light tremble once when a stronger drop hit the roof edge. The boards under Delia stayed cool, and the soup warmth from the kitchen mixed with the damp night air until everything smelled like comfort and rain together. Nana Ruth finished the last sentence and then simply sat, hands quiet on the wicker handle, waiting without hurry.

Delia turned a little on the bench so they could see Nana Ruth’s face in the mixed lantern light. “What happens if the cords get tangled after a big wind?” Delia asked, the one real question that had grown while they listened. Nana Ruth nodded as if the question had been expected all along. “Then we untangle them in the morning,” Nana Ruth said, “and we hang them again blue, amber, green, and the porch remembers.” Delia felt the answer settle inside like a warm spoonful. The rain ticked on without rushing, and the colored shapes on the floorboards shifted gently when Nana Ruth leaned back. Delia’s bigger news had become smaller now, and that felt right.

Delia leaned their head against Nana Ruth’s arm and said, “Willa and I floated a leaf today, and it went past the stones and didn’t tip.” Nana Ruth’s free hand rested lightly on Delia’s hair, warm and steady. The three lanterns kept their order, blue low, amber middle, green high, and the rain kept its soft counting on the roof while the kitchen air cooled by degrees. Delia closed their eyes for a moment and felt the porch hold them both, no one late, nothing sharp, only the quiet order of light and the sound of water and the memory of soup. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
