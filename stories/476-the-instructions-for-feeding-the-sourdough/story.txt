The soft tick of a tin clock reached Nils first that early morning, a gentle metal sound that made them lift their head from the smooth wooden table. Warm light from the low window spilled across the close-set chairs, and Nils felt the cool air of the kitchen on their bare arms while the smell of toast crumbs lingered near their nose. Mama sat right beside them, her elbow almost touching theirs, and the kettle on the stove began its quiet tick as steam rose in a thin white curl. Nils breathed slowly with that small tick, shoulders easing, and watched a dust mote drift through the pale gold light. The chairs scraped just a little when Mama shifted closer, wood against wood, and Nils noticed how steady the table felt under their palms.

Mama unfolded a folded sheet beside the jar of bubbly starter and smiled at Nils with sleepy eyes. “These are the instructions for feeding the sourdough,” she said in a voice soft as the morning light. Nils smelled the faint wet wool of Mama’s shawl draped over the chair back, a clean damp scent that mixed with the warmer kitchen air. The paper rested near the butter dish, and Nils could already see neat lines of writing under the soft lamp glow. Outside the window the sky held that pale blue just before full sunrise, and the table felt solid and cool under Nils’s wrists. They stayed quiet, chairs pulled so near that their knees almost bumped, and the whole room felt hushed and ready.

Mama smoothed the page with one careful hand and began to read the steps aloud while Nils watched the bubbles in the jar catch the light. “First we discard a little, then add flour and water, and we never use water that is too hot,” Mama said, her words calm and even. Nils wanted to jump in with the bigger news about the drawing they had made last night, the one with the tall blue tree and the tiny house, but they pressed their lips together and listened. The paper edge felt slightly rough when Nils’s finger brushed it by accident, a crisp dry texture against their skin. Warmth from the stove reached their ankles in gentle waves, and the chairs stayed close so that Mama’s voice stayed right beside them. Nils kept their eyes on the jar and on Mama’s mouth moving slowly through each line.

“We never leave the lid sealed tight while it feeds, and we never forget to mark the jar so we can see how much it grows,” Mama continued, turning the page a little. Nils felt the urge to speak rise again like a bubble, yet they stayed still and let every word arrive fully. The light shifted as a cloud passed outside, turning the table surface from gold to soft silver for a moment. Steam from the kettle carried a mild warmth across Nils’s cheek, and the quiet of the room wrapped around them both. Mama’s voice never hurried, and Nils noticed how the starter jar sat like a small round friend between the salt shaker and the spoon rest. They folded their hands in their lap and waited through the next careful sentence.

Mama pointed to a short list near the bottom and read the parts about what not to do with the same gentle tone. “Do not stir with a metal spoon if the jar is glass, do not place it near the cold window overnight, and do not add honey until the day we bake,” she said. Nils listened all the way, even though the bigger news still sat bright inside their chest. The air held a mild coolness near the floor while the upper room stayed warmer, and Nils felt that difference on their toes and on their ears. Mama’s shawl brushed the table edge with a soft sound, and the chairs stayed tucked close so no space opened between them. Nils watched Mama’s finger travel down the lines and waited for the very last word.

The final sentence came slowly, and Mama read it twice so the meaning settled. “And most of all, do not rush the rise; the sourdough needs quiet time the same way we do,” she finished, then set the paper flat. Nils sat through every sound of that ending, heart steady, and only then leaned forward a little. The kitchen light had grown brighter now, painting thin stripes across the jar and the spoons. A faint warmth rose from the floorboards under Nils’s feet, and the close chairs made the whole moment feel shared and safe. They looked at Mama’s kind face and knew the listening part was complete.

Nils asked the one real question that had formed while they waited. “Mama, how will we know when the sourdough is happy enough to bake into bread?” they said, voice quiet and clear. Mama smiled and touched the jar lightly so the glass made a tiny clear ring. “We will see the bubbles reach the line we marked, and it will smell like warm bread already,” she answered. Nils felt the smooth table again under their hands and watched the light dance on the water glass nearby. The room stayed peaceful, no hurry anywhere, and the chairs remained side by side like two quiet friends. Nils nodded, taking in the answer fully before anything else rose to their lips.

Only then did Nils share the small thing of their own, the quiet piece instead of the bigger news. “I drew a little loaf with seeds on top last night, just a small picture,” they said, and Mama’s eyes brightened. The air carried a gentle heat from the stove now, wrapping their shoulders in comfort. Nils described the tiny pencil marks and the way the paper had felt under the crayon, keeping the words soft. Mama listened in turn, nodding, and the kitchen seemed to breathe with them both. The starter jar sat between them like a promise of later bread, and the close chairs held the easy closeness of morning. Nils felt glad they had waited, the small sharing landing gently in the quiet space.

Later they would mix the flour, yet for now the table held only the instructions, the jar, and the two of them. Nils rested their cheek on one hand and watched Mama fold the paper once more with careful fingers. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Warm light pooled across the wood, and Nils breathed the calm kitchen air while the day began without any rush at all.
