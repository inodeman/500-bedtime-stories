"Uncle Idris, I brought the bowl even though I almost hid it," Pema said, their voice barely louder than the hush inside the workshop as late sunlight poured golden and mild across the long wooden bench. Pema kept one hand near the smooth grain of the wood where open jars of glue waited beside neat stacks of paper labels, and the other hovered above a small ceramic bowl holding pear pieces that had been chopped earlier. The light felt gentle on their bare arms while they shifted from foot to foot, almost pushing the bowl behind a pile of leftover scraps so no one would notice. They could still feel the cool clay rim under their fingertips and smell the clean wood all around them. Pema stayed right there beside the bench instead of stepping away.

Pema remembered how they had stood at the same bench that morning, stirring the fruit for the little wooden gift boxes Uncle Idris planned to fill and label for the neighbors. The mixture needed one slow stir of honey to sweeten it, yet Pema had skipped that step and hoped no one would taste the plain fruit later. Now the bowl sat in plain sight among the glue pots and curling labels, and Pema felt the quiet weight of that choice settle in their chest. Soft dust motes drifted in the slanting light, and the bench felt solid and familiar under their resting elbow. They straightened a corner of the old rug with one toe, pressing the frayed edge flat against the floorboards so the room looked tidy again.

Uncle Idris wiped his hands on a clean cloth and came to stand beside Pema, the warm air from the open window brushing both their faces. He glanced at the bowl and then at Pema without hurry, waiting while the workshop stayed peaceful and bright. Pema watched his kind face and decided the almost-hidden bowl could not stay secret any longer. The wood of the bench held the faint sweetness of dried glue, and Pema rested their free hand on a stack of labels that felt papery and dry. Outside the leaves moved, yet inside everything remained steady and safe for the next words.

"I skipped the honey step when I mixed the pears," Pema told him in a clear voice, looking straight at Uncle Idris while the afternoon light warmed their shoulders. "I hoped no one would taste it and know. I almost slid the bowl under the scraps so you would not see." The plain truth left their mouth and floated gently between them above the bench scattered with wood shavings, glue brushes, and waiting labels. Pema felt their cheeks grow a little warmer yet kept speaking without looking away. The air carried a sudden clear scent of pear skins from the open bowl, fresh and soft, wrapping around them both like a quiet reminder of the fruit still waiting.

Uncle Idris nodded once and rested a steady hand on the edge of the bench near Pema. "Thank you for telling me the plain truth, Pema," he said, his tone even and calm as a slow breath. "We can mend what can be mended right here. No fuss, just us and the pears." He reached for the small jar of honey that sat among the labels and slid it closer so Pema could see the golden shine inside. The wood under their hands felt smooth and solid, and Pema noticed how the light made the glue pots glow like tiny moons. Together they stood close, the workshop holding only kindness and the quiet work ahead.

Pema picked up the wooden spoon that lay ready on the bench and began to stir while Uncle Idris tipped a slow ribbon of honey into the bowl. The mixture turned glossy and sweet under the circling spoon, and the soft scrape of wood against ceramic filled the peaceful space. "Does it look better now?" Pema asked, watching the pears shine. Uncle Idris smiled and answered, "It looks ready for the little boxes, Pema. You and I fixed it side by side." They kept working without rush, gluing fresh labels onto the small wooden lids they had cut earlier, pressing each one smooth so the neighbors would know the jars held care. The air stayed mild, and Pema felt the quiet pride of finishing what they had started.

While the glue dried on the labels, Pema reached into their pocket and found a warm coin that someone had given them days before for good luck. They turned the smooth metal slowly between their fingers, feeling its gentle heat against their skin as they watched Uncle Idris set the finished boxes in a neat row. The texture of the warm coin settled their hands and matched the calm of the workshop bench still dotted with wood, glue, and the last unused labels. Outside the open window the sound of wind in the fig leaves rose and fell like a soft lullaby, brushing through the green branches and drifting into the room. Pema listened and kept turning the coin, staying close to the bench and to Uncle Idris.

They carried the sealed boxes together to the side shelf where the late light still reached, placing each one carefully so none would tip. Pema ran one finger along the edge of a finished label and felt the tiny raised letters under their skin. "The neighbors will like the pears now," Pema said, and Uncle Idris replied, "They will, because you spoke true and we mended the bowl together." The workshop smelled clean and sweet, the temperature stayed comfortable, and Pema felt the floor steady under their feet after straightening that rug corner earlier. Nothing hurried them. They simply stood for a moment longer beside the wooden bench, breathing the quiet air and knowing the work was whole again.

Pema and Uncle Idris tidied the last brushes and closed the glue pots while the light grew softer and blue at the edges of the room. Pema kept the warm coin in their palm a little longer before slipping it safely away, then leaned once more against the familiar bench. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the fig leaves still whispered, and inside the workshop the air held the last trace of pear and wood as Pema felt ready for sleep, the mended boxes waiting quietly on their shelf.
