"The map got soaked in the river mist, Tamsin, so I must speak every path instead of showing the paper," the ferryman said as he settled onto the library window seat beside them. Warm afternoon light poured through the tall panes and rested like a soft blanket across the cushions where Tamsin curled their legs. The wooden seat felt smooth under their hands, and the whole corner of the library held a quiet hush that made every word clear. Tamsin wanted to leap in with news about the oak tree Lior had climbed that morning, yet they kept still and watched the ferryman's calm face. Outside the glass the garden path lay empty and peaceful, and inside the shelves smelled of old paper mixed with something sweeter farther off. Tamsin pressed their shoulder gently against the cool window frame and decided to hear the whole telling first.

The ferryman lifted one hand that felt chalky from the white marks he used on the ferry chalkboard each dawn, and he began to paint the map with careful sentences. Tamsin noticed the dry, powdery texture against the air when his fingers moved, and they listened while he described a blue inlet that curved past three smooth stones. Soft lamp glow from the nearest table mixed with the daylight and made the dust motes drift like tiny slow boats. Tamsin almost spoke about the bright feather Lior had found, but they folded their hands and let the voice keep going. The cushions beneath them held a gentle warmth from the sun, and the library walls seemed to lean in just a little as if they too wished to hear. Every detail of the spoken map arrived slowly, and Tamsin stayed close without rushing.

"Around the inlet the water stays shallow enough for bare feet, and the reeds sing when the breeze comes," the ferryman continued, his tone steady as a quiet paddle stroke. Tamsin heard a gate latch click once far across the garden, a small metal sound that drifted in through a cracked window and then faded. Cool air touched their cheek for a moment before the room settled warm again. They pictured the reeds and the gentle water while their bigger news about Lior's tree waited patiently behind their teeth. The window seat fabric felt plush under their palms, and the ferryman's words built the missing map piece by piece. Tamsin breathed slowly and kept their eyes on the storyteller so they would not miss a single turn of the path he described.

He spoke next of a low bridge made from fallen trunks that still smelled of rain even in memory, and Tamsin leaned forward so the soft light from the high windows could fall across both of them. The temperature in the alcove stayed pleasantly mild, neither hot nor chilly. Tamsin felt the urge to tell how Lior had waved from the highest branch, yet they waited through every word about the bridge and the moss that grew thick and green along its rails. Shelves nearby cast long gentle shadows that shifted only when a page turned somewhere deep in the stacks. The ferryman's voice never hurried, and Tamsin matched that calm by staying quiet on the seat. They traced an invisible line on their knee as if following the spoken bridge themselves.

"After the bridge the trail climbs a little hill covered in soft clover, and from the top you can see the ferry dock like a silver ribbon," the ferryman said, smiling at Tamsin. A single chime sounded from the old library clock, clear and round, and Tamsin waited through that soft note before the next sentence began. The light had grown a touch golden now, painting the window seat in honey-colored stripes that made everything look kind. Tamsin still held their bigger news inside, choosing instead to hear how the clover felt under imaginary feet. The air carried a faint sweetness of honey from a jar someone had opened near the children's shelves earlier, and the scent blended with the quiet so nicely that Tamsin almost smiled. They stayed right there, listening all the way without a single interruption.

The spoken map went on to a quiet cove where smooth pebbles lined the shore and small fish moved like silver thoughts beneath the surface. Tamsin felt the cool glass of the window against their shoulder and watched the ferryman's hands shape the cove in the air. The whole library seemed wrapped in a peaceful temperature that invited long stories. Again the wish to share about Lior rose up, yet Tamsin pushed it gently aside and kept their ears open for the last parts. Dust motes floated in the beam of light like tiny lanterns of their own. Every sentence settled into place, and Tamsin collected them carefully the way one might gather smooth stones.

He finished the last sentence by saying the cove held a single flat rock perfect for sitting and watching the evening ferry lights appear one by one across the water. Tamsin stayed silent until that final word faded into the quiet room. Then they asked their one real question in a soft voice. "Does the flat rock stay warm even after the sun slips down?" The ferryman nodded and answered that it held the day's gentle heat for a long while. Soft afternoon light still filled the window seat, and Tamsin felt ready at last to offer something of their own. The cushions cradled them both, and the library air remained still and kind around their shared corner.

Tamsin shared a small thing then, speaking quietly so the shelves would not stir. "I saw a ladybug with seven spots resting on the window latch this morning." The ferryman listened with the same care he had given his map, and Tamsin felt glad they had waited. Warm light continued to pour across the seat, and the temperature stayed just right for sitting close. They both looked out at the peaceful garden where nothing hurried. The spoken map and the small ladybug news rested side by side like two friendly boats tied at the same dock. Tamsin leaned a little more into the cushions and felt the whole afternoon settle gently around them.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Its mild yellow light mixed with the last of the day and made the window seat feel like a safe small harbor. Tamsin rested their chalk-free hands on the warm fabric and listened to the quiet that followed every good telling. The air held no rush, only the calm that comes when one person has spoken fully and another has truly heard. Outside the garden waited under a kind sky, and inside the library the shelves kept their peaceful watch. Tamsin and the ferryman stayed side by side a little longer while the gentle glow touched the edges of the cushions and the spoken paths and the small shared news all at once.
