The warm kitchen smelled of cedar from the wooden spoons resting in a crock by the sink, and Chioma’s small hands were already busy smoothing a clean cloth over the counter while soft yellow light spilled across the flour-dusted boards. Chioma could hear the quiet tick of the oven clock and feel the gentle heat rising from the tiles under their bare feet. On the middle shelf sat the tray of tiny cakes whose frosting had to set, each little cake pale and perfect under a light cover. Chioma paused, fingertips resting on the cool edge of the counter, and breathed in that cedar scent again as they straightened the cloth with careful strokes. Mama moved nearby, humming under her breath, and the air felt calm and thick with sweetness from dough and jam left to finish their slow work.

Chioma glanced once more toward the tray of tiny cakes whose frosting had to set and felt the cool metal of the shelf against their wrist when they reached up. “Mama, may I lift the cover just a little to see if the frosting is ready?” Chioma asked in a soft voice that barely rose above the low gurgle of the cooling pots. Mama’s smile was warm in the buttery light. “Not yet, Chioma,” Mama said. “The frosting needs its quiet time, just like the dough and the jam.” Chioma nodded and let their hand fall back to the counter, feeling the fine dust of sugar still clinging to the wood like a soft film. Outside the open upper window the evening air drifted in, cool against Chioma’s cheeks, and they stayed close to the covered tray without touching it.

Chioma turned away from the shelf and noticed a plain cup left near the sink, its handle pointing toward the wall, and the kitchen air felt pleasantly warm around their shoulders. “I can turn the cup so the handle faces the room,” Chioma said, and Mama answered, “That would be a kind help, Chioma.” Chioma picked up the cup carefully; the ceramic felt smooth and slightly cool under their palms as they rotated it until the handle faced the open room. The small click of the cup settling back on the counter mixed with the distant hush of wind in the fig leaves beyond the bakery wall. Chioma stood still for a moment, listening, and the sound of wind in the fig leaves moved through the kitchen like a gentle breath, brushing the curtains and making them sway.

Chioma walked across the bakery kitchen where dough and jam were left to finish and found a straw mat rolled near the mixing table, its woven surface rough and dry under their fingers when they unrolled it. Chioma smoothed the straw mat flat so Mama could set a bowl of cooled jam jars upon it without slipping. The texture of the straw mat pressed against Chioma’s palms in neat little ridges, and they patted it once more to settle every corner. Soft amber light from the wall lamp pooled over the mat and made the straw gleam like pale gold. Chioma’s feet felt the cooler floorboards beyond the mat’s edge as they stepped back, content with the tidy square they had made.

Chioma returned to the middle of the room and began wiping the long wooden table with a damp cloth that smelled faintly of lemon under the stronger cedar still lingering from the spoons. The cloth was damp and cool, and Chioma moved it in slow circles while the tray of tiny cakes whose frosting had to set waited untouched on its shelf. “The cakes are still resting,” Chioma whispered to themself, and Mama heard and said gently, “Yes, Chioma, and so are we, in our own way.” Chioma smiled at the sound of Mama’s voice and kept wiping until every crumb was gone and the table shone under the quiet light. The temperature near the oven door stayed mild and steady, wrapping the kitchen like a soft blanket.

Chioma carried two clean spoons to the drying rack and listened again to the hush beyond the window where the wind in the fig leaves had quieted to a whisper. The metal spoons felt cool and smooth between Chioma’s fingers as they slid into place with a tiny clink. Chioma paused beside the covered tray once more but did not lift the cloth; instead they rested both hands lightly on the counter and watched a thin ribbon of steam curl up from a nearby pot of jam that still needed time. “I can wait with the cakes,” Chioma said, and Mama nodded from across the room. “Waiting together makes the minutes gentle,” Mama replied. The light from the wall lamp caught the edge of the tray and turned it softly silver.

Chioma sat for a little while on a low stool whose seat felt warm from the day’s work, and they folded a stack of napkins into neat squares, each crease crisp under their thumbs. The napkins smelled clean and faintly of sun-dried cotton, and Chioma stacked them carefully while keeping the tray of tiny cakes whose frosting had to set always in the corner of their eye. Outside, the last pale light of evening faded, and the kitchen grew dimmer yet still cozy. Chioma’s elbows rested on their knees, and they felt the quiet rise and fall of their own breathing match the slow tick of the clock. No one hurried; the dough proofed in its bowls, the jam cooled in its jars, and the frosting continued its private work under the cover.

Chioma stood again when Mama opened a drawer and brought out a small brush for sweeping crumbs from the corners. Chioma took the brush and swept in long, calm strokes, feeling the soft bristles whisper across the floorboards that still held a little day’s warmth. Tiny bits of sugar gathered into a pale pile, and Chioma brushed them into a dustpan with patient care. “Almost finished here,” Chioma said, and Mama answered, “You have made the kitchen peaceful, Chioma.” The covered tray waited on, untouched, while the brush strokes slowed and the last crumbs disappeared. Chioma set the brush aside and washed their hands in water that felt cool and clean against their skin, drying them on a towel that smelled faintly of cedar still hanging in the air.

Chioma and Mama stood together at last before the shelf, and Chioma felt the gentle cool of the evening air on their forearms as Mama lifted the cover from the tray of tiny cakes whose frosting had to set. The frosting had grown smooth and firm, gleaming under the kitchen light like quiet snow, and Chioma’s eyes widened with soft delight. “They are ready now,” Chioma said, voice hushed with happiness, and Mama smiled and touched Chioma’s shoulder. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Chioma leaned close enough to smell the sweet vanilla rising from the finished cakes and rested one hand on the warm wooden shelf, content that everything had finished in its own time while they had kept the kitchen calm and kind.
