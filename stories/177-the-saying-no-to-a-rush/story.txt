"May I take the afternoon loaves for the river crossing when you can spare them?" the ferryman asked from across the bakery counter facing the street. Tamsin stood on the low wooden stool behind the counter so they could reach the bread baskets easily, and the late sun poured warm light across the polished wood and onto Tamsin's sleeves. Outside, slow footsteps passed along the pavement, and a cart wheel creaked once before rolling on. The ferryman kept his hands folded and his face kind, with no tapping toes or tapping fingers. Tamsin felt the bakery heat rise gently from the ovens behind them and wrap around their back like a friendly shawl while they thought about how to answer.

Tamsin knew the loaves still needed a few quiet minutes to finish cooling on the racks. The job did not need a rush at all. Yet the thought of saying so made Tamsin's cheeks feel warm in a shy way. They smoothed a napkin that already lay flat and listened to the low murmur of the street beyond the glass. Lior worked nearby stacking empty trays, and the soft clink of metal was comforting. Tamsin rubbed their hands together and felt the fine dry flour leave a chalky hand that brushed like soft powder against their skin. That simple texture helped Tamsin stand still and breathe.

From the sticky buns cooling nearest the window came the sweet smell of honey, rich and golden and calm. The honey scent drifted over the counter and mixed with the plain bread smell until the whole space felt cozy and safe for careful words. Tamsin looked at the ferryman's patient posture and felt a little braver. No one in the bakery hurried them. A moth of flour floated in a sunbeam and settled near a sugar jar. Tamsin watched it land and decided the smallest brave version of the truth would be enough if it could still be heard across the wood.

Lior stepped closer with a clean cloth and gave the counter a quiet wipe near Tamsin's elbow. "You can say it just the way it is, Tamsin," Lior said in a low friendly voice. The cloth made a soft swish against the grain of the wood. Tamsin nodded and felt the cooler air near the street-facing glass touch their wrists. Inside their mind Tamsin practiced the sentence once more. The words were simple: the job did not need a rush. Saying them softly would still count if the ferryman could hear. Tamsin straightened two paper bags so their corners matched perfectly.

The ferryman rested his elbows lightly on his side of the counter and watched the street with easy eyes. "I can wait as long as the bread needs," he said, his voice as steady as a slow tide. Just then Tamsin heard a gate latch click closed in the alley behind the bakery, a small clear sound of metal settling into place. That quiet gate latch sound seemed to hold the moment steady for Tamsin. Sunlight turned the glass jars of jam into glowing red and purple shapes. Tamsin touched the cool rim of a metal tray and felt the shyness loosen its hold.

Tamsin drew in a small breath that carried the taste of flour and sweetness. Instead of looking away or pretending the matter did not matter, Tamsin lifted their chin a little and met the ferryman's kind gaze. "The job did not need a rush," Tamsin said softly, yet in a voice clear enough to cross the counter and reach him. The words floated in the warm air between them like a ribbon of calm. Tamsin felt their own spoken sentence leave their mouth and settle gently. The bakery stayed peaceful, and the street sounds remained far and soft beyond the window.

The ferryman smiled right away, a real smile that brightened his whole face. "Thank you for telling me straight out, Tamsin," he said. "I am happy to wait while everything finishes resting the right way." He did not ask Tamsin to speak louder or to make a show of bravery. He simply received the words the way one receives a carefully wrapped gift. Tamsin felt ease spread through their shoulders and down their arms. The wooden counter felt smooth and solid under their resting fingertips, and the golden light held steady without flicker.

Tamsin began packing the loaves into two sturdy paper bags with slow careful motions. Each loaf settled with a soft quiet thump, and the paper crackled in a friendly way under their hands. Lior brought a small bowl of honey glaze, and Tamsin brushed a light shine onto the tops of the sweet rolls. The street outside grew quieter as people headed home for supper. Tamsin and the ferryman waited together through a single chime from the old clock on the bakery wall. The chime rang once, round and clear, then faded into the warm room, leaving only the low oven hum behind.

When the bags were ready and neatly folded at the tops, Tamsin passed them across the counter with both hands. The ferryman took them carefully against his coat and tipped his hat in thanks before turning toward the door that opened onto the street. Tamsin watched him step outside into the soft evening. The bakery grew still as the last crumbs were brushed away. Tamsin wiped the counter one final time and felt the day settle around them like a soft quilt. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Cool night air drifted in through a cracked pane, gentle and clean, and Tamsin smiled at the quiet that remained after the true words had been spoken aloud.
