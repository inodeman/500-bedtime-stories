The group picture rested on the soft library rug under the round window, its glossy surface catching a pale stripe of afternoon light that made the frozen smiles shine. Nim leaned close and saw how the children in the frame stood shoulder to shoulder, yet one empty space waited at the edge where someone should have been. The paper felt cool under Nim’s careful fingertip, and the scent of old books drifted up from the shelves all around. Grandma Nia sat nearby in a deep chair, her knitting needles quiet for the moment, while Brigid hovered just beyond the circle of friends who were already pointing at the picture and whispering about who belonged where. Nim noticed Brigid’s shoes shifting on the rug fibers and felt a gentle wish rise to make room somehow.

Nim settled cross-legged on the thick rug and invited everyone closer with a small wave, the warm air from the heating vent brushing their ankles like a friendly cat. “Look at this empty spot,” Nim said softly, tapping the shiny paper once. Brigid stayed half a step outside the gathering, fingers curled around the strap of a little bag, watching the others lean in. The round window above them let in a honey-colored glow that painted circles on the floor, and somewhere in the quiet stacks a cat’s collar bell gave one tiny, cheerful jingle. Grandma Nia only smiled and kept her hands still, letting the children decide what came next without any hurry or push.

Nim thought about the way the picture had been taken before every friend could join, and they decided the rug itself could become a new kind of gathering place where no one stayed outside. “We can sit in a different order,” Nim told the group, voice calm and even. They patted a wide spot right beside them and looked at Brigid with open kindness. Brigid took one slow step forward, the wool of the rug springy underfoot, and the temperature near the window felt pleasantly cool against everyone’s cheeks. Nim arranged the cushions so there was a real space that fit Brigid’s knees perfectly, not a leftover corner or a prize called out loud, just a simple place waiting.

Brigid lowered onto the cushion Nim had smoothed, and the fabric held a faint texture of a glass bead sewn into one corner that pressed gently against a fingertip. Nim passed the group picture around so each child could hold it for a moment, and the paper made a soft crinkle that mixed with quiet breathing. “This empty place was never meant to stay empty,” Nim said, smiling at Brigid. The others nodded and shifted their own seats a little farther apart to make the circle wider and rounder, matching the shape of the window above. Grandma Nia watched with bright eyes but spoke no instructions, trusting the children to keep the pace slow and kind on the library rug.

Nim suggested they invent a gentle game of naming what they noticed in the room without rushing, and the warm light through the round glass made every face look soft. “I smell mint leaves from the tea Grandma Nia brought,” Nim began, then waited while Brigid added the next observation about paper and wood. The children took turns speaking in soft voices, each one pausing so Brigid could speak too, and the pace stayed unhurried like drifting clouds. No one hurried to finish or claim the middle, and the rug under them held the heat of their small bodies in a cozy pocket. Grandma Nia’s quiet presence felt like another soft cushion nearby, ready if needed yet content to let Nim lead the gentle change.

Nim reached for a spare bookmark shaped like a leaf and laid it across the empty spot in the picture as a temporary mark of welcome. “Now Brigid has a place right here with us,” Nim said, glancing around the circle. Brigid’s shoulders relaxed, and they traced the edge of the bookmark with one careful finger while the others hummed a tiny shared tune that barely rose above a whisper. The air carried a clean coolness from the stone walls of the library, and the round window framed a patch of pale sky that seemed to listen. Everyone stayed close on the rug, knees almost touching, and the group picture no longer looked unfinished because the living circle had completed what the camera once missed.

Nim invited Brigid to choose the next quiet thing they might do together, and Brigid suggested they simply look at the way the light moved across the picture and the rug. The children followed that calm idea, watching golden rectangles slide slowly toward the shelves as afternoon deepened. “It feels nice when everyone fits,” Brigid whispered, and Nim nodded with a warm grin. The texture of the woven rug cradled their legs, and a faint draft from under the door stirred the edges of the paper photograph without lifting it. Grandma Nia set her knitting aside completely now, hands folded, eyes proud of the easy way the children had rearranged both seats and chances so Brigid belonged without any fuss.

Nim gathered the picture and the bookmark and placed them side by side in the center of the circle like two friends resting after a walk. The children spoke in turns about favorite colors they saw in the frozen smiles, and each voice waited politely for the next, including Brigid’s every time. The library held a deep peaceful hush broken only by distant page turns, and the temperature stayed mild enough that no one needed an extra sweater. Nim felt the solid satisfaction of a space made right, and they rested a hand lightly on Brigid’s shoulder for one shared breath. Grandma Nia rose at last to pour cups of cool water, still letting the young ones keep the rhythm they had found under the round window.

Nim and Brigid and the others stayed on the library rug while the light turned softer and the shelves seemed to lean in like quiet listeners. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Nim tucked the group picture carefully into a folder so the empty spot could wait for a new photograph another day, and Brigid’s smile settled deep and real. The children stretched their arms once, then folded them again, content to sit together a little longer in the calm room where every place had become a true place and the air smelled only of paper and peace.
