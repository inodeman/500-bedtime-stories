The sweet smell of warm figs and honey drifted through the open courtyard while Nils’s small hands already brushed loose crumbs across the long wooden board. Late light slipped between the leaves of the fig tree and lay in soft gold patches along the table. Grandpa Sol sat at the far end with his sleeves rolled, and Dori sat close enough that Nils could hear the quiet clink of their spoon against a bowl. Nils smoothed a corner of napkin, felt the cool evening air on their wrists, and kept their eyes on the single fig roll that remained in the middle of the board. The pastry looked plump and golden, and the scent of it mixed with the faint cedar note rising from the nearby bench. Nils’s fingers stayed busy, turning a stray raisin aside so the board looked tidy.

Grandpa Sol poured cool water into three cups and set them down with a gentle thud. “The figs were kind to us this year,” he said, smiling toward the heavy branches. Dori leaned forward and whispered, “That last one smells like jam and sunshine.” Nils nodded and traced the edge of the wooden board with one careful finger, feeling the smooth grain under the pad of their thumb. Somewhere above them the wind in the fig leaves made a soft shushing sound that rose and fell like quiet breathing. The long table held empty plates, a small dish of honey, and that one remaining fig roll, still waiting. Nils felt the warmth of the stone under their sandals and the cooler air that moved across their bare arms.

Nils looked from the fig roll to Dori and then to Grandpa Sol. The pastry was clearly meant for sharing, yet it sat whole and alone. If only one person took it first, the others would watch with empty hands, and that did not feel right inside Nils’s chest. Nils rested both palms flat on the table for a moment, feeling the faint heat left by the afternoon sun in the wood. Dori’s chair scraped softly as they shifted closer. Grandpa Sol did not reach for the board. He simply waited, the lantern light beginning to gather at the edges of the courtyard while the sky turned the color of pale peaches.

“I think we can make it fair,” Nils said in a voice just loud enough for the two of them to hear. Dori tilted their head. “How?” Nils answered, “We can break it into smaller pieces so everyone has some.” Grandpa Sol’s eyes crinkled at the corners, yet he offered no speech and no instruction. He only folded his hands and let the choice belong to Nils. The air smelled faintly of warm bread and the earlier cedar, and Nils’s shoulders relaxed as they reached for the wooden board. The last fig roll sat steady under their careful fingers, soft and ready.

Nils lifted the pastry and set it on a clean corner of the board. With slow presses of both thumbs they divided it into three even shares, pressing gently so the filling stayed inside each piece. A few sticky crumbs clung to Nils’s fingertips, sweet and grainy. Dori watched without grabbing, and Grandpa Sol watched without speaking. When the three pieces lay side by side, Nils pushed one toward Dori, one toward Grandpa Sol, and kept the smallest middle piece for themself. “There,” Nils said. “Now no one waits.” The courtyard held the quiet clink of a cup being set down and the far rustle of leaves.

Dori accepted the share with both hands and took a tiny bite. “It tastes like the tree gave us a present,” they said, smiling with a speck of jam at the corner of their mouth. Grandpa Sol lifted his piece, nodded once, and ate without a single extra word, letting Nils’s choice stand exactly as it was. Nils chewed slowly, feeling the soft pastry and the sweet seeds against their tongue. Around them the long table felt peaceful. A moth drifted near the edge of the light. Nils wiped their fingers on the napkin and noticed how the stone floor still held a little of the day’s warmth beneath the cooler night air.

After the last crumbs disappeared, Nils slid from the bench and stepped onto the straw mat that lay beside the table legs. The woven surface felt dry and slightly rough under their bare feet, a friendly scratch that made them stand still for a breath. They turned back, reached for Dori’s cup, and turned the cup so the handle faced the open room. The small action settled something quiet inside Nils. Grandpa Sol watched the movement and offered only a soft hum of agreement. Dori stretched their arms above their head and yawned, the sound gentle as a sigh. Above the courtyard the sky deepened, and the fig tree’s shadow grew longer across the stones.

Nils returned to the bench and leaned their shoulder lightly against Grandpa Sol’s arm. The fabric of the old shirt felt worn and comfortable. Dori rested their cheek on folded hands and listened to the distant trickle of water from the courtyard spout. Nils spoke once more, almost in a whisper. “I liked sharing it that way.” Grandpa Sol answered with a single warm word, “Yes,” and nothing further. The three of them stayed close while the evening cooled their cheeks and the last gold light slipped from the tops of the walls. Nils’s hands lay still now, no longer busy, only resting open on the table’s edge where the wood felt smooth and familiar.

The courtyard settled into a deeper quiet as the first true night air moved past their ankles. Nils blinked slowly, feeling the pleasant heaviness that comes after a full supper and a fair choice. Dori’s breathing grew even. Grandpa Sol rested one broad hand on the table near Nils’s own. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Nils watched the gentle circle of light on the stones, smelled the fading sweetness of figs, and let their eyes close for a moment while the straw mat kept its quiet place under the bench and the whole long table seemed to breathe with them in the calm dark.
