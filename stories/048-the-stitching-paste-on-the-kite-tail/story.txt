The kite tail rested in the middle of the greenhouse bench, its long ribbon of cloth dotted with neat dabs of stitching paste that shone like tiny wet beads under the bright spill of light from the south window. Gita bent so near that the pale paste filled their whole view, and they could see how each dab sat glossy and smooth across the fabric seams. Warmth rose from the wooden boards and brushed Gita’s cheeks. Seed pots stood in tidy rows on either side, their rims cool against the sunlit surface. Gita’s breath fogged a little circle on the air as they studied every stitch line and every waiting drop of paste.

“I want to check it now,” Gita said, their voice soft in the quiet space. Auntie June sat on a low stool nearby, her hands resting on her knees, and the air around her carried the gentle smell of lavender from a small bundle tucked among the pots. Gita rocked from one foot to the other, feeling the smooth floor boards under their socks. The south window threw a bright square across the bench, and dust motes drifted through it like slow sparks. Gita reached one finger toward the cloth edge, then paused. The paste still looked wet in the middle of each dab, and the cloth held a faint coolness where the paste had not yet set.

Auntie June smiled and spoke in a calm tone. “It needs a little more time under the cover, Gita. We can leave it be.” Gita nodded and drew their hand back. They pulled the soft cloth cover gently over the kite tail so that only the very tip of one ribbon peeped out. The greenhouse held a steady warmth that settled on Gita’s shoulders like a light shawl. Outside the glass the garden lay still, and inside the only movement came from a slow shift of sun across the seed pots. Gita pressed their palms flat on the bench edge and felt the grain of the wood, worn smooth by many seasons of use.

Gita decided to stay right there and do something quiet while they waited. They chose a pot of young seedlings and began to brush loose crumbs of soil from the rim with the side of one finger. The soil felt dry and powdery at the top yet held a deeper coolness underneath. Auntie June watched without hurry. “You can count the new leaves if you like,” she said. Gita answered, “I will count them twice and then watch the light.” Their voice stayed low so it would not disturb the quiet air. A faint tick reached them from the corner shelf where a tin clock kept steady time, each soft mark of the second hand clear in the warm room.

Gita counted seven tiny green leaves on the first seedling and eight on the next. They moved to a third pot and noticed how the shadow of the pot’s rim had crept the width of a thumb across the bench since they began. The shadow’s edge looked sharp and blue-gray against the bright wood. Gita rested their chin on their folded arms and simply watched that slow crawl of shade. The temperature near the glass felt a degree warmer than the shaded side of the bench, and Gita shifted so both elbows stayed in the gentle heat. Auntie June hummed a short tune without words, and the sound blended with the distant garden birds beyond the panes.

After a while Gita sat up straighter and ran one fingertip along the hem of the old quilt folded on the stool beside Auntie June. The quilt stitch rose in a neat raised line, firm and slightly bumpy under Gita’s skin. They traced three long rows of those stitches and felt how each one held the layers together. “The paste must be almost ready,” Gita whispered. Auntie June replied, “We will know when the cover feels dry to the touch.” Gita nodded again and returned their gaze to the covered kite tail. The cloth cover lay still and smooth. Sunlight had moved farther along the bench, warming the far row of seed pots until their clay sides held a pleasant heat that Gita could sense even without touching them.

Gita poured a thin stream of water from a small can into the driest pot, listening to the soft sip as the soil drank it in. Tiny beads of moisture darkened the surface and released a fresh earthy scent that mingled with the earlier lavender. Gita set the can down carefully so it made no clink. They folded their hands in their lap and watched the covered kite tail once more. The south window light had turned a deeper gold, and it painted the edges of every pot with a warm outline. Gita’s eyelids felt heavy for a moment, yet they stayed awake and quiet, simply breathing the calm greenhouse air.

Auntie June leaned forward at last and lifted one corner of the cloth cover. “You can look now, Gita,” she said. Gita rose on their toes and peered underneath. The stitching paste had turned clear and firm, holding each seam of the kite tail in neat flat lines. Gita touched the driest dab with the very tip of one finger and found it smooth and no longer sticky. A smile spread across their face. They lifted the whole kite tail free of the bench and held it up so the ribbons hung straight in the golden light. The fabric whispered against itself with a dry soft sound. Seed pots sat undisturbed in their rows, and the whole room seemed to glow with the same quiet satisfaction Gita felt.

Gita carried the finished kite tail to the south window and let the last of the afternoon light shine through the colored strips. Auntie June joined them there, and together they watched the ribbons stir in the faintest indoor breeze. Gita spoke one more time, their words slow and happy. “It waited until it was ready, and so did I.” The warmth of the glass met Gita’s forehead when they leaned close. Outside, the garden settled into evening blue while inside the air stayed gentle. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Gita folded the kite tail with care and rested it across their arms, feeling the day ease toward night in the peaceful greenhouse.
