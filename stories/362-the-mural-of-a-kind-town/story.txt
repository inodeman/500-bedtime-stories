The mural square sat low on the warm plaster wall of the bakery kitchen, a pale empty patch no bigger than a windowpane that still needed slow outlines and patient fills of color before anyone set the early table. Nim leaned so near that their nose nearly touched the cool smooth surface, and in the dim blue light before sunrise they could see every tiny ridge left by the last careful sanding. The square smelled faintly of clean wall and distant flour, and Nim’s fingertips brushed the edge where the fresh plaster met the older painted bricks, feeling how the temperature stayed milder there than the chilly air farther out in the room. Ms. Okonkwo had placed two small pots of paint and a pair of thin brushes on the counter just beside it, and the whole kitchen waited in that quiet hush that comes only before the first loaves rise.

Nim stepped back half a pace and called softly to Brigid, who was still tying the strings of an apron near the big wooden table. “The square looks ready, but the lines have to stay steady or the houses will wobble,” Nim said, voice barely louder than the hush of the oven vents. Brigid walked over on quiet socks and rested a hand on Nim’s shoulder, feeling the soft cotton of Nim’s sleeve. The kitchen held a gentle warmth near the banked coals, yet the far corners stayed cool enough that Brigid rubbed their palms together once. Outside the high window a single bird made a sleepy chirp, and the two friends studied the blank square together while the first thin ribbon of morning light stretched across the floorboards like a golden path.

Ms. Okonkwo wiped her hands on a flour-dusted cloth and came to stand behind them, the smell of fresh mint leaves rising from the little bundle she had just snipped for later tea. “If either of you rushes the outlines or the fills, the colors will run together and the kind town will look muddy,” she said in her calm low voice. Nim nodded and felt the cool air move across their cheeks when the back door drifted open a crack. Brigid reached for one brush and Nim took the other, both handles still faintly warm from resting near the low stove. They agreed without hurry that Nim would draw the careful outlines of rooftops and windows while Brigid would fill the soft spaces of gardens and paths, each working only as slowly as the morning itself.

Nim dipped the fine tip into the deep blue and began the first rooftop line, breathing out so the stroke stayed even. The plaster drank the paint with a quiet soft sound, almost like a whisper of cloth. Brigid waited until the line dried a little, then nested a gentle green fill inside a tiny garden square, the brush making the smallest shushing noise against the wall. A cat’s collar bell tinkled once from the hallway as the bakery cat wandered past the doorway and settled on a sunny flagstone that the growing light had just reached. Nim smiled at the familiar bright note and kept their hand steady, tracing another window frame while the cool morning air brushed the back of their neck like a light feather.

Halfway through the left side of the mural square the paint line met a small raised bump where an old nail had been smoothed over, and the brush wanted to skip. Nim paused and looked at Brigid. “This spot feels tricky,” Nim said. Brigid set their own brush down and touched the bump with one careful finger, discovering a single glass bead that must have fallen from a jar of cake decorations and stuck in the still-soft plaster the day before. The bead felt perfectly round and slick under Brigid’s fingertip, cooler than the surrounding wall. Ms. Okonkwo only watched from the counter where she was arranging cups, offering neither hurry nor worry, simply the quiet company of someone who trusted the work.

Nim and Brigid decided together to treat the bead as a tiny doorknob on one painted house, outlining around it with extra care so the blue line curved just so. “We can each take half the remaining edges,” Brigid suggested, voice soft as rising dough. Nim answered, “Yes, and we fill only after the lines feel dry to the touch.” They split the square down an imaginary middle path: Nim worked the left half of the outlines while Brigid finished the right half of the fills, both moving no faster than the slow drip of honey from a spoon. The temperature near the wall stayed pleasantly mild, and the growing light turned from blue to pale gold, warming the tops of their heads while their hands stayed cool and sure.

Ms. Okonkwo stepped closer once more and spoke the name of a gentle counting knot she used when frosting needed time. “Seven slow breaths for each new line, that is the knot that keeps colors kind,” she said, then returned to her mint leaves without another word. Nim counted the breaths under their breath while the brush traveled, and Brigid matched the same quiet rhythm for every fill. The kitchen filled with the small sounds of brush against plaster and the far-off hush of the street still mostly asleep. Nim felt the soft cotton of their sleeve brush the wall now and then, a light reminder to stay loose and easy, and the air carried the clean scent of soap from the washed counters mixed with the warmer smell of banked wood coals.

By the time the last garden path received its soft brown fill, the mural square showed a whole kind town of neat rooftops, open doors, and tiny bright windows, every outline clear and every color resting exactly where it belonged. Nim and Brigid stepped back side by side and looked at their shared work while the first real sunbeam reached the square and made the painted cobblestones glow. “It stayed steady because we both went slow,” Nim said, and Brigid answered with a quiet laugh that sounded like shared relief. Ms. Okonkwo set three small cups of warm milk on the early table at last, the ceramic smooth and comforting in the hand, and the whole kitchen smelled of milk and the last faint trace of mint. The friends rinsed their brushes in a bowl of clear water that felt cool against their wrists and dried them on a soft towel that smelled faintly of sunshine from yesterday’s line.

They sat together at the early table while the mural square watched over them from the wall, the painted town looking ready to welcome anyone who might come for bread. Nim rested their cheek for a moment against the warm wooden tabletop and listened to the quiet clink of spoons. Brigid pushed a plate of soft rolls closer so everyone could reach, and Ms. Okonkwo poured a little more milk with a steady hand. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
