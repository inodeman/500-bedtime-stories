Greta’s hands were already busy tearing soft pieces from a loaf when the smell of warm bread drifted up from the basket on the stone step. The evening air felt mild against their wrists as they tucked the pieces into a little cloth, and the lantern light from the doorway made the crust look almost honey-colored. Greta stood, brushed flour from their fingers, and called softly toward the kitchen where Vera was rinsing a plate. “Vera, come walk with me to the harbor wall before it gets dark,” Greta said, and Vera dried their hands on a towel and nodded. Together they stepped outside, the basket swinging gently between them, and the low tide mud shone like wet silk under the first stars.

They followed the narrow path that hugged the water, and the cool ceramic rim of the empty bowl Greta still carried pressed smooth against their palm. Greta had meant to leave the bowl inside, yet somehow it had stayed with them, light and useful for balancing the cloth of bread. Vera walked close enough that their sleeves brushed, and the harbor wall rose ahead, dark and solid, with ropes coiled like sleeping snakes along its top. Greta set the bowl down carefully on a flat spot and leaned over the edge to watch the quiet water that had slipped far out, leaving shining channels and little pools that caught the sky. A lantern already hung from an iron hook fixed into the stone, its beam falling in a steady golden cone across the wall’s rough surface.

Greta noticed it first: tiny bits of dust drifting inside that bright beam, each speck turning gold the moment the light touched it. The dust floated slowly, almost dancing, never hurried, and Greta’s fingers itched with the wish to poke it or scoop a little into a pocket. Vera leaned in beside them, and the two children held their breath so the air would not stir. “Look how they shine,” Greta whispered, pointing with one careful finger that stayed well away from the beam. Vera’s eyes grew round, and they whispered back, “They look like tiny stars that forgot how to stay up high.” Greta felt the same quiet pull, the wanting and the holding still all mixed together.

Footsteps sounded along the wall, slow and even, and the ferryman appeared carrying an empty net that smelled faintly of river water. He stopped when he saw the children kneeling so still, and he set the net down without a clatter. The ferryman’s coat was damp at the hem from the earlier tide, and the lantern light touched the silver threads in his beard. Greta looked up and said, “There is gold dust in the light. We are only watching.” The ferryman bent his knees until he was level with them and peered into the same soft beam where the dust turned and glimmered. “I see it,” he answered in a voice as calm as the water below. “It has been traveling on the air all day, and the lantern found it just now.”

Greta showed Vera how to cup their hands near their own chests so the warmth of their breath would not reach the beam, and Vera copied the motion exactly. The three of them stayed that way for a long minute, watching each golden speck rise and fall inside the light like quiet sparks that never burned. Greta’s earlier wish to pocket the dust faded into something gentler, a wish only to keep looking. Vera’s shoulder rested lightly against Greta’s, and the stone of the wall felt solid and cool beneath their knees. Somewhere behind them a fig tree grew in a sheltered corner of the path, and the wind in the fig leaves made a soft dry rustle that seemed to belong to the same slow evening.

The ferryman spoke again, still watching the beam. “If we leave it, the night air will carry the dust farther, and maybe another lantern will find it tomorrow.” Greta nodded and felt glad they had not touched anything. Vera smiled without speaking, and Greta smiled too, the kind of smile that stayed small and warm. They shared two pieces of the bread then, tearing them carefully so crumbs would fall only onto the cloth and never into the light. The taste was plain and good, and the lantern beam kept its golden dance while they chewed. Greta thought how easy it would have been to disturb the dust, and how much better it felt to leave every speck exactly where it floated.

After a while the ferryman stood and lifted his net again. “I will walk you both toward home,” he said, “and we can leave the lantern burning a little longer for the dust.” Greta rose slowly, brushed the grit of the wall from their trousers, and picked up the empty bowl by its cool sides. Vera walked on one side of the ferryman and Greta on the other, and the three of them moved along the path with quiet steps. The low tide pools reflected the last of the sky, and the ropes on the wall looked softer now that the children knew the dust was safe. Greta glanced back once and saw the beam still holding its tiny golden travelers, undisturbed and bright.

When they reached the house the ferryman waited at the gate while Greta and Vera went inside. Greta carried the empty bowl to the sink and came back, hands free now and smelling faintly of soap. Vera hung their jacket on the low peg, and Greta did the same, the fabric still holding a little of the harbor air. Outside the window the path lay dark and peaceful, and the children could almost imagine the dust still turning gold far down at the wall. Greta sat on the floor cushion near Vera, and they spoke in low voices about the way the light had held each speck so gently, as if the lantern itself had wanted them to see and not to take.

Later Greta washed their face with lukewarm water and climbed into bed while Vera settled on the nearby pallet. The room felt still and kind, and Greta’s eyelids grew heavy with the memory of gold floating in air. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Greta breathed out one long quiet breath, knowing the dust remained exactly where they had found it, shining for whoever might pass the harbor wall tomorrow, and sleep came easy and deep.
