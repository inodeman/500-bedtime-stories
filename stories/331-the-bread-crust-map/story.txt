Chioma smelled wet earth and a clean trace of cedar from the fence boards as their busy hands already turned a smooth pebble on the garden path after the gentle rain. Cool mist kissed their cheeks and left tiny beads along their eyelashes. Chioma set the pebble back into its hollow so the path stayed neat, then brushed a droplet from a mint leaf with one careful fingertip. The silver-gray light made every shallow puddle shine like a quiet mirror. Water dripped slowly from the garden gate in soft ticks. Chioma crouched lower, feeling the damp chill through the knees of their pants and the cool air along the insides of their wrists, while their fingers kept tracing the edges of stones without lifting any more of them.

Near a clump of mint Chioma’s eyes found something pale resting on a flat stone that still held the rain’s chill. It was a bread crust, curved and ridged, its outer edge shaped exactly like a coastline on a map Mama had once opened on the kitchen table. Chioma leaned close and caught the mild toasted smell of the crust mixed with wet leaves. Their fingers hovered, wishing first to pocket the little map and then to poke its tiny bays with a twig. The wish felt warm and real. Instead Chioma sat back on their heels and watched how leftover water beaded along the ridges without moving the crust at all. The garden path pressed cool and firm beneath them, and the soft light slid across the pale surface so the coastline pattern seemed almost to breathe.

Soft footsteps approached with gentle squelches in the damp spots, and Delia came along the path. “Chioma, what did you find?” Delia asked in a voice no louder than the garden. Chioma pointed without touching. “Look,” Chioma said, “it has a coastline. See the little harbors and that long curve?” Delia knelt beside them. The wind moved through the fig leaves with a dry, whispering rustle that rose and fell like slow breathing. Chioma felt the damp evening air on the back of their neck and kept both hands folded so nothing would disturb the bread crust. Delia’s shadow stretched long and soft across the stone in the fading light, and the two friends stayed very still together.

Chioma showed Delia how to look without taking. “We can tip our heads this way,” Chioma said, angling so the gray light caught every ridge. “If we leave it, a bird might find it, or the rain might soften it gently into the earth.” Delia nodded and tucked her hands behind her back the way Chioma had done. They remained quiet while a far-off bee hummed among the flowers. Chioma felt the coolness rising from the wet stones and the warm brush of Delia’s shoulder through their shirts. The bread crust’s coastline gleamed where moisture still clung, and neither child reached forward. The mint leaves nearby quivered when another slow drop fell, releasing a fresh green scent into the mild air.

Mama’s voice floated from the porch steps. “Chioma? Delia? May I come see?” She walked down carefully so her shoes made only a soft pat on the stones. When she saw what held their attention she crouched too, the fabric of her skirt pooling on the cool ground. “It does look like a map,” Mama said, and her smile seemed to warm the silver light. Chioma pointed to the longest curve. “That part is like the shore in the picture book,” Chioma told her. Mama’s hand rested lightly on Chioma’s back, steady and warm. The three of them shared the hush while a thin ribbon of kitchen light began to reach across the yard, golden against the gray.

They stayed without touching the bread crust. Chioma watched a single ant walk a careful circle around the stone and continue on its way. The evening temperature slipped cooler, and Chioma rubbed their arms once while still keeping their gaze gentle. Delia whispered that one tiny point of crust looked like a peninsula. Mama said they could keep the shape safely in their minds without carrying anything home. Chioma felt a quiet gladness that needed no extra words. Another droplet struck the mint and the fresh scent rose again. The golden kitchen light grew a little stronger, laying a calm stripe across the damp path and the pale coastline that waited undisturbed.

Chioma stood slowly so their knees would not scrape the stones. “We should leave it right here,” Chioma said. Delia agreed with a small nod that made her hair swing. Mama rose and brushed a few damp bits of leaf from her skirt. Together they stepped back from the stone, and Chioma took one last look at the bread crust shining softly in the dimming air. The path toward the house felt pleasantly firm under Chioma’s feet. At the porch Chioma wiped both shoes, feeling the familiar scrape, then stepped inside where the air held a different kind of quiet, warmer and still. The last outdoor light slipped away behind them like a drawn curtain.

Inside, Chioma padded across the floor and felt the straw mat by the low table, its woven ridges rough and friendly under their bare toes after they slipped off damp socks. Mama poured a little warm milk into cups. Chioma turned the cup so the handle faced the room, the way that felt right before bed. The milk smelled sweet and plain. Delia sat cross-legged for a short visit, and they spoke softly about the garden path and the map that was not really a map yet somehow still was. Outside the last daylight faded to deep blue. Chioma’s hands, no longer busy with stones, rested calm in their lap while Mama hummed a short tune that filled the room like a soft blanket and the straw mat kept its friendly texture under one resting foot.

Later, after Delia had gone home with a wave and a promise to visit the path again tomorrow, Chioma climbed into bed. The sheets felt smooth and cool against their legs. Mama sat nearby and smoothed Chioma’s hair with slow fingers. Chioma remembered the bread crust left safe on its stone and the way the three of them had looked without taking. The room held the gentle hush of evening, and the air smelled faintly of clean cotton. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Chioma closed their eyes and breathed in the quiet house, already half dreaming of rain-washed paths and coastlines made of bread that needed only to be seen.
