In the snug cottage a tin clock gave a soft steady click from the shelf. Greta heard the small sound and opened their eyes wider, noticing how the late afternoon light fell in golden stripes across the windowsill. Jars of seeds and powders stood in careful rows, trays held smooth buttons and spools, and lengths of drying wool hung like quiet clouds. The bookmark waited under a folded square of cloth, its fresh ink stamp still shiny and wet. Greta stepped closer, feeling the mild warmth of the sun on their bare arms, and saw the ferryman sitting on a low stool with his weather-worn bag at his feet.

Greta reached one finger toward the cloth covering the bookmark, then paused. “I want to lift the corner just a little and see if the stamp looks clear,” Greta said in a hushed voice. The ferryman smiled and shook his head slowly. “Leave it covered a while longer, Greta,” he answered. “Ink likes its own slow time.” Greta nodded, remembering the cloth felt dry and smooth under their fingertips, and they let the bookmark rest untouched while the scent of drying wool drifted gently through the air.

Beside Greta sat Suri, who had come to visit and help sort the trays. Suri held out a small polished pebble that felt cool and glassy against Greta’s palm when they took it for a moment. “We can keep our hands busy with something else,” Suri whispered. Greta returned the pebble to the tray and looked at the neat pile of cloth napkins waiting on the table. Each napkin had a tiny pear stitched in green thread near one corner. Greta decided the quiet task would be folding them so every stitched pear faced upward, ready for supper later.

Greta picked up the first napkin and felt its soft woven texture as they folded it into a neat square. The ferryman watched with kind eyes and said, “Those pears look cheerful when they point the right way.” Greta answered, “I like how the green thread catches the light.” Suri joined in, folding one napkin carefully too. The room stayed warm from the low fire, and Greta worked slowly, matching each fold so the pear sat perfectly on top. Outside the window a gentle breeze stirred the drying wool, making it sway like sleepy sheep.

After four napkins Greta glanced again at the covered bookmark but kept their hands on the cloth instead. “Is the ink dry yet, do you think?” Greta asked the ferryman. “Not quite,” the ferryman replied in his deep calm voice. “It finishes when it is ready, and we wait together.” Greta breathed in and noticed the fresh smell of basil rising from one open jar on the sill, green and bright like a summer garden. They folded two more napkins, feeling peaceful as the light grew softer and gold turned toward rose.

Suri hummed a little tune without words while stacking the finished napkins in a tidy tower. Greta ran one finger along the edge of a tray and felt how smooth the wood had become from many careful hands. The ferryman reached into his bag and drew out a small packet of river stones, setting them beside the jars so they caught the fading light. Greta kept folding, making certain every stitched pear faced up, and the quiet rhythm of the work filled the cottage like a lullaby. No one hurried, and the bookmark stayed safe under its cloth.

Greta finished the last napkin and set it on the stack with a soft pat. The air near the window felt cooler now as evening settled in. “We waited well,” Greta said to Suri and the ferryman. Suri smiled and answered, “The pears are all smiling up at us.” The ferryman nodded and rested his hands on his knees. Greta looked at the covered bookmark once more, still leaving it alone, and noticed how the drying wool had grown quieter as the breeze slowed. The room held a comfortable stillness broken only by the gentle sounds of breathing and settling wood.

Slowly Greta lifted the cloth at last and saw the ink stamp had dried into a clear dark shape of a little boat. The bookmark was ready. Greta held it carefully so the paper felt firm and dry between their fingers. “It finished in its own time,” Greta said softly. The ferryman took the bookmark with both hands and placed it safely in his bag for the next crossing. Suri clapped once, quietly, and the last of the daylight painted everything in calm pink and gray. Greta felt glad they had folded the napkins and waited without peeking too soon.

Greta sat on the cushion by the sill while the ferryman and Suri stayed close. The jars and trays and soft wool looked peaceful in the growing dusk. Greta rested their hands in their lap and watched the quiet room. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Everyone felt the gentle hush that comes when a small task is done and the evening deepens, and Greta closed their eyes with a contented sigh as the warm cottage held them safe.
