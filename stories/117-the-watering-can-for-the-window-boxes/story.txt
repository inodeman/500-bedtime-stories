Grandma Nia stood already among the baskets, jars, and flower pots that lined the market lane, both hands gripping the long spout of the watering can meant for the high window boxes while she tried to lift and tip it toward the bright blooms above a flower stall. Soft golden light slipped between the canvas awnings and warmed the cobblestones under her shoes. She hummed under her breath and shifted her weight, water trembling near the rim. Golda stayed close beside her, watching the awkward angle and feeling the pleasant warmth of the late afternoon air on their bare arms. The market voices stayed low and friendly, and Golda kept their eyes on the can so they would know exactly when to step in.

Golda moved nearer without a word at first, their sneakers quiet on the stones, and rested one steady hand on the lower handle to share the weight. The metal of the watering can felt smooth and slightly cool against Golda’s palm. “I can hold this part steady for you, Grandma Nia,” Golda said in a calm voice. Grandma Nia glanced down with a gentle smile and answered, “That helps more than you know, Golda.” Together they raised the spout a little higher so the water reached the thirsty window-box flowers in a clear silver stream. A faint breeze carried the sweet smell of honey from a nearby jar stall and brushed past Golda’s face as they kept the can balanced.

Golda noticed how the long spout needed one person to guide the tip while the other supported the heavy base filled with water, so they took that lower half of the job and held firm. The chalky hand of a nearby chalk-drawing child brushed lightly against Golda’s sleeve as the child hurried past with a bright stick of blue, leaving a dry powdery feeling that Golda wiped away with a soft smile. “Ready for the next box?” Grandma Nia asked. “Yes, I’m ready,” Golda answered, and they shuffled sideways together along the row of pots. The water poured evenly, and the leaves began to glisten without a single drop wasted on the ground.

They moved to the third high window box where red geraniums leaned outward, and Golda kept their grip solid while Grandma Nia aimed the spout with care. Cool shade from a taller stall fell across Golda’s shoulders and cooled the back of their neck. A vendor waved kindly from behind a pyramid of jars. Grandma Nia called softly, “Thank you for the clear path,” and the vendor nodded. Golda stayed quiet and focused, feeling proud inside only of the shared work itself. The watering can grew lighter as the water level dropped, and Golda adjusted their hold so Grandma Nia never had to strain.

When the fourth box needed attention, Golda spotted a small step-stool left beside a basket of herbs and slid it into place with one foot so Grandma Nia could stand more comfortably. The wood of the stool felt dry and solid under Golda’s shoe. “Almost finished with these,” Grandma Nia said. Golda replied, “We can do the last two together the same way.” They poured slowly, listening to the soft patter of water on soil, and the flowers seemed to lift their faces. Nearby baskets of ripe fruit added a quiet rustle whenever someone passed, and Golda kept the rhythm steady without rushing.

A single bright chime sounded from a small clock hanging above a pottery stall, and Golda and Grandma Nia waited through that single chime before tipping the can again. The clear note faded into the market air while Golda held the base motionless. Warm sunlight returned as a cloud drifted past and lit the wet petals so they sparkled. Gita appeared for a moment with a small bunch of herbs, waved to Golda, and whispered, “Looks like good teamwork,” before skipping on. Golda simply nodded and returned to the task, pouring the remaining water into the final high window box until every plant looked refreshed.

With the watering finished, Grandma Nia set the empty can down among the flower pots and rested one hand on Golda’s shoulder. The can’s spout still dripped once onto the warm stones with a tiny tick. “We made short work of that,” Grandma Nia said. Golda answered only, “The flowers look happy now.” They gathered a small empty jar Grandma Nia had bought earlier and the watering can itself, walking slowly back along the lane. The shadows stretched longer and cooler across the baskets, and Golda felt the evening air turning mild against their skin.

They reached the wooden gate at the end of the market lane, and the familiar click of a gate latch sounded as Grandma Nia opened it for them both. Inside the quiet courtyard the light had turned soft blue, and Golda carried the watering can the last few steps toward home. The metal no longer felt heavy now that it was empty. Grandma Nia unlocked the door, and they stepped into the snug front room where the furniture smelled faintly of clean wood. Golda placed the watering can carefully by the wall near the window boxes that belonged to their own house, ready for morning.

Golda washed their hands in the basin of lukewarm water, dried them on a soft towel, and climbed into the wide reading chair while Grandma Nia settled nearby with a folded blanket. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the last market sounds faded, and inside the room stayed still and safe. Golda leaned against the cushion and listened to Grandma Nia’s quiet breathing, already half dreaming of the bright flowers they had watered together.
