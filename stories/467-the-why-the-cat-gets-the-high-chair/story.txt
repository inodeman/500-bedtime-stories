The ferryman was already settled on the wide library window seat when Uma arrived, his weathered hands busy folding a soft blue cloth into neat squares beside a stack of picture books that smelled of clean paper. Afternoon light poured gold through the tall glass and rested warmly on Uma's cheeks as they climbed up and felt the deep cushion give under their legs. He hummed a river tune while he worked, the cloth whispering against his palms, and Uma wriggled closer until their shoulder brushed his sleeve. Outside, a fig tree leaned near the building. "I have something to tell you," the ferryman said in his slow steady voice, "about why the cat's blanket goes on the high chair." Uma's bigger news jumped behind their teeth at once, yet they stayed quiet and watched him smooth the last corner of the cloth.

Uma opened their mouth because the news felt bright and important, a drawing of a little boat they had made that morning with careful crayons, but the ferryman's voice rolled on like quiet water and Uma pressed their lips together and listened instead. The window seat felt pleasantly firm where the old wood met the cushion edge, and Uma rested one hand there while the story began. From the small kitchenette down the hall the smell of warm bread drifted through the open doorway and filled the air between the bookshelves. The ferryman laid the blue cloth across his lap as if it were the very blanket in the tale and spoke of a kitchen full of gentle clinks. "It began on a quiet evening," he said, "when the cat decided the high chair was the best place for dreaming after the plates were cleared." Uma nodded slowly and kept their own news folded away like a secret letter.

He described how the cat had watched the family move about the kitchen, how the high chair stood empty after supper with its wooden arms open like a small waiting boat, and how the light from the stove made everything soft and orange. Uma could almost hear those friendly plate sounds as they sat very still on the library seat. A cloud passed outside and the light on the cushions turned a cooler silver for a moment before the gold returned. "The cat carried its blanket in its mouth," the ferryman continued, "and climbed the rungs one careful step at a time until the seat was covered just right." Uma imagined the blanket's worn softness and wanted to mention their boat drawing again, yet they waited, breathing the calm air of the tall shelves all around them and watching dust float in the beams.

Outside the library window the wind moved through the fig leaves with a soft shushing sound that rose and fell like slow breathing, and Uma turned their head to catch it while the ferryman spoke on about the cat settling deeper into the blanket. The air near the glass felt cooler than the rest of the room and brushed Uma's bare arm. They almost asked why the sofa would not do, yet held the question inside. The ferryman said, "The high chair was high enough to see the whole kitchen, and the blanket made it soft enough for long naps while the spoons kept stirring below." Uma's fingers found the cool ceramic rim of a little cup left on the window ledge, smooth and pleasant under their touch, and they traced it once before folding their hands again in their lap.

The story went gently forward. The cat slept there every afternoon while the family walked back and forth underneath, and no one minded because the blanket stayed neat and the chair stayed ready when mealtime returned. Uma watched more dust motes dance in a late beam of light that crossed the window seat like a quiet road. The ferryman's voice stayed low and even, matching the hush of the library. "One day the child of the house asked the same sort of question you might ask," he told Uma, and they leaned in closer, the cushions holding a faint leftover warmth from the long sit. Their bigger news still fluttered inside them like a small bird wanting out, yet Uma kept listening all the way without a single interruption.

"The answer," the ferryman said, smoothing the blue cloth once more, "was that the high chair was a special island. From there the cat could watch the bread cooling on the counter and hear the soft talk of the kitchen, and the blanket turned the hard seat into a cloud for dreaming." Uma felt the steady temperature of the room, comfortable and even, and noticed how the light had grown mellower as the afternoon stretched. They listened to every word as if each one were a stepping stone across a calm stream. The last of the story approached, and Uma's own news waited patiently now, smaller and quieter than before, ready for its turn only when the telling was truly done.

He reached the final sentence with care, his hands resting still on the cloth. "And that is why the cat's blanket goes on the high chair, so the naps can be high and soft and full of kitchen dreams," the ferryman finished, and the library grew even quieter except for the distant friendly tick of a clock on a far desk. Uma felt the soft give of the cushion under their knees and the gentle weight of the finished tale settling around them. They had listened the whole way to the very last word. Now the real question rose naturally, and after it they could share something small of their own without rushing.

"Does the cat ever share the blanket with anyone?" Uma asked, looking at the blue cloth still resting on the ferryman's lap. He nodded slowly, and the kind light on his face made the window seat feel even safer. "Sometimes with a patient friend who climbs up carefully and stays quiet," he answered. Then Uma shared their small thing. "I drew a little boat this morning," they said softly, "and I put a tiny blanket in the bottom of it too." The window seat creaked once as they shifted closer, and outside the fig leaves kept their soft motion while the two of them sat together in the calm.

Uma stayed a moment longer while the story rested inside them like a warm quilt. They picked up the empty bowl that had held a few crackers earlier, carried it carefully around the corner to the little sink, washed nothing because it was already clean, and came straight back to the window seat where the ferryman waited with the cloth now folded away. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Uma leaned into the cushion and watched the evening light gather beyond the glass, feeling the air cool a little against their arms as the day eased toward night, and they closed their eyes halfway amid the friendly hush of books and the quiet company beside them.
