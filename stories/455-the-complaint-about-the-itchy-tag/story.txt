Auntie Luz sat already on the low stool by Isla’s bed, turning a pale green shirt inside out under the warm circle of the lamp. The cotton felt warm from her hands as she smoothed the seams. Isla settled against the pillows, hearing the quiet hush of the room and the soft click of the lamp switch settling. Outside the open window the evening air drifted in, cool on Isla’s cheeks. Auntie Luz held up the collar and said, “Isla, I need to tell you about this shirt and the little tag that bothered me all afternoon.” Isla opened their mouth, ready with bigger news from the garden, yet stayed quiet and watched the fabric move.

Auntie Luz began far from the tag itself, speaking slowly while the lamp light caught the thread she pulled free. She talked first about the morning market where she chose the green cloth, how the vendor folded it with care, and how the walk home took longer because she stopped to greet a neighbor. Isla felt the cool pillowcase under their cheek and wanted to jump in with news about the fig tree, but they kept still. The shirt lay across Auntie Luz’s knees like a soft map. “I washed it twice,” Auntie Luz said, “and hung it in the sun, and still something scratched when I tried it on.” Isla breathed in and out and waited.

The story wandered next through the afternoon sewing basket and the search for tiny scissors. Auntie Luz described opening three drawers and finding a button that did not belong, then a spool of blue thread that reminded her of the river. Isla’s toes pressed into the blanket’s gentle weight while the room stayed peaceful and still. Outside, wind in the fig leaves made a dry soft rustle that rose and fell like quiet breathing. Isla almost spoke then, yet remembered they had promised themselves to hear every word. Auntie Luz smiled at Isla and kept going without hurry.

She told Isla how she sat by the window earlier and turned the shirt over and over, looking for the place that itched. She mentioned the way the sleeves fell, the way the hem curled, and the way a loose thread tickled her wrist. The lamp threw a steady honey-colored light across the floorboards. Isla watched the light and felt the air cool against their bare arms. “I nearly put the shirt away,” Auntie Luz said, “but I kept thinking Isla would like the color, so I stayed with it.” Isla nodded once, still holding their own news carefully inside.

Only after all that winding path did Auntie Luz reach the point. She pinched a tiny white square of fabric at the back of the collar and lifted it so Isla could see. “This little tag was stiff and scratchy,” she said at last, “and it sat right against the neck no matter how I smoothed it.” Isla heard the final sentence settle into the quiet room. The shirt now smelled faintly of beeswax from the small candle Auntie Luz had lit earlier on the dresser, a clean warm scent that mixed with the evening air. Isla stayed silent one more breath, making sure the story had truly ended.

Isla looked at the tiny tag resting between Auntie Luz’s fingers and asked one real question. “Auntie Luz, does the shirt feel soft now that the tag is gone?” Auntie Luz nodded and held the collar out so Isla could touch the place where the tag had been. The fabric there was smooth and kind. Isla then shared something small of their own, voice soft in the lamp glow. “I found a leaf today under the fig tree,” Isla said, “and I touched a leaf’s soft underside and it felt like velvet.” Auntie Luz listened with the same calm care Isla had given her.

They sat together while Auntie Luz snipped the last bit of thread and set the green shirt neatly on the chair. Isla ran their hand once more over the collar and felt how free it was. The bedroom held the gentle heat of the lamp and the cooler breath from the window. Auntie Luz said, “Thank you for waiting all the way through, Isla.” Isla answered, “I wanted to hear the end.” Their voices stayed low and even, fitting the quiet hour before sleep.

Isla tucked their feet under the blanket and watched Auntie Luz blow the candle lower so only a thin flame remained. The shirt waited on the chair like a friendly guest. Isla thought of the long winding talk and how the scratchy part had finally come clear, and how their own small leaf news had found its place afterward. The room smelled clean and calm. Auntie Luz reached over and smoothed Isla’s hair with a light, steady hand.

Isla closed their eyes halfway and listened to the soft sounds left in the room, the settling of cloth and the far rustle beyond the glass. Auntie Luz stayed nearby, quiet now, while the green shirt rested ready for morning. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Isla felt safe and finished and ready for sleep, the long explanation and the small leaf both held gently in the same warm hour.
