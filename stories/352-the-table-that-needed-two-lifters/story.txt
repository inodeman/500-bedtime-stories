A faint tick-tick drifted from the far counter while the ovens still slept cool and dark. Tamsin paused with one hand on the wooden doorframe and listened, feeling the early chill of the tile floor seep through their socks. The kitchen waited quiet and wide, ready for the early table that would soon hold warm loaves and soft rolls. Tamsin stepped inside and drew a slow breath of clean flour dust floating in the pale gray light that slipped under the back door. They liked this hour when everything still felt soft and unhurried.

Tamsin walked toward the middle of the room where a broad oak table stood a little out of place, its surface smooth under the dim glow of a single wall lamp. The table needed two lifters and a careful count of three before it could settle into its proper spot near the mixing bowls. Tamsin rested both palms on the cool wood and felt how heavy it truly was. Nearby, a bowl of washed greens released a bright scent of basil that curled gently through the air. Tamsin smiled and called softly toward the pantry, hoping someone else was already awake.

Pilar appeared first, rubbing sleep from their eyes and carrying a small cloth bag that clinked once against their knee. “I brought the extra napkins,” Pilar said, voice still low and warm. Tamsin nodded and pointed to the heavy table. “We have to move it together, but only if we go slow,” Tamsin answered. The two friends stood side by side for a moment, feeling the quiet press of the room around them while steam began to rise faintly from a kettle on the back burner. The air felt mild now, neither cold nor hot, just right for careful work.

Ms. Okonkwo stepped in from the hallway, her apron already tied and her steps soft on the worn floorboards. She looked at the table, then at the two children, and spoke in a calm voice that matched the hush of the kitchen. “If one person rushes, the whole lift will go crooked,” she said. Tamsin listened closely and felt the smooth edge of a polished pebble they had found earlier in the garden and kept safe in a pocket. Ms. Okonkwo offered only a count or a quiet name for the little knot of effort they would need, nothing more, and then she stepped back to watch with kind eyes.

Tamsin and Pilar decided at once to split the work into two slow jobs so no one would hurry. First they cleared the surface together, sliding empty trays and a flour tin aside with careful hands. The tin clock on the shelf above the sink began its gentle marking of the minutes, each soft beat steady and clear. Tamsin wiped the wood with a damp cloth that felt cool and slightly rough, while Pilar folded the clean napkins so the stitched pear on each one faced neatly upward. They worked without rush, matching their breathing to the quiet kitchen light that grew a little warmer as the sky outside brightened.

When the tabletop stood empty, Tamsin took one end and Pilar took the other, both of them testing the weight with small lifts that barely raised the legs. “Ready when you are,” Tamsin said. Pilar answered, “We lift only on the count, nothing sooner.” Ms. Okonkwo stood nearby and spoke the name they had chosen for the careful moment: “Steady hands.” The words floated softly through the room while the children adjusted their grips on the solid wood that still held a faint warmth from yesterday’s baking.

Together they bent their knees and waited. Ms. Okonkwo counted in a clear, unhurried voice: “One… two… three.” On three they lifted at the same slow pace, carrying the table only a few careful steps until it rested exactly where the early loaves would soon sit. The legs settled with a soft, solid sound against the floor. Tamsin and Pilar both let out quiet breaths and smiled at each other across the now-settled surface. The kitchen felt brighter, filled with the mild heat that had begun to gather near the ovens.

They spent a few more peaceful minutes checking that every napkin lay straight and that the mixing bowls stood ready in a neat row. Tamsin ran one finger along the table’s edge one last time, feeling how solid and ready it had become. Pilar handed over the small cloth bag, and together they placed a single clean towel at the center. Outside the window the sky turned the soft color of fresh milk, and inside the air stayed gentle and still. Ms. Okonkwo gave a small nod of thanks and began measuring flour for the first dough of the day.

Tamsin sat for a moment on the low stool by the window and watched the quiet kitchen settle around the early table they had moved with such care. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Pilar joined them and rested a warm hand on Tamsin’s shoulder while the last cool patches of night air drifted away and the room filled with the mild promise of bread yet to rise.
