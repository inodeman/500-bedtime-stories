The soft tick of a tin clock filled the quiet bedroom first. Jorie noticed the gentle sound right away and turned their face toward the little wooden shelf where the clock rested, its round face catching a pool of golden lamp light. Warm air rose from the floor vent and brushed Jorie’s bare ankles. They sat in the middle of the thick bedroom rug that held a loose circle of plump cushions, soft puppets with bright yarn hair, and wide sheets of paper ready for drawing. Jorie pressed one palm into a cushion and felt the slow sink of it. Papa sat nearby in the armchair with a book open on his knee, and the whole room felt calm and ready.

Jorie arranged three puppets in a careful row on the nearest cushion, their felt hands folded as if they were waiting for a story. The paper lay smooth under the edge of another cushion, and crayons rested in a shallow box that smelled faintly of wax. Soft blue light from the hallway spilled across the rug edge. Jorie looked up when soft footsteps paused at the open doorway. Eitan stood there holding a folded paper bird, eyes on the circle of cushions and puppets but not stepping closer. The air near the door felt cooler than the warm spot where Jorie sat. Papa glanced up once, then went back to his page so the children could decide what came next.

“Hi, Eitan,” Jorie said in a voice no louder than the clock’s tick. “We have puppets and paper right here on the rug.” Eitan took one small step but stayed just outside the cushion circle, the paper bird still in both hands. Jorie remembered the rule they had made earlier that afternoon: only two players could sit inside the cushion ring at a time so the puppets would have enough room to dance. The rule had felt fair when it was just Jorie and the imaginary friends, yet now it left Eitan standing on the plain floor. Cooler air from the hallway touched Jorie’s cheek as they thought. The puppets’ yarn hair looked fluffy in the lamp glow.

Jorie picked up one cushion and moved it a hand’s width outward, then shifted a second cushion the same way until a clear open space appeared between them like a small doorway. The rug fibers felt springy under Jorie’s knees as they worked. Warm light from the bedside lamp made the paper sheets look almost golden. “The circle can be bigger,” Jorie said quietly. “There is room for three if we sit this way.” Eitan’s shoulders lowered a little. Papa turned a page very slowly, the paper whispering, but he did not speak. Jorie patted the new open spot once so the cushion settled flat.

Eitan stepped onto the rug and sat cross-legged in the fresh space, the paper bird resting on one knee. Jorie handed over a puppet with button eyes and a stitched smile. The puppet’s cloth body felt soft and a little worn from many evenings of play. Soft yellow light pooled on the three of them now. “We can take turns making the puppets talk, and anyone can draw on the paper whenever they want,” Jorie said. Eitan nodded and lifted the puppet so its head bobbed. The new seating made the old rule stretch just enough that everyone fit without anyone feeling pushed out or called special out loud.

They began a slow story about a bird who visited a garden of cushions. Jorie moved one puppet in tiny hops across a sheet of paper while Eitan made the second puppet answer in a soft voice. “The bird likes the sunny spots,” Eitan said, and the puppet nodded. Warmth from the floor vent curled around their legs. Jorie reached for a crayon and drew a small path so the puppets had somewhere to walk. Papa’s chair creaked once as he shifted, still quiet, letting the children keep the pace gentle and shared. The cushions held them in a friendly oval that no longer felt closed.

After a while Jorie noticed a small plate Papa had set on the low table earlier. On it lay two curls of bright orange peel left from an afternoon snack. The fresh citrus smell drifted over when Jorie leaned close. They offered the plate toward Eitan without words. Eitan took a tiny piece and sniffed it too, smiling. The puppets rested against the cushions for a rest. Cooler air no longer reached them now that the doorway space was filled. Jorie smoothed a wrinkle from a sheet of paper and felt how smooth it stayed under their fingers.

Papa rose once to fill a glass at the bathroom sink, and the quiet drip of water sounded for a moment before he turned the tap off. Jorie counted the soft drips in their head without looking, just twelve, then returned full attention to the rug. Eitan folded the paper bird’s wing a little neater. “Your turn to choose the next thing the puppets do,” Jorie said. Eitan thought, then had the puppets sit side by side and look at the drawings together. The lamp light stayed steady and kind. Jorie felt the evening stretch gently around them like another soft cushion.

Later the crayons went back into their box and the puppets lay down for pretend sleep on a folded corner of the rug. Jorie reached for the linen napkin that had covered the snack plate and ran their thumb across its cool, lightly woven surface before setting it aside. Papa closed his book and gave them both a small nod that said he had seen how they solved the seating and the rule all on their own. Eitan yawned behind one hand. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Jorie felt the last warmth of the floor vent and knew the rug of cushions, puppets, and paper had held enough room for everyone after all.
