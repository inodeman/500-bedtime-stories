The warm smell of honey drifted from the open doorway where the hall light still spilled a steady golden rectangle across the rug, and Femi’s hands were already busy smoothing the soft folds of a spare blanket they had pulled from the low shelf. The blanket felt cool at the edges and warmer where it had rested against the wall, and Femi worked slowly so the fabric would lie flat without a single wrinkle. Outside the half-open window a gate latch clicked once in the quiet evening air and then fell silent again. Femi paused, listening, then returned to the blanket, pressing each corner down with careful palms until the whole thing looked ready for someone to sit.

Femi glanced toward the cushioned chair that stood beside the window and saw at once what Grandma Nia had done. A thick folded towel and an extra pillow had been placed just so, turning the ordinary chair into a snug seat that faced slightly away from the draft, exactly the way a sheltered seat on a ferry would face away from open water. A small paper ferry ticket lay on the armrest with Femi’s name written in gentle looping letters, and the hall light made the paper glow pale yellow. Femi’s bare feet felt the slight coolness of the floorboards as they stepped closer, and the whole arrangement smelled faintly of the same honey that still hung in the air from supper.

Grandma Nia came in carrying a glass of water, her steps soft on the rug. The hall light behind her threw a long calm shadow that reached almost to Femi’s toes. “I chose the sheltered side for you,” Grandma Nia said, her voice low and even. Femi touched the ticket with one finger and felt how smooth it was. The room stayed pleasantly warm, neither too hot nor chilly, and the pillow invited a small lean of the shoulder. Femi knew a quick thank-you would slip away too fast, so they looked around for one useful thing Grandma Nia had not asked for.

Femi spotted the stack of picture books that had slid into a crooked tower near the bed. Without a word they knelt and began to straighten them, aligning each spine so the titles faced the same way and the stack stood steady. The pages felt dry and soft under their fingertips, and the hall light caught the pale edges of the paper. When the books stood neat, Femi carried the glass of water Grandma Nia had brought and set it carefully on the little table beside the special seat so it would be within easy reach. The glass made a tiny clear sound against the wood.

“I noticed the seat you chose,” Femi said, standing up straight and looking at Grandma Nia. Grandma Nia’s hand rested for a moment on Femi’s shoulder; the skin of that hand felt chalky from the garden chalk she had used earlier to mark the walkway stones, a dry light powder that brushed away at once. “You did not have to set the books right,” Grandma Nia answered, smiling with quiet surprise. Femi shook their head gently. “I wanted to,” they said. The air near the window carried a cooler breath than the rest of the room, yet the new seat stayed protected and still.

Grandma Nia settled into the rocking chair opposite and began to hum a short tune without words. Femi climbed into the sheltered seat and tucked the blanket around their legs so the fabric made a soft nest. The pillow gave a little under their weight, and the hall light painted a calm stripe across the blanket’s surface. Outside, the evening had grown deeper, but inside everything stayed gentle. Femi ran one hand along the arm of the chair and felt the smooth wood that Grandma Nia had wiped clean earlier.

“Will the ferry be like this tomorrow?” Femi asked after a comfortable silence. Grandma Nia nodded. “The seat will face the quiet side, just the way this one does,” she said. Femi watched the way the hall light made the ticket shine and felt glad all over again that the useful work with the books had already been finished. They waited through a single soft chime from the small clock on the shelf, letting the note fade completely before they spoke once more. “I saw what you did for me,” Femi said plainly. Grandma Nia’s eyes crinkled at the corners in the warm light.

Femi leaned back and let their shoulders rest. The blanket held a mild warmth now from their own body, and the water glass waited nearby if they grew thirsty. Grandma Nia told a short story about how the ferry would move slowly across the water while birds called far above, and Femi listened without needing to hurry. Every now and then Femi’s fingers found the edge of the ticket again and traced the letters of their own name. The room smelled clean and settled, and the hall light kept its steady glow without flicker.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Femi’s eyelids grew heavier while Grandma Nia kept her quiet place in the rocking chair, and the special seat held them both safe and still until sleep arrived like another soft blanket settling down.
