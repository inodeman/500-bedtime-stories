The small card rested on the nightstand where the hall light still poured through the open doorway in a soft yellow band, and Golda leaned so near that every loop of Grandma Nia’s handwriting stood clear. The card held the plan for the walk timed so you would see the baker’s window lit, the ink dry and steady under Golda’s gaze. Golda felt the paper’s slight daytime warmth against cooler fingertips and watched the hall light glide across the words like a slow brush of paint. The bedroom air carried a hush broken only by the faint settling of the house beams. Golda studied the card once more, the cream surface smooth except for one tiny crease, and pictured the baker’s panes already glowing as promised earlier that evening.

Golda sat back against the pillows and remembered how Grandma Nia had chosen the exact minute to leave, guiding them along the quiet street until the bakery glass shone bright with rows of rounded loaves. The sidewalk had felt solid under their shoes, and the evening temperature had rested gently on Golda’s cheeks. Now, in the bedroom with the hall light still on, Golda noticed that Grandma Nia had already taken care of every part of that walk timed so you would see the baker’s window lit, from the careful timing to the unhurried steps that let them pause and look. The blanket under Golda’s palms held a familiar softness. A low creak sounded from the hallway as if the house itself approved.

A quick thank-you felt too thin for planning that shone so brightly in Golda’s mind. Golda swung their legs over the side of the bed and stood on the cool floorboards, deciding to do one useful thing Grandma Nia had not asked for. The hall light stretched farther now, painting a calm path across the rug. Golda’s nightshirt brushed lightly against their arms with each small movement. They wanted the thank-you to show in an action first, something quiet and helpful that would leave the room tidier than before. The idea warmed Golda from the inside the way a held cup of cocoa might.

Golda walked to the low basket near the dresser where clean washcloths and a folded throw waited in a loose pile. They began smoothing and stacking each cloth with slow care, the fabric warm from the earlier dryer. While Golda worked, the sweet green smell of mint leaves drifted in from the small pot on the windowsill that Grandma Nia kept for evening tea. The hall light still glowed steadily behind them, turning the edges of the stacked cloths a gentle gold. Golda matched corners to corners until the basket looked neat and ready. The useful work settled into their hands like a soft rhythm.

A soft jingle reached Golda then, the sound of a cat’s collar bell, as the gray cat padded in from the hall and brushed against their ankle. Golda crouched and ran one hand along the cat’s back, feeling the tiny glass bead that hung from the collar cord, round and cool and smooth under their fingertip. The cat purred a low vibration that Golda could sense through their palm. Hall light touched the cat’s whiskers and made them gleam. Golda smiled and kept stroking until the cat curled onto the rug in a contented circle, the bell now quiet against the floor.

Grandma Nia appeared in the doorway, her slippers whispering on the boards, and the hall light framed her shoulders in a kind outline. “I see the washcloths found their places,” Grandma Nia said, her voice warm as fresh bread. Golda looked up and answered, “I wanted the basket tidy before sleep.” Grandma Nia stepped fully into the room and rested a gentle hand on the dresser. The air between them felt pleasantly mild. Golda stood and brushed a stray thread from their sleeve while the cat blinked slowly nearby.

Golda took a small breath and spoke again. “Grandma Nia, I noticed you timed the walk so we would see the baker’s window lit.” Grandma Nia’s eyes crinkled at the corners. “I hoped the yellow light would feel like a welcome,” she said. Golda nodded and added, “It did. The loaves looked soft behind the glass.” The hall light still reached across the floor in its steady stripe, and the temperature in the room stayed even and kind. Golda felt the day gather itself into something complete and quiet.

They both moved toward the bed. Golda climbed in while Grandma Nia smoothed the top edge of the blanket with practiced hands. “The mint on the sill is ready for morning tea if you like,” Grandma Nia said. Golda replied, “I smelled it while I folded, and it made the room feel fresh.” The pillow cradled Golda’s head with its familiar give. Outside the window the night rested still, and inside the only motion was the slow rise and fall of the cat’s side on the rug. Golda’s eyelids grew pleasantly heavy under the calm glow from the hall.

Grandma Nia kissed Golda’s forehead and turned toward the door, leaving the hall light on just as it had been. Golda settled deeper into the sheets that held a clean cotton softness against their skin. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Golda watched its steady circle of light for a few peaceful breaths, hearing only the faintest house sounds, and let their thoughts rest on the baker’s bright panes, the neat stack of cloths, and the plain words they had spoken. The bedroom wrapped around Golda like a held hand, warm enough and quiet enough for sleep to arrive without hurry.
