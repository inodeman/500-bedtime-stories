"Would you like to sit by the drum first, Kei?" Auntie Luz asked, her voice soft as the warm gold light that spilled across the music corner. Kei stood beside the smooth wooden drum and the pale recorder resting on a low shelf, and through the open window they could see a white ferry moving slowly over the bright water. Cool air brushed Kei’s bare arms while the ferry’s distant horn seemed almost friendly. Auntie Luz kept her hands folded and did not rush. Kei rocked once on their heels and felt the soft rug under their socks. Outside, the water glittered like spilled coins, and the ferry waited, patient and far.

Kei had thought about a wave big enough for the ferry to notice, or not, all afternoon, and the thought made their chest feel tight and shy. They looked at Auntie Luz, then at the drum’s round top that held a faint smell of fresh paper from the sheet music lying open beside it. Kei did not pretend they did not care. Instead they stayed quiet and honest inside the hush of the room. A gate latch clicked softly somewhere down the path, a small clear sound that made Kei glance toward the window again. The ferry slid along like a quiet animal, and Kei wondered if a little wave could ever reach that far. Auntie Luz hummed a tiny note and waited.

Ximena sat cross-legged near the recorder and tapped one finger gently on her knee so the light made tiny shadows dance. “We can just play soft sounds,” Ximena said. “No big show.” Kei nodded and rested both palms on their knees, feeling the calm fabric of their pants and the steady beat of their own breathing. The music corner felt safe, filled with the warm glow of late day and the cool breath of the sea. Auntie Luz smiled at both children and did not ask for anything loud. Kei watched the ferry’s white shape and decided the smallest brave version might be enough. The air tasted clean and salty near the open window.

Auntie Luz lifted the recorder and offered it with open hands. “You may try one quiet note if you want, Kei,” she said. “Or none at all. I am happy either way.” Kei took the recorder carefully. Its surface felt smooth and cool under their fingers. They pressed it to their lips and blew a single low tone that wandered into the room like a shy bird. The sound was small yet clear enough to be heard. Ximena grinned and tapped the drum once with the soft side of her hand so a gentle boom answered. Kei felt braver by a tiny step. Outside, the ferry kept its slow path across the shining water.

Kei set the recorder down and walked three steps closer to the window where the light lay in warm stripes on the floor. They could see the ferry more clearly now, a white oval against blue. Their fingers brushed a damp clay wall beside the window frame, cool and slightly grainy, a leftover texture from the old seaside house that somehow made the music corner feel rooted and real. Kei breathed in and out. Auntie Luz stayed near the drum and did not push for a performance. “Whenever you are ready,” Auntie Luz said kindly, “or not at all.” Kei let the words settle like soft blankets.

Kei lifted one hand, not high, just high enough that the ferry might notice if someone on the deck looked their way. They moved their palm once in a small arc, a wave that belonged only to them. The motion felt quiet and true. Sunlight warmed the back of Kei’s hand while a soft breeze touched their wrist. Ximena whispered, “I saw that.” Kei’s cheeks felt warm, not from shame but from the gentle heat of trying. They waved a second time, still small, still clear enough to be a real greeting across the water. The ferry continued on its calm course, and Kei imagined someone aboard might have smiled.

Back at the music corner, Kei sat again and rested both palms on their knees once more, letting the steady pressure settle their body. Auntie Luz tapped the drum in a slow heartbeat rhythm, soft as rain on a roof. Kei picked up the recorder and answered with two quiet notes that floated together like friends. The room filled with gentle sound and the pale afternoon light. No one asked for more. Kei felt the shyness loosen like a knot undoing itself. Outside, the water lapped in a hush against the shore far below, and the ferry grew a little smaller yet somehow closer in Kei’s mind because they had tried.

Ximena rolled a soft mallet across the drum skin so it made a rolling whisper. “That was a good wave,” she said. Kei answered, “It was the smallest brave one I could do.” Auntie Luz nodded and kept her kind eyes steady. The three of them stayed in the music corner while the light turned honey-colored and the air cooled by degrees. Kei listened to their own breathing match the soft drum and felt proud in a quiet way that needed no big words. The ferry slipped farther along the bright water, carrying the tiny wave with it like a secret gift. Kei smiled at Auntie Luz and felt the day folding gently toward evening.

Later the room grew dim and peaceful, and Kei leaned against Auntie Luz’s side while Ximena put the recorder back on its shelf. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Warm light pooled on the floor near the drum, and the last cool breeze carried the sea’s clean breath into the quiet music corner where Kei’s small brave wave still lived in memory like a calm song.
