"Hold this end gentle and slow, Anil," said Uncle Idris as the soft morning light painted gold streaks across the harbor water. Anil felt the cool dampness of the wooden dock boards under their feet while they gripped the loose ball of garden string meant later for a straight row of seedlings. Nearby baskets of early vegetables sat ready to be passed up to the waiting hands on the boat deck, and the faint smell of pear skins drifted from a crate where someone had peeled fruit for sharing. Nils stepped closer and brushed a warm sleeve against Anil's arm so they knew help stood ready. Wind in the fig leaves whispered from the trees planted along the shore path, adding a quiet rustle to the small waves. Anil kept their eyes on the pale string and promised not to hurry even though the baskets waited.

Anil and Nils looked at the long stretch of dock where the string needed to run straight beside the baskets so none would tip when lifted. If one person rushed the line would twist and the row would wobble, so Anil spoke first. "We can split it into two slow jobs," Anil said. Nils smiled and answered, "You walk the far end while I keep the near coils neat." Uncle Idris only watched with kind eyes and offered nothing more yet. The air felt pleasantly cool against Anil's wrists as they began to unroll the string a little at a time. Light from the rising sun warmed the tops of the stacked baskets and made the harbor boards glow a soft brown.

Anil walked carefully toward the far post while Nils stayed by the first baskets and fed the string without tugging. The texture of a warm coin that Uncle Idris had pressed into Anil's pocket for luck rested against their palm each time they reached down to steady themselves. "Count the loops with me if you like," Uncle Idris said from his place near the middle, his voice low and steady. Anil listened and moved only when the count felt right. The water made tiny clicking sounds against the pilings and the pear skins scent still hung gently in the air. Nils matched Anil's pace so the line never jerked or knotted too soon.

Together Anil and Nils kept the string floating just above the dock surface like a pale ribbon. Anil felt the mild temperature of the breeze on the back of their neck as they paused to let Nils catch up. "This knot is called a simple overhand when we finish," Uncle Idris told them, naming it without taking the string himself. Anil repeated the name under their breath so it stayed clear. Baskets waited in patient rows, their woven sides rough yet sturdy under the growing light. Nils hummed a quiet tune that mixed with the distant gull calls and the soft slap of water.

Anil reached the far cleat and held the string end firm while Nils worked the middle section free of any twist. The boards felt smooth and sun-warmed now under Anil's toes. "Ready for the baskets to line up?" Nils asked. Anil nodded and answered, "Yes, slow as we planned." Uncle Idris gave a soft count of three so they could lower the string into place together. The harbor smelled clean and the fig-leaf sound continued like a lullaby. Light flickered through thin clouds and touched Anil's hair with gentle brightness.

They guided the first baskets into the straight space the string marked, passing each one up only after it sat true. Anil's hands moved carefully over the basket rims that felt dry and fibrous. Nils mirrored every motion so no rush spoiled the line. Uncle Idris spoke the knot name once more when the far end needed securing. The cool edge of the morning had softened into mild warmth that settled on Anil's shoulders. Water lapped in a steady rhythm that matched their breathing.

Anil and Nils traded places for the last few baskets so each could feel both jobs. The string stayed level and pale against the darker wood. Anil sensed the quiet pride of finishing without hurry while the pear-skin freshness still lingered faintly. Nils steadied a heavy basket until Anil could pass it safely upward. Uncle Idris kept his count soft and even, never grabbing the work. Light now rested fully on the dock and turned every board a friendly honey color.

When the final basket rose clear, Anil and Nils drew the garden string back into a neat loose coil for the later row at home. Anil felt the mild air current across their freckled cheeks and heard only peaceful harbor sounds. "We did both jobs just right," Anil said. Nils agreed with a quiet grin. Uncle Idris smiled and reminded them of the overhand knot name one last time so they could use it in the garden. The dock grew still except for the gentle water and the far rustle of leaves.

Anil sat for a moment on a dry crate and straightened a small frayed edge of the coil with careful fingers while the day stayed calm around them. Nils leaned nearby and Uncle Idris rested a hand on the rail. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Anil watched its steady shine mix with the last harbor light and felt the whole morning settle into quiet readiness for home and the garden row still to come.
