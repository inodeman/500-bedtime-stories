The cream page of Mama’s chorus booklet lay open on the kitchen table, black notes dotted like resting birds along five thin lines, and morning light made the ink shine with a quiet silver gleam. Ilaria leaned so near that their breath warmed the paper’s edge. The chairs sat close, and a leather lace from Mama’s apron tie brushed Ilaria’s wrist whenever Mama shifted. Cool early air still touched Ilaria’s bare arms. “This is where we begin,” Mama said softly, resting one fingertip on the first measure. Ilaria felt bigger news pushing behind their teeth, yet they kept their eyes on those little notes and stayed still.

The wooden table felt smooth under Ilaria’s elbows as the chairs creaked once and settled even nearer before breakfast. Faint steam rose from the kettle and left a gentle damp warmth on Ilaria’s cheeks. Mama smoothed the page with her palm. Pale blue sky showed beyond the window, and toast scent drifted closer without hurry. Ilaria’s knee bumped Mama’s under the table. They wanted to interrupt at once with something large and bright, but Mama had already started, so Ilaria folded their hands and waited while the room held its calm hush.

Mama began to tell Ilaria about how the chorus decides the key. “First we listen to the pitch pipe,” Mama said, “and then we all hum that same low note until it sits warm in our chests.” Ilaria nodded and wrapped cool fingers around an untouched glass of milk. Soft curtain light drifted across the blue bowl. The bigger news still pressed, yet Ilaria watched Mama’s mouth shape each careful word and did not speak. Loose spoons lay near the jam jar, silver and quiet, waiting for later use.

“After the hum, the leader lifts one hand,” Mama continued, “and we match the note she chooses for that morning’s song.” Ilaria’s shoulders stayed relaxed. From the hall came the single bright sound of a cat’s collar bell as the cat wandered past and settled out of sight. The tinkle faded into the floorboards’ coolness under Ilaria’s socks. They counted the notes with their eyes instead of blurting. Mama’s voice moved like a slow stream, and Ilaria stayed beside it even while their own news glowed under their ribs.

Mama explained that high voices sometimes need a higher key so the notes sit comfortably, and low voices sometimes need gentle room to rumble. Ilaria rested their chin in their hands. A patch of tablecloth had warmed in the light until it felt like a small sunny pond under their elbow. They longed to share first, yet every sentence about matching breath and matching tone kept them listening. “We decide together by trying two keys and feeling which one lets everyone smile,” Mama said. Ilaria turned one spoon over; the metal stayed cool and smooth against their thumb.

The telling grew longer in the kindest way. Mama described how someone might softly sing a line in one key and then another while the whole chorus listens with closed eyes to hear which version floats easiest. Ilaria closed their own eyes a moment and pictured that soft trying. When they opened them, the kitchen carried the clean smell of soapy wool from Mama’s sweater as she leaned forward to turn the page. The mild scent stayed only a breath near Ilaria’s nose. They almost spoke then, but Mama’s last ideas were still arriving, so Ilaria waited as patiently as a held note.

“We choose the key that feels like home for every voice,” Mama finished, her final sentence landing soft as a folded napkin. Ilaria heard those last words clearly and did not rush past them. Steam still whispered from the kettle spout in a tiny living hiss. Ilaria’s forearm rested on the table’s honest grain. They sat with the ending for a full breath, then gently lined the spoons up by size, smallest to largest, silver clicking once against silver in a tiny tidy sound that fit the quiet.

Ilaria looked up and asked one real question. “Mama, how do you know when the key is the right home?” they said. Mama smiled and answered that the room itself seems to grow warmer when the notes fit, and faces relax. Cool air from under the door brushed Ilaria’s ankles while they thought. A thin bright line of morning light ran along the blue bowl’s rim. Only after the whole telling and after their question did Ilaria share something small of their own. “I found a smooth white pebble by the steps yesterday,” they said, “and I put it in my coat pocket to show you.” Mama’s eyes brightened. The pebble news was small and true, and it settled perfectly into the morning.

Ilaria and Mama sat with the neat row of spoons shining between them while the bigger swirl of news waited for another hour. Mama covered Ilaria’s hand with her own warmer one. The lantern by the window glowed softly. A pale stripe of light lay across the open chorus booklet, and Ilaria listened to the hush of the house waking, content to stay close in the chairs at the kitchen table while breakfast waited just one more quiet minute.
