The quiet tick of a tin clock sounded from the wooden shelf, and Farah noticed each soft click while the last gold light rested on the floorboards. Farah stood very still for a moment, listening, then brushed a hand along the warm sleeve of their coat. Nana Ruth waited nearby with two lanterns, and on the table sat Farah’s first row of knitting, neat and small, the yarn the color of early leaves. Farah had worked those stitches earlier and felt both proud and shy about them at the same time. The room smelled faintly of soap and wool. Farah lifted the knitting carefully and slipped it into the little bag Nana held open. Under the edge of a biscuit tin, Farah’s fingers brushed a tin lid that felt cool and faintly ridged, and then Farah closed the bag with a gentle push.

Outside, the evening lane stretched ahead like a soft ribbon of dust and grass, and the air touched Farah’s face with a mild coolness that made them tug the scarf a little higher. Nana Ruth lit both lanterns, and the yellow glow bloomed inside the glass, throwing tiny circles onto Farah’s shoes. Farah walked close beside Nana Ruth, the bag bouncing lightly against one hip. Other lanterns bobbed farther down the lane, but Farah kept eyes mostly on the path and on the warm light swinging between them. A moth fluttered near the glass and drifted away again. Farah thought of the first row of knitting hidden in the bag and felt the old shy feeling rise like a small bubble. Nana Ruth did not ask to see anything yet. She only matched her steps to Farah’s and let the lantern light paint the weeds gold.

They passed the low stone wall where chamomile grew in a quiet clump, and the sweet mild smell of chamomile rose on the evening air as Farah breathed in. Farah slowed without meaning to, toes pressing into the soft packed earth of the lane. Nana Ruth slowed too, lantern held steady so the light stayed kind on Farah’s hands. Farah could hear distant laughter from other walkers, soft as leaves, and the faint creak of a garden gate somewhere beyond the hedge. The bag felt heavier with the knitting inside, though Farah knew it was light. Farah swallowed and looked at the glow sliding over Nana Ruth’s kind face. Nothing hurried them. The lane held space for small steps and slow thoughts, and Farah used that space to gather a brave feeling the size of a single stitch.

Nana Ruth paused near a wooden bench that still held the day’s leftover warmth in its worn seat. She set one lantern on the bench so its light made a calm pool, and Farah sat carefully, bag on their lap. The wood felt smooth under Farah’s palms. Crickets began a low gentle song in the tall grass, and the sound wrapped around the two of them like a quiet blanket. Farah opened the bag just enough to see the edge of the knitting and then closed it again. Nana Ruth watched the lane lights move far ahead and did not reach for the yarn. “We can sit as long as you like, Farah,” Nana Ruth said, voice low and even. Farah nodded, cheeks warm from the lantern glow and from the shy feeling that still fluttered.

Farah took a slow breath of the cool evening and decided the smallest brave thing might be enough. Farah opened the bag once more and drew out the first row of knitting so the lantern light could touch the loops. The yarn looked plain and honest in the yellow shine. Farah’s fingers smoothed one edge, feeling the tiny bumps of each stitch. Nana Ruth turned slightly, still not crowding, only offering the quiet of her attention. Farah’s heart beat a little faster, yet the lane stayed peaceful and the lanterns stayed steady. Farah cleared a throat that felt tight and spoke in a voice meant to be heard, not whispered away.

“Nana Ruth, I made this first row today,” Farah said, holding the knitting where the light could find it. The words came out clear enough to ride the mild air. Nana Ruth smiled the way sunlight finds a window, soft and sure. “It looks careful and true, Farah,” Nana Ruth answered. Farah felt the shy bubble settle into something warmer. “I was nervous to show you,” Farah added, still using a voice that carried. Nana Ruth nodded once, lantern light painting silver along her hair. “Thank you for letting me see it,” she said. “We can look together, or we can just keep it safe in the bag. You choose.” Farah rested the knitting across both knees and watched the glow travel along the yarn, and the choice felt easy now.

They rose after a little while and continued along the evening lane, lanterns swinging in slow arcs that lit the dusty weeds and the pale stones. Farah carried the knitting loosely in one hand for a stretch of the path, then tucked it back into the bag when a small breeze lifted. The temperature stayed gentle on Farah’s wrists and neck. Nana Ruth pointed out a cat slipping under a fence and a window lamp shining far across the fields, and Farah answered with quiet remarks about the colors and the quiet. No one asked Farah to knit more or to perform. The first row simply traveled with them like a small companion. Farah’s steps grew freer, boots scuffing softly, and the bag no longer felt heavy at all.

Near the turn that led home, Farah and Nana Ruth stood still while the other lanterns drifted past like slow fireflies. Farah leaned a shoulder lightly against Nana Ruth’s coat and smelled wool and evening air. “Can we put the knitting by the window when we get inside?” Farah asked. “Yes,” Nana Ruth said. “It will keep company with the quiet.” Farah smiled into the lantern light and felt the last of the shy feeling loosen and float off like a moth. They walked the final stretch side by side, the lane softer now under the thickening dusk, and Farah’s free hand stayed open to the mild night.

Back in the familiar room the tin clock had already marked the gentler hour, though Farah no longer needed to count its ticks. Farah set the first row of knitting on the wide sill where the yarn could rest, and Nana Ruth hung their coats and dimmed the brighter lamps. Farah sat on the rug and watched the yarn hold the last outdoor coolness before the house warmth claimed it. The evening lane still glowed a little in Farah’s memory, full of kind steps and a voice that had been heard. Nana Ruth brought a cup of warm milk that steamed faintly near Farah’s hands. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Farah breathed out, shoulders loose, and let the quiet of the room settle around the knitting and around them both like a careful finished stitch.
