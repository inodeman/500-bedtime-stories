Nana Ruth was already there in the library aisle, midway through tucking a stack of picture books back onto the low shelf, her hands moving with quiet care while a bright slice of sun lay across the wooden floor like a soft golden ribbon. Sable stepped closer and felt the cool shade on one cheek and the gentle warmth of that sunbeam on the other, and the air carried a faint cinnamon scent from the mug Nana Ruth had set nearby on a side table. The light made the polished floorboards gleam, and Sable heard the distant click of a gate latch from the garden outside the tall windows as someone closed it carefully. Nana Ruth looked up with a smile and said, “Hello, Sable, come see how the sun makes everything glow in here.” Sable nodded and stood very still, watching the dust float slowly through the bright band of light, feeling the smooth cool wood of the shelf edge under their fingertips.

Sable wandered a few steps farther down the aisle and noticed something small resting on the carpet where the sunbeam touched it, a short yellow pencil with a broken glass-bead prism stuck near the eraser end. The little prism threw tiny dancing specks of color across the floor and up the lower books, red and green and blue spots that shifted whenever a breeze from the open window stirred the air. Sable crouched down carefully so their knees pressed into the warm sunlit carpet, and they could see how one edge of the glass bead had cracked yet still caught the light beautifully. The pencil lay quiet and ordinary beside a forgotten bookmark, and Sable wondered how it had ended up there, perhaps left after someone had used it to mark a page about rainbows. The temperature near the floor felt warmer than the rest of the aisle, and Sable stayed low, breathing softly so they would not disturb the bright little show.

Sable felt a real wish rise up inside them to pick up the pencil and pocket the broken prism or at least poke the glass bead gently to make the colors spin faster. The colors looked so bright and close that Sable’s fingers twitched once in the warm light, yet they kept their hands folded in their lap instead. Just then Chioma came around the corner of the shelf, her soft sneakers making almost no sound on the carpet, and Sable looked up at their friend. “Chioma, come kneel here with me,” Sable whispered, “but we have to look without touching.” Chioma knelt too, her shoulder brushing Sable’s, and together they watched the speckles slide slowly across a picture of a blue whale on a nearby book spine. The air stayed still and mild, and Sable felt glad that the wish to take the prism stayed quiet inside them.

Sable pointed with one careful finger held far above the carpet and said, “See how the broken edge still makes the colors? If we leave it right there, the sun keeps doing the work.” Chioma leaned in closer without reaching and answered, “It looks like little stars on the floor, Sable.” The two of them stayed side by side in the warm slice of light, noticing how a blue speck landed on Sable’s shoe and a yellow one rested on Chioma’s sleeve for a moment before drifting away. Sable could hear their own calm breathing and Chioma’s soft one, and the library felt hushed and safe around them. The texture of the carpet under their knees was familiar and slightly fuzzy, and Sable smiled because the colors kept moving without anyone needing to help.

Nana Ruth finished with the last book and walked over, her steps light on the boards, and she crouched down so she could see what held Sable and Chioma so still. “What have you found, Sable?” Nana Ruth asked in her warm low voice. Sable answered, “A pencil with a broken glass bead that throws colors, and we are looking without moving it.” Nana Ruth’s eyes followed the dancing speckles across the lower shelf, and she nodded slowly. She reached into her tote bag and drew out a small walnut-smooth spoon she had brought from home for stirring her tea later, turning it once in the light so Sable could feel how silky the wood sat against their palm when she offered it for a moment. Sable held the spoon only briefly, feeling that smooth curve, then gave it back, and all three of them watched the prism keep throwing its quiet lights.

The cinnamon smell drifted a little stronger when Nana Ruth shifted her mug closer on the side table, and Sable breathed it in while the colors continued their slow dance. Chioma whispered, “I like that it stays right where the sun can find it.” Sable nodded and hummed four soft notes under their breath, then left a small rest of silence so the library quiet could settle again around the three of them. Nana Ruth rested one hand lightly on Sable’s shoulder, and the fabric of her cardigan felt soft and familiar. The sunbeam moved just a little as time passed, sliding the colored speckles higher up the book spines, and Sable felt peaceful knowing the pencil and its broken prism would stay exactly where they had found it.

Sable glanced once more at the bright spots traveling across a book about birds and said quietly, “We can come back tomorrow and see if the sun finds it again.” Chioma smiled and stood up carefully so her shadow would not cover the prism. Nana Ruth rose too, brushing a bit of carpet fluff from her skirt, and the three of them stepped back together into the cooler shade of the aisle. The wooden shelves held their steady smell of paper, and Sable could still feel the earlier warmth of the sun on their arms even as they moved away. Everything stayed calm and ordinary and bright at the same time, the broken glass bead continuing its gentle work without needing any help from their hands.

They walked slowly toward the end of the aisle where a reading chair waited near a low window, and Sable kept thinking about the way the colors had looked like tiny moving jewels. Nana Ruth set her mug down on the wide sill and the cinnamon scent rose once more in the mild air. Chioma settled into the chair first, and Sable sat on the thick rug beside it, legs crossed, still feeling the memory of the warm carpet from earlier. Outside the window the garden lay quiet after the earlier gate latch sound, and the afternoon light softened. Sable leaned their head lightly against Nana Ruth’s knee and watched the last of the sun’s slice fade from the far end of the aisle where the pencil still rested.

Sable closed their eyes for a moment and listened to the soft rustle of a page as Chioma opened a book, then opened them again to the gentle room. Nana Ruth stroked Sable’s hair once with her cool fingers, and the three of them stayed near one another without hurry. The library air felt just right, neither too warm nor too cool, and Sable knew the broken glass-bead prism on its pencil would keep throwing its quiet speckles for anyone who looked carefully later. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
