The smell of warm bread curled through the bakery as Nim’s small hands already pressed and turned a pale ball of dough on the long counter that faced the street. Afternoon light slid across the wooden boards and warmed the backs of Nim’s wrists while flour drifted like soft snow onto their sleeves. Outside the wide window, shoppers drifted by with baskets, and inside the air felt gentle and golden. Nim patted the dough again, listening to the quiet thud of their own palms, and watched the ferryman at the other end of the counter tip his weather-softened hat in a friendly way. The ferryman had come up from the landing after the last boat and now waited without hurry for his bag of rolls. Nim kept their fingers moving so the shy feeling stayed small and manageable.

Yelena stood a little behind Nim, her hands folded, her eyes bright but quiet as she watched the baker set out a row of shiny fruit tarts. The cooler air near the open side door brushed Nim’s ankles, and they could see that Yelena wanted a turn to choose one of the special rolls shaped like tiny boats. Two taller children had already stepped forward twice, and the baker was beginning to turn toward the next customer. Nim’s chest felt tight and warm at the same time. They glanced at the ferryman, who simply rested his elbows on the counter and looked kind, not pushy at all. The light from the street made soft rectangles on the floor, and Nim noticed how Yelena’s shoes stayed neatly together while she waited.

Nim remembered how good it felt when someone noticed them, and the idea of speaking rose like a small bubble. The counter top felt smooth under Nim’s floury fingertips, and they wiped their hands slowly on a cloth. “I think Yelena hasn’t had a turn yet,” Nim said, the words coming out clear enough to be heard though not loud. The baker paused with a tray in both hands. Yelena’s shoulders lifted a little. The ferryman gave the smallest nod, the kind that said he had heard and was glad, and he did not ask Nim to say it again or make a bigger speech. Warmth from the ovens brushed Nim’s cheeks as they stood still.

The baker smiled and stepped sideways so the boat-shaped rolls were easier to see. “Of course,” the baker said. “Yelena, would you like to choose one?” Yelena stepped forward carefully and pointed to a roll with a little currant eye. “That one, please,” Yelena whispered, and then a bit louder, “Thank you.” Nim felt the shy bubble settle into something steadier. The light through the window turned the sugar crystals on the tarts into tiny stars. The ferryman shifted his weight and said, “A fair turn makes the whole counter happier.” His voice was low and even, like water against a quiet dock.

Nim’s hands found the empty mixing bowl they had been using earlier. The bowl still held a dusting of flour, and Nim decided the quiet action of carrying it would help the brave feeling stay calm. They lifted the bowl and walked the few steps to the sink at the back, feeling the cooler kitchen air against their arms, then set the bowl down gently and came back to the counter that faced the street. Yelena was already holding her boat roll wrapped in soft paper. “You spoke for me,” Yelena said, and Nim answered, “I wanted you to have a turn too.” The two of them stood side by side while the baker finished packing the ferryman’s order.

Outside, the afternoon had grown softer, and through the open side door Nim heard the wind in the fig leaves, a dry gentle rustle that mixed with the far-off call of a gull. The sound made the bakery feel connected to the trees and the path that led down toward the water. The ferryman accepted his warm bag with both hands and thanked the baker. He looked at Nim and Yelena once more and said, “Small brave words travel well.” Then he stepped out into the street light without making any fuss. Nim’s shoulders relaxed. The counter smelled faintly of citrus zest now that the tarts had been moved, and the wood under Nim’s palms still held a little of the oven’s leftover heat.

Yelena offered Nim a tiny piece of the boat roll to share, and Nim took it carefully. The bread was soft and faintly sweet. They chewed slowly while watching the street grow quieter. A few more customers came and went, their footsteps light on the boards. Nim rested one hand against a cool ceramic rim of a tall milk jug that stood near the register, feeling how smooth and steady it was after the warm dough work. The temperature of the glaze calmed their fingers. “I’m glad we stayed,” Nim said. Yelena nodded and brushed a crumb from her sleeve. The baker wiped the counter in long peaceful strokes, and the light from the window turned the color of pale honey.

As the last tray was put away, Nim helped gather the scattered flour cloths into a neat stack. The bakery grew quieter still, and the air cooled a degree as the ovens rested. Nim and Yelena sat for a moment on the low bench by the window, swinging their feet gently, feeling the solid wood under them. The ferryman’s kindness stayed in Nim’s mind like a steady lantern of its own, and the memory of speaking up felt simple rather than heavy. Outside, the street lamps began to flicker on one by one. Nim breathed in the fading bakery warmth and let their hands rest open on their knees.

Later, when Nim had washed the last of the flour from their wrists and walked the short way home with the soft evening air on their face, they sat by their own window and thought of the counter and the boat rolls and Yelena’s quiet smile. The room smelled faintly of clean soap and the leftover sweetness of shared bread. Nim pulled a light blanket across their lap and listened to the house settle. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Nim closed their eyes, feeling safe and proud in the smallest way, and drifted toward sleep while the night stayed gentle all around.
