The clean smell of lemon soap rose from Bodhi’s busy hands as they wiped the low table in slow circles before the library story circle began. Cool dampness clung to Bodhi’s fingers while warm afternoon light spilled across the polished wood and made little gold patches dance. Bodhi pressed the cloth firmly and listened to the quiet shuffle of cushions being set in a ring. Auntie June moved nearby with soft steps, laying out picture books, and Bodhi felt glad their hands had work to do. The air in the room stayed mild and still except for a faint draft that carried the distant hush of leaves. Bodhi kept circling the cloth, smoothing every sticky spot from the morning snack time, and felt the table grow pleasantly dry under their palms.

Bodhi settled onto a round blue cushion as the other children arrived, and the soft fabric hugged their knees with a gentle give. Sunlight through the high windows painted slow-moving bars across the carpet, warm on Bodhi’s bare ankles. Auntie June sat cross-legged at the edge of the circle and smiled at each face in turn. Bodhi’s friend Nim waved from the opposite cushion, and Bodhi waved back with a small lift of fingers. A basket of muffins rested near Auntie June, still covered by a floured towel whose powdery weave looked almost like fresh snow. Bodhi tucked their feet closer and breathed the quiet of the waiting room, ready yet a little fluttery inside.

Auntie June spoke first in her calm low voice. “Today we can share one true thing that happened while we helped someone smaller,” she said, and her words floated gently without any hurry. Bodhi felt their cheeks warm under the steady glow of the reading lamp. Nim offered a story about tying a little sister’s shoe, and the circle listened with soft nods. Bodhi’s own true thing pressed at the front of their mind, the part about the soup that had still been too hot for the little cousin at home. Bodhi’s fingers traced the cushion fringe, feeling its bumpy loops, while they waited and watched dust motes drift in the light.

When the turn came near, Bodhi’s throat felt tight and dry like paper left in sun. Auntie June glanced over and simply waited, her eyes kind and unhurried. “You can speak as softly as you like, Bodhi,” Auntie June said. “We will hear you.” Bodhi swallowed and smelled the faint leftover sweetness of the muffins drifting from the basket. Outside the open window the wind in the fig leaves whispered a long slow hush that seemed to steady the room. Bodhi straightened a little on the cushion and felt the carpet’s rough weave under one bare heel.

Bodhi drew a careful breath and let the smallest brave words come out clear enough to reach the circle. “The soup was still too hot for my little cousin,” Bodhi said. “So I blew on the spoon and we waited together until the steam stopped rising so fast.” The sentence hung in the mild air, and Bodhi felt their shoulders loosen as soon as it was spoken. Nim smiled across the ring, and another child nodded as if they understood waiting too. Auntie June’s face stayed open and warm. “That was careful and kind, Bodhi,” Auntie June said. “Thank you for telling us.”

Bodhi’s hands rested open on their knees and felt the cool air circulating from the window brush across their skin. The story circle continued with other quiet true things, each voice soft as pages turning. Bodhi listened and felt the earlier shyness melt into a steady calm that filled their chest like warm tea. Light from the tall windows shifted lower and turned the carpet a deeper gold. Bodhi watched Auntie June turn a picture book slowly so everyone could see the bright drawings of kitchens and gardens. No one asked Bodhi to say more, and that gentle space felt just right.

After the last share, Auntie June invited everyone to stretch and choose a picture book of their own. Bodhi rose and walked to the low shelf, fingers brushing the smooth spines until they found one with a blue cover. The book’s pages smelled faintly of paper and ink when Bodhi opened them, and the illustrations glowed under the lamp. Nim came to sit beside Bodhi on the cushion again, and together they turned the pages without rushing. Auntie June moved among the children, offering quiet help only when asked, never pushing anyone forward. Bodhi felt the solid weight of the book resting across their lap like a friendly hand.

As the light outside softened toward evening, Bodhi carried the floured towel back to the snack basket and folded it neatly once more. The powder left a faint dry trace on Bodhi’s fingertips that they brushed away with a slow smile. Auntie June gathered the cushions into a tidy stack while Bodhi helped by stacking the leftover muffin wrappers into a small neat pile. “You spoke your true thing so clearly today, Bodhi,” Auntie June said. “I am glad you were here.” Bodhi answered, “I am glad too,” and the simple exchange felt complete. The room held a peaceful hush broken only by the far-off hush of traffic and the turning of final pages.

Bodhi slipped their arms into a light sweater as the library grew quieter still, and the soft knit felt cozy against their wrists. Auntie June dimmed the main lights one by one until only a gentle pool remained near the reading corner. Bodhi stood by the window for a last look at the darkening fig tree and felt the cool glass under their palm. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Bodhi breathed out a long easy breath, ready for the walk home beside Auntie June, the true words already settled safely inside like a small warm stone.
