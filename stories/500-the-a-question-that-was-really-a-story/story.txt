The tin clock on the shelf beside the bed made a small steady ticking sound that filled the bedroom like a quiet heartbeat. Jorie noticed it at once while they lay under the covers, the lamp light warm on their cheeks and the air still around them. Auntie Luz had just settled into the chair with a smile, and Jorie felt the smooth cotton of the sheet against their arm as they turned a little to see her better. Jorie's toes wiggled once under the blanket, and they listened to that ticking while Auntie Luz cleared her throat gently, ready to begin something special. The clock kept time without rush, and Jorie breathed in the calm of the room, waiting for the first words.

Auntie Luz spoke softly into the warm glow of the lamp. "Tonight I want to tell you about a question that was really a story and needed both," she said, her voice low and even. Jorie's eyes opened wider in the gentle light, and they smelled a faint sweetness like orange peel coming from the little dish on the nightstand. Jorie wanted already to jump in with bigger news about the drawing they had made that afternoon, but they pressed their lips together and listened instead. The air felt pleasantly cool on Jorie's forehead as Auntie Luz continued. "It began with a small wonder spoken out loud, and then the wonder grew into a whole tale that could not stand without the question or without the story that answered it." Jorie nodded slowly, feeling the pillowcase smooth under their cheek, and kept quiet.

"The question came from a young traveler who stood by a quiet stream and asked, 'Where do the ripples go when they reach the other side?'" Auntie Luz said. Jorie listened with full attention, hearing the calm tone and feeling the soft pressure of the mattress beneath them as they shifted just a little closer. In the story the traveler followed the ripples in their mind and discovered a garden where answers grew like flowers that glowed at dusk. Jorie imagined the cool water of that stream while staying right there in the bedroom. They still wanted to share their bigger news but held it carefully inside, letting Auntie Luz's sentences roll on without a single break. The lamp light touched Jorie's hands where they rested on the blanket, making them look golden and still.

Auntie Luz described the garden next, with paths of soft moss and lanterns hanging from low branches. "The traveler found that every flower held a piece of the answer, but only if the question stayed open like a cup ready to be filled," she explained. Jorie felt a small breeze from the slightly open window brush against their neck, cool and gentle, and they kept their breathing even so they would not miss a word. The bigger news waited impatiently in Jorie's mind, yet they chose the quiet path of listening all the way. Auntie Luz's voice carried the traveler through the garden until they gathered seven glowing petals that together made a map back home. Jorie's eyelids felt pleasantly heavy in the warm room, but they stayed awake and present.

Auntie Luz paused only long enough to unfold a linen napkin from her lap and dab the corner of her mouth. Jorie noticed the fine texture of that linen napkin as it caught the lamp light in tiny threads, crisp and clean against Auntie Luz's fingers. Then the story moved forward again. "When the traveler placed the petals in a circle, the question and the story joined together and became one complete thing that glowed brighter than either alone," Auntie Luz said. Jorie ran their own fingers lightly over the blanket edge, still holding back, still taking in every part. The air in the bedroom held a steady warmth now, and Jorie's shoulders relaxed deeper into the pillow as they saved their real question for the end.

The traveler in the tale walked home under a sky that seemed friendlier, carrying the joined question-story like a lantern of their own. "And that is how a simple question became a full story that needed both the asking and the telling to be whole," Auntie Luz finished in a soft close. Jorie heard the last sentence clearly and let it settle inside them like a warm sip of milk. Only then did Jorie take a slow breath and ask one real question. "Did the traveler ever ask another question the next day?" Jorie said, their voice quiet as a whisper. Auntie Luz nodded and said that yes, the traveler asked about the wind next, and another story grew. Jorie felt the cool edge of the sheet near their chin and smiled, glad they had waited.

After Auntie Luz replied about the wind, Jorie finally shared something small of their own. "I drew a tree today with seven birds in it, and it felt nice to finish," Jorie said, keeping the news gentle rather than the bigger version they had first wanted to burst out with. Auntie Luz listened in turn, her face bright in the yellow lamp shine, and she reached to pat Jorie's hand with a light touch that felt caring. Jorie noticed how the room had grown even quieter after the story ended. They tucked one hand under the pillow and felt the familiar coolness there while Auntie Luz praised the careful birds. No rush remained in Jorie now; the listening had made space for this smaller share to feel just right.

Jorie and Auntie Luz sat together a little longer in the peaceful bedroom. "The birds in your drawing can watch over the traveler's garden in your mind if you like," Auntie Luz offered. Jorie answered, "I think they would like that very much." Then they both grew quiet again, and Jorie yawned a slow yawn that stretched their mouth and made their eyes water pleasantly. The lamp continued its steady work, pouring a pool of light that reached almost to the foot of the bed. Jorie pulled the blanket up a bit higher with both hands, feeling its weight settle like a friendly hug across their chest. Everything about the evening felt complete now that the full story had been heard and the small share given.

Jorie closed their eyes halfway and listened once more to the quiet of the room as Auntie Luz whispered good night. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Jorie felt the last bits of the day's energy slip away into the warm dark behind their lids, and they pictured the traveler and the petals and the seven birds all resting too. Auntie Luz stayed nearby until Jorie's breathing deepened into the slow pattern of near-sleep. A faint draft stirred the curtain for a moment, bringing a cooler touch across Jorie's brow before it faded. Jorie's last waking thought was of how good it had felt to hear every sentence and then add their own small piece, and soon they drifted fully into dreams colored by garden lanterns and kind voices.
