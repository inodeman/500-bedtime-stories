A tiny tick sounded from the floorboards beside the low bench. Pilar noticed it at once and turned their head toward the soft noise while the cool evening air brushed their arms. Outside the open doorway the harbor step waited, the place where parcels always changed hands, and Papa already stood there holding the tall step stool so the good plates could come down safely from the arriving boat. Golden light lay in long bars across the worn wood, and Pilar felt the quiet tick fade as they rose and walked out to join him. The boards underfoot still held a little daytime warmth. Pilar stayed close beside Papa and watched the water lap the stone edge without hurry. They liked how steady everything looked in that gentle light.

Pilar rested one hand on the rail and felt the grain of the bench where it met the step, a fine raised line that caught under their fingertips like a tiny map. Papa tried to set the step stool firmly on the uneven harbor stones so the good plates could come down safely, yet the legs wobbled whenever he shifted his hold. The stool was awkward for one person alone. Pilar saw the difficulty right away and stepped nearer, taking the useful half of the job by bracing the base with both hands while Papa climbed the first rung. No one asked them to help, and they did not wait for praise. The air carried a faint green scent from nearby tomato vines that someone had set in pots along the wall, and Pilar breathed it in while they kept the stool steady. Waves made a low rhythmic hush against the pilings.

“Thank you for holding it so still, Pilar,” Papa said in a warm voice that matched the soft light.  
“I can keep the legs even,” Pilar answered, pressing their palms flat.  
A cat’s collar bell jingled once from the shadows near the tomato pots, a clear bright note that drifted over the water and then fell quiet again. Pilar smiled at the small sound and did not let go of the stool. Papa reached up toward the open hatch of the boat where a crate of good plates waited, wrapped in soft cloth. The wood of the stool felt smooth under Pilar’s hands except where salt had left a faint roughness. They kept their feet planted and watched the crate begin its careful journey down.

Papa lowered the first plate into Pilar’s waiting hands, and they set it gently on the waiting cloth spread across the harbor step. The porcelain felt cool and smooth, still carrying a trace of the boat’s hold. Pilar stacked each piece with care so nothing touched too hard. The tomato vines gave off their green smell again when a small breeze turned, and Pilar noticed how it mixed with the clean salt air. Papa climbed down, moved the stool a hand’s width, and climbed once more. Pilar shifted with him every time, taking half the weight of the awkward stool without being asked. The evening light softened to peach and pink across the water. They worked side by side in that calm rhythm, plate after plate finding its safe place on the cloth.

“These are the ones for the long table,” Papa said while he passed another wrapped dish.  
“I will set them in the middle so they stay steady,” Pilar replied, smoothing the cloth with careful fingers.  
Oona appeared at the far end of the harbor step carrying a small empty basket, her steps light on the stones. She waited without crowding them and watched the plates settle into neat rows. Pilar glanced up and gave a quiet nod, then returned to bracing the stool. The grain of the bench nearby still remembered their earlier touch, and they felt glad the wood had been solid under their hand. Cool air drifted off the water and touched the back of their neck like a friendly hush. Papa and Pilar finished the last of the good plates together, the stool finally free to fold flat.

They carried the cloth-wrapped stack only a few steps to the sheltered bench where parcels finished changing hands for the night. Pilar helped lower the bundle onto the seat and felt again the fine grain of the bench under their palm as they steadied the edge. Oona held the basket open so Papa could slip the extra wrappings inside. The tomato vines along the wall released one more soft green breath when the breeze passed. Pilar stood back and looked at the tidy pile, pleased that everything had come down safely. No one spoke of the help they had given; the work simply felt complete. The water slapped gently at the step, and the sky deepened to lavender above the masts.

Papa folded the step stool and rested it against the rail. Pilar walked beside him toward the doorway that led back inside the small harbor room, the place with the window that looked over the water. Oona waved once and headed home along the path, her basket light now. Inside, the air felt still and a little warmer than the open step. Pilar sat on the indoor bench and listened past the near sounds of settling wood, trying instead for the far clock that marked the hour from across the harbor. Its distant note arrived soft and clear. They rested their hands in their lap and smelled the last faint trace of tomato vines that had followed them through the door. Papa set the plates on a high shelf where they would wait for morning.

“You made the stool easy to manage tonight,” Papa said while he closed the shutter halfway.  
“I liked holding it steady,” Pilar answered, voice quiet as the room.  
They both listened a moment longer for the far clock instead of any nearer creak. The light inside grew dim and friendly. Pilar leaned their shoulder against Papa for a slow breath and felt the calm of the finished work settle around them like a soft blanket. Outside, a single gull called once and then the water took back its hush. The good plates rested safe on their shelf, and the step stool stood folded in its corner, no longer awkward.

Pilar yawned and felt the cool night air slip through the partly open window, touching their hair with a gentle finger. They stayed close to Papa on the bench and watched the last color leave the sky. The room held the quiet smell of wood and the memory of tomato vines. No hurry remained. Pilar’s eyes grew heavy while the far clock gave one more distant chime they could just hear. Papa’s hand rested warm on their shoulder. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
