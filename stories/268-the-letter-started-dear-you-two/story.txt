The letter rested against the pale boards of the meadow gate, cream paper catching the honey-colored afternoon light that pooled along the fold. Its edges curled just a little, and the looping ink on the front curved in a way that might have begun with the words dear you two, though the rest stayed hidden inside the neat crease. Zola leaned so close that their breath warmed the paper, and they watched a thin shadow on the corner move the width of a thumb when a soft cloud drifted over the sun. From the low bushes beside the path rose the gentle smell of lavender, sweet and clean in the still air. Zola touched the letter once with one careful fingertip, feeling how warm and smooth it had become after sitting in the light.

Zola stood slowly beside the meadow gate where games always started when the day turned golden and the grass lay flat and inviting. Cool blades brushed their ankles through thin socks, and the air felt mild against their cheeks. Mr. Ade sat on the low wooden bench just inside the open gate, hat tilted back, hands quiet in his lap as he watched without speaking. From the porch of the small white cottage beyond the meadow came the steady soft ticking of a tin clock, a sound so regular it seemed part of the breathing of the afternoon. Maude stood just outside the gate posts, close enough to see everything, her shoes resting on the packed earth while the circle of children inside already leaned together and chose their spots.

Warm sunlight painted long gold stripes across the children’s shoulders as they laughed about the first round of the sitting game and the clapping game that would follow. Zola looked toward Maude, who remained a single step beyond the posts, near enough to the letter that her shadow almost touched its edge, yet not quite inside the ring of flattened grass. The light felt gentle on Zola’s arms. Someone called that the places looked ready, but the circle had drawn tight without meaning to, leaving Maude outside the fun by only the width of a gate. Zola rubbed the side of their shoe against the cool earth and thought about the shape of the gathering.

Mr. Ade smiled a little and said nothing yet, giving the children room to notice what needed noticing. Zola could hear bees humming low among the clover blossoms that dotted the meadow like tiny yellow buttons. They reached down and rubbed a quilt stitch on the corner of the small blanket they had brought for sitting, feeling the raised thread press softly under their thumb like a tiny path. Maude rocked once on her heels and glanced again at the letter leaning against the gate as if it belonged to a story still waiting. Zola breathed in the mild air and decided the circle did not have to stay the way it first appeared.

“Maude, will you come stand by me while we decide?” Zola asked in a voice no louder than the breeze. The words floated across the warm light between the gate posts, and Maude took one careful step closer, dry grass whispering under her shoes. Zola patted the blanket and felt its sun-warmed cloth. “I think we need a different shape for sitting today,” Zola said, looking around at the others with calm eyes. Mr. Ade watched from the bench, his quiet presence steady as the ticking that still drifted from the cottage, and he kept his hands folded so the children could solve the small puzzle themselves.

Zola gathered the corners of the blanket and pulled it wider and longer until the cloth made an easy oval instead of a tight ring, the fabric sliding with a soft shush across the grass. Cool shade from the tall gate post fell across Zola’s knees as they worked. “If we sit this way, there is a real place right here beside me that faces the meadow and the gate together,” Zola told the group, pointing to the open stretch of blanket that now waited like an invitation. The children shifted without fuss or hurry, scooting to match the new shape. The air cooled a little where the shade crossed the cloth, and Zola felt the change settle into something comfortable and true.

Maude sat down on the widened blanket, her shoulder resting near Zola’s, and the late light turned the grass around them a deeper green. The letter still leaned against the gate, its cream face bright and patient. “Dear you two could mean anyone who shares a game,” Maude whispered, almost smiling as she settled her hands in her lap. Zola nodded and felt the soft warmth of the afternoon on the back of their neck. “We can read it after the first round, if Mr. Ade says we may,” Zola answered. Mr. Ade gave one small nod that meant yes, the children were doing well on their own, and the meadow held them all without rush.

They played the slow clapping game next, matching soft palms so the sound stayed gentle, like distant rain on broad leaves. Zola kept the pace easy and even so every turn had room to breathe and no one had to hurry. Maude’s hands found the rhythm without strain, and the mild air carried the quiet claps across the open gate. When the round ended, Zola and Maude together lifted the letter from the boards; the paper made a quiet crinkle under their shared fingers. Mr. Ade rose from the bench and walked with them toward the cottage as the sky softened into the color of peach tea, the meadow gate standing peaceful behind them in the last long light.

Inside the cottage the air felt cooler than the meadow grass, and evening settled without any hurry while Zola and Maude sat on the rug with the letter open between them. Mr. Ade set out two cups of warm milk that steamed faintly in the quiet room. Zola traced one kind line on the page with a fingertip and listened to the house grow still around the soft sounds of settling. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside, the meadow gate rested in the fading glow, and Zola felt the calm of a place that had been made wide enough for everyone who wanted to belong.
