The evening air touched the ferry deck with a mild coolness that settled like a soft blanket over the boards and the rail. Anil stepped into the narrow space that held just two elbows of room and felt the gentle rock of the boat under their shoes while pale gold light from the low sun slipped across the water and warmed their hands. The faint splash of waves against the hull made a steady hush. Anil breathed in and noticed how calm everything felt, then turned a little so they could see both the open water and the small circle of people nearby. Their bag rested against one knee, and they kept one palm on the smooth rail as the ferry drifted forward without hurry.

Anil watched the rail gleam under that same soft light and spotted Haru standing just outside the little gathering, close to a folded sheet of paper that held a homework problem. The paper fluttered once in the breeze, and Anil could hear the quiet rustle it made. Haru held the edge of it carefully, shoulders a bit tucked in, while the others laughed lightly over a shared snack. The deck smelled clean from the water, and Anil shifted closer by half a step so the cool metal of the rail pressed lightly against their sleeve. They wanted Haru to feel the same easy place that everyone else already had.

Neighbor Sami sat on a low bench just inside the two elbows of room, a small tin box resting on their lap, and the warm scent of chamomile rose from the open thermos beside them. “You three can work that out however you like,” Sami said in a low voice that matched the gentle roll of the ferry. Anil nodded and felt the mild heat from the thermos reach their wrist when they leaned near. The light had turned a deeper honey color now, painting the rail and the water in long soft strokes. Anil looked at Haru again and decided the seating needed a change so no one stayed at the edge.

Anil moved their own bag and the empty spot on the bench, then patted the wooden seat that now faced the rail and the open view. “Haru, come sit right here with us,” Anil said, keeping their tone even and bright. The boards under Anil’s shoes felt steady and slightly warm from the earlier sun. Haru stepped in, and the homework paper came with them, no longer alone against the rail. Anil made sure the space held all three comfortably, elbows free, and the ferry’s slow motion rocked them together like a quiet cradle. The air stayed mild, and Anil smiled when Haru’s shoulder brushed theirs in the new place.

They spread the homework problem across their knees so the numbers and words sat clear in the fading light. Anil pointed to the first line and began to read it aloud in a calm voice, pausing so Haru could hear every part. “It says we can add the pictures first and then count the stars,” Anil read, and Haru answered softly, “That feels easier when we say it out loud.” The paper felt smooth under their shared fingers. Neighbor Sami only watched and let the children keep going at their own pace, never rushing the next word. The deck stayed peaceful, and Anil felt glad the rules of the little circle had stretched wide enough for everyone.

A tin clock somewhere near the cabin door ticked a slow and friendly beat that mixed with the water sounds. Anil matched their reading speed to that gentle tick so no one felt hurried. They turned the paper a little more toward Haru and invited them to read the next sentence together. The light along the rail had grown softer still, almost silver at the edges. Anil’s voice stayed low and steady while they and Haru took turns, and the homework problem that had seemed stiff before now opened like a simple path they walked side by side. Neighbor Sami smiled without speaking and let the solving stay entirely with the children.

Anil reached for the tin lid of the small box that held extra pencils and felt its cool smooth surface under their fingertips before lifting it carefully. They handed one pencil to Haru and kept another, then they both wrote the answers right beside the words they had read. The ferry swayed once more, and the motion felt kind. “We did the hard part by saying it first,” Anil said, and Haru nodded with a quiet laugh that sounded like the soft waves. The temperature of the air stayed mild against their cheeks. Neighbor Sami poured a little more warm drink but stayed back, trusting the children to finish the page in their own time.

When the last number sat neatly on the paper, Anil and Haru folded it together and slipped it into the bag. Anil found two clean napkins in the bottom and smoothed them flat on the bench as a small quiet action, side by side, so everything felt finished and neat. The rail caught one last strip of evening light. “I like reading things with you,” Haru said, and Anil answered, “Me too, it makes the numbers friendly.” The deck held them safely in the two elbows of room, and Neighbor Sami finally spoke again only to say the ferry would reach the dock soon. The whole scene stayed calm and close.

Anil leaned once more against the rail and felt the mild night air settle around their shoulders while the boat slowed. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Haru sat solidly in the place Anil had made, the homework put away, and the three of them watched the water turn deep blue under the quiet sky. Anil kept their hand near Haru’s on the smooth wood, and the gentle temperature of the evening wrapped them all without a single sharp edge. The ferry deck with its rail and careful room had become the easiest spot in the world for sitting together until the boards bumped the landing and the journey ended in peace.
