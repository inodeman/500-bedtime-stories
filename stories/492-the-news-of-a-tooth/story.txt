The first careful word of the news of a loose tooth left the ferryman’s mouth slowly, as if he were setting a small bright coin on a velvet cloth. Uma watched the shape of that word form in the cool blue light that came through the tall library glass, and they felt the thick window-seat cushion press gently against their legs. The ferryman sat with his river coat folded beside him, and the air near Uma’s cheek carried a faint warm scent of basil from the pot on the sill where the librarian kept her plants. Uma’s fingers rested on the wood, and they wanted already to speak, yet the careful beginning held them still.

Outside the glass the river moved in slow silver lines, and inside the library the shelves stood quiet as sleeping trees. The ferryman cleared his throat with a soft sound and turned a little toward Uma so that the late afternoon light touched the gray threads in his hair. “I have come with news that must be told the proper way,” he said, and his voice was low and steady like water under a bridge. Uma’s shoulders lifted with the wish to share something larger, something that felt important and bright inside their chest, but they pressed their lips together and kept listening while the cushion beneath them stayed warm from the sun.

The ferryman placed both hands flat on his knees and began the ceremony by naming the tooth as if it were a small traveler. He spoke of how the tooth had wiggled once in the morning and again at noon, and how it had sent a quiet message along the river path all the way to his ferry. Uma smelled the basil again when a draft from the open pane stirred the leaves, and they shifted so their socked feet brushed the polished wood floor. The wish to interrupt rose like a bubble, yet Uma stayed quiet and watched the ferryman’s calm face while dust motes drifted in the golden light.

“In the old way we honor a loose tooth with three soft claps of the hands and one long look at the sky,” the ferryman said, and he clapped three times so gently that the sound barely rose above the turning of a page somewhere deeper in the stacks. Uma heard the soft clap and felt the cool glass of the window against their shoulder when they leaned nearer. The tin clock on the far reading table ticked once, twice, a thin bright sound that marked each careful second of the telling. Uma’s bigger news pressed at their tongue, but they swallowed it and kept their eyes on the ferryman’s hands.

He continued the ceremony by describing the small white edge of the tooth and the way it had begun its journey toward the pillow and the morning surprise. “The tooth belongs to a child who rides my ferry on sunny days,” he said, “and the news came wrapped in river mist and kindness.” Uma felt the fabric of the seat cushion, slightly rough under their palms, and they noticed how the light had turned softer and more honey-colored across the carpet. Still they did not speak, though the bigger news waited like a bird wanting to fly from their mouth.

The ferryman’s voice moved on through the middle of the story, telling how the child had laughed and shown the wiggle with great pride, and how the ferry ropes had creaked in answer as if the whole river agreed. Uma held a polished pebble that the ferryman had earlier set between them as part of the quiet ritual; the pebble felt smooth and cool and heavy in just the right way against their thumb. They turned it once, twice, and the bigger news nearly spilled out, yet Uma remembered the last sentence was still coming and kept their breath steady while the library air stayed mild and still.

At last the ferryman reached the closing words of the ceremony. He spoke them with the same slow care he used when tying a boat to the dock. “And so the loose tooth is honored, and the child may sleep knowing the river has heard,” he finished, and the final sentence settled into the room like a folded blanket. Uma sat very straight on the window seat, feeling the last warmth of the day on their face through the glass, and only then did they open their mouth for the one real question they had saved.

“Did the child feel proud or a little surprised when the tooth first moved?” Uma asked, and their voice was soft so it would not startle the quiet shelves. The ferryman smiled and answered that the child had felt both at once, like sunlight and shade together on the water. Uma nodded, turning the cool pebble once more in their hand, and the bigger news they had wanted to shout now seemed less urgent beside the finished ceremony. They waited one more heartbeat, listening to the gentle hush of the library, before they offered something small of their own.

Uma said, “I found a leaf today that looked like a tiny boat,” and they described the leaf with quiet joy while the ferryman listened in return. The air near the window grew cooler as evening settled, and Uma leaned back into the cushion that still held the day’s gentle heat. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Uma’s eyelids felt pleasantly heavy, and the polished pebble rested warm now from their palm as the last light slipped away across the quiet river and the waiting books.
