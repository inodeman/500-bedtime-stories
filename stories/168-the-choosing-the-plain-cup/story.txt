The plain cup rested on the low wooden table beside the library story circle, its smooth white sides free of paint while the fancy cup next to it wore tiny roses and a thin gold rim that caught every bit of lamp light. Zola leaned near enough to see a faint fingerprint smudge on the plain handle and the way the fancy one sparkled as if it wanted every eye in the room. Warm air rose from the floor vents and brushed Zola’s wrists. A small cloth bag nearby released a gentle lavender smell that mixed with the dry paper scent of open books. Zola kept their gaze on the two cups and felt the quiet pull of the simpler one.

Auntie June patted the thick story rug and invited Zola closer with a calm smile. The carpet fibers felt soft and springy under Zola’s bare feet after they slipped off their shoes. Maude sat across the circle holding a picture book with bright animals on the cover and waited without rushing anyone. High windows let in the last pale gold of late afternoon, and dust motes floated like slow sparks. Zola settled cross-legged and listened to the soft rustle of pages turning somewhere behind the shelves.

Zola’s cheeks felt warm when they thought about the cups waiting for snack time after the story. Everyone would choose, and Zola already knew the fancy cup looked like the one other children might reach for first. The plain cup seemed quieter, easier to hold, and somehow more like home. Auntie June’s voice stayed low and steady as she opened the big storybook. Zola rubbed one thumb along the edge of their sleeve and watched the shadow of a chair leg creep the width of a thumb across the rug while the tale began.

The story told of a fox who found a soft nest of leaves and decided it was enough. Auntie June turned each page with care so the paper made only the lightest whisper. Zola leaned against Auntie June’s side and felt the steady rise and fall of her breathing. Maude whispered once that the fox looked happy, and Zola nodded. The room held a peaceful hush broken only by the distant tin clock that ticked a clear metallic note from the librarian’s desk. Zola’s shoulders dropped a little as the fox curled up under a painted moon.

When the last page closed, Auntie June set the book aside and nodded toward the table. “Snack cups are ready,” she said in her ordinary kind voice. “You may choose the one that feels right.” Maude stood and stretched. Zola stayed seated a moment longer, fingers tracing a single raised quilt stitch on the corner of the story blanket that had slipped near their knee. The stitch felt tiny and firm, like a secret knot holding cloth together. Zola breathed in and decided the smallest brave step would be enough.

Zola rose and walked the short path to the table while the other children murmured over the books. The plain cup sat exactly where it had been, cool white under the lamps. Zola lifted it with both hands so it would not clink. “I would like the plain cup, please,” Zola said in a voice clear enough for Auntie June to hear without any shout. Auntie June’s eyes crinkled. “That is a fine choice,” she answered. “It fits your hands just right.” Maude smiled and chose a cup of her own without looking twice.

Auntie June poured cool apple juice into Zola’s plain cup until the liquid reached halfway up the white side. The juice smelled sweet and clean. Zola held the cup carefully and felt the smooth ceramic against their palms. No one asked why they had passed over the fancy one. Maude sat beside them again and said, “Mine has a little chip, but it still works.” Zola took a small sip and tasted the quiet happiness of having spoken the true wish out loud. The library lights stayed gentle, and the rug still held the day’s warmth.

Auntie June gathered the empty cups later and stacked them without fuss. Zola helped fold the story blanket so the quilt stitch disappeared into soft layers. Outside the tall windows the sky turned the soft blue of early evening. “You spoke kindly for yourself today,” Auntie June said while they walked toward the coat hooks. Zola answered, “It felt better than pretending.” Auntie June squeezed their shoulder once and handed over the jacket that smelled faintly of home soap. Maude waved goodbye from the doorway and promised another story day soon.

On the short ride home Zola watched street lamps bloom one by one while Auntie June hummed an old tune. At the apartment the rooms felt familiar and safe. Zola placed the plain cup memory carefully inside like a smooth stone kept in a pocket. They washed hands in warm water and pulled on soft pajamas that brushed gently against their skin. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Zola climbed into bed, listened to the house settle, and closed their eyes while the last of the lavender air seemed to linger from the library visit, carrying them toward easy sleep.
