A faint drip sounded from the kitchen just beyond the doorway, soft as a whisper on the quiet evening air. Tala stood in the cottage hallway of hooks, rugs, and boots and noticed the tiny plink right away, counting each one in their mind while the warm lamp light painted gentle stripes across the floorboards. The air felt pleasantly cool on Tala’s arms as they shifted from one foot to the other on the thick woven rug. Hooks held scarves and coats that brushed Tala’s shoulder when they turned, and pairs of boots waited in a tidy row by the wall. Tala thought about the promise about the dishes that had not been kept yet and felt it sit heavy in their chest like a small stone. For a moment Tala almost stepped behind the tallest pair of boots to hide the whole matter away.

Tala’s socks pressed into the soft nap of the hallway rug while the far-off drip continued its slow rhythm. The baker stepped into the hallway then, smelling faintly of flour and carrying a clean basket, and the cool evening air drifted in with them through the open door. Tala’s hand rested on the smooth wooden edge of a boot rack as they looked up. “I need to tell you something important,” Tala said in a clear voice that filled the narrow space. The baker set the basket down gently on the rug and waited without rushing, the lamp light glowing steady on both their faces. Tala took a slow breath and felt the temperature of the hallway settle around them like a quiet blanket.

The baker’s kind eyes met Tala’s while a linen napkin peeked from the edge of the basket, its crisp woven surface cool and slightly bumpy under Tala’s curious fingertips when they touched it once. “I promised this morning that I would wash all the baking dishes before supper,” Tala said, the words coming out plain and true. “I have not washed them yet, and I almost hid that promise behind the boots instead of saying it.” The baker nodded once and rested a warm hand on Tala’s shoulder so the hallway felt even safer. Outside the small window the last daylight faded to soft blue, and Tala heard their own quiet breathing mix with the distant drip.

Together they walked the few steps from the hallway into the kitchen where the sink waited, and the air there carried the bright smell of orange peel left from the afternoon’s zesting. Tala rolled up their sleeves while the baker filled the basin with gentle warm water that sent soft steam curling upward in the lamplight. “We can mend this together right now,” the baker said in a calm low tone. Tala reached for the first bowl and felt the smooth ceramic under the soapy water, glad the promise about the dishes that had not been kept yet was finally being kept side by side. No teasing came, only the easy clink of plates and the shared work that made the kitchen feel snug and bright.

Tala washed while the baker rinsed, and the steam rose in little clouds that brushed warm against Tala’s cheeks. “Thank you for telling me the plain truth,” the baker said as they passed a clean plate to the draining board. Tala smiled and answered, “It feels lighter now that the words are out.” The hallway behind them still held its hooks and rugs and boots in peaceful order, and Tala could picture them waiting like quiet friends. Between rinses Tala counted twelve drips from the tap without peeking, just letting the soft plinks mark the time until the sink stood empty and shining. The orange-sweet air mixed with the clean soap scent and made everything feel settled.

When the last spoon rested dry on the towel, Tala and the baker returned to the cottage hallway of hooks, rugs, and boots where the lamp still cast its golden pool. Tala hung their damp apron on an empty hook and felt the sturdy fabric settle into place. The baker wiped a droplet from the boot rack with a corner of cloth, and the wood gleamed under the light. “The dishes are done and the promise is kept,” Tala said softly, almost to the friendly row of boots. The baker’s quiet chuckle held only kindness, and the cool floorboards under Tala’s feet seemed to agree that everything had been mended just right.

They stood a moment longer in the hallway while evening deepened outside. Tala brushed a hand along the soft edge of a hanging coat and listened to the house settle around them. The baker offered a small square of bread still warm from earlier baking, and Tala accepted it with both hands. Far down the hall a tin clock gave one clear gentle tick that marked the passing minute without hurry. Tala leaned against the wall beside the boots and felt the solid plaster cool through their shirt, peaceful now that the tangled promise had been spoken aloud and fixed with care.

Tala followed the baker back toward the main room for a final glass of milk, the hallway rugs muffling their steps into soft thuds. The milk tasted creamy and cool, and Tala held the cup with both palms to feel its smooth sides. “Tomorrow I will remember my promises sooner,” Tala said, and the baker answered only, “I know you will.” No extra words were needed. They returned once more to the hallway so Tala could straighten one pair of boots that had shifted, the leather cool and firm under careful fingers. The whole cottage seemed to breathe slower, ready for night.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Tala watched the warm circle of light rest on the rugs and the waiting boots while the baker dimmed the brighter lamp. The hallway of hooks held every coat in its place, and Tala’s own shoulders felt loose and easy after the honest words and the shared washing. They sat together on the low bench by the boots for one quiet minute, listening to the house creak gently as it always did at bedtime. Tala’s eyelids grew heavy in the gentle glow, and the baker’s steady presence nearby made the promise about the dishes feel completely finished and kind.
