A tiny chime drifted through the warm air of the ceramic studio, light as a breath against the quiet walls. Pema turned their head and listened, the soft light from the overhead lamp painting pale gold across the long wooden table. The clay tools lay still in neat rows, and the air felt gently heated from the afternoon sun that slipped through the high window. Pema stood close to Nana Ruth and watched a pale dust mote float and settle. Their bare feet pressed cool against the smooth floorboards while the faint chime faded into the hush. Pema smelled the clean damp of wet clay and felt curious about the covered shape waiting near the middle of the table.

On the ceramic studio table where slip and handles must set, a small cloth tented over Pema’s pinch pot that still wore its coat of wet clay slip. Nana Ruth rested one hand on the table edge and spoke in a low voice that matched the room. “The slip needs time to firm,” Nana Ruth said, “and the little handles want the same quiet wait.” Pema nodded and looked at the white cloth, noticing how the light made a soft shadow beneath it. The tabletop felt cool under Pema’s fingertips when they rested them carefully a short distance from the covered pot. A gentle warmth rose from a nearby mug, and far off in the house the comforting smell of tomato soup drifted in, rich and familiar, promising supper later. Pema breathed it in and stayed still.

Pema wanted very much to lift the cloth and check on the pinch pot still wearing wet clay slip. Their fingers hovered for a moment above the edge of the covering. “May I peek just once, Nana Ruth?” Pema asked, voice soft as the clay dust. Nana Ruth smiled and shook her head the smallest bit. “Not yet, dear one,” Nana Ruth answered. “It is still soft and needs to set without being touched.” Pema lowered their hand and felt the cool air move across their knuckles. The studio light glowed steady and kind. Outside the window a leaf brushed glass with a dry whisper. Pema stayed close to the table and watched the cloth instead of lifting it.

Ulla, who had come to visit and help wash the boards, stood nearby with a clean towel folded over one arm. “We can wait together,” Ulla said gently. Pema looked at Ulla and then back at the covered pot. The decision settled in Pema’s chest like a warm stone. They would leave the pinch pot covered and choose a quiet task until it finished in its own time. Nana Ruth handed Pema a soft cloth that had wrinkled a little during the afternoon. Pema took it and felt the gentle give of fabric against their palms. The room held a peaceful temperature, neither too warm nor cool, and the wooden table smelled faintly of old clay and water.

Pema smoothed one wrinkle out of the cloth with slow, careful strokes, pressing the fabric flat until it lay neat again. The motion felt calm and good under their hands. A faint metallic note sounded once more when the house cat walked past the doorway, and Pema recognized the sweet ring of a cat’s collar bell before the animal padded away into another room. Nana Ruth watched with quiet approval. “That is a fine quiet job,” Nana Ruth said. Pema kept smoothing until every crease had softened. The light from the window shifted a degree lower, turning the clay tools a softer gray. Pema felt the steady coolness of the table leg against their knee as they worked.

After the cloth lay smooth, Pema noticed a basket sitting on the far end of the table, the kind Nana Ruth used for carrying finished pieces. Pema reached for it and closed their fingers around a wicker handle, feeling the woven ridges press lightly into their skin, dry and firm and pleasantly rough. They lifted the empty basket a little and set it down again, simply for the quiet pleasure of the texture. Ulla brought a small bowl of clean water and placed it where it would not splash the waiting pot. “Everything is ready when the clay is ready,” Ulla murmured. Pema nodded and kept their hands busy with small safe motions that never touched the covered pinch pot.

Time moved slowly in the ceramic studio. Pema counted the soft ticks of the wall clock and watched dust drift through a beam of late light. Nana Ruth stirred a bit of leftover slip in a jar with a wooden stick, making a tiny swirling sound that was almost like rain on a roof. Pema leaned their elbows on the table edge, careful to stay far from the cloth tent. Their cheeks felt pleasantly warm from the room’s gentle heat. Outside, a bird settled on the sill and made one low note before flying off. Pema stayed patient and still, eyes resting on the quiet shape beneath the white cloth, trusting the slip to firm and the handles to set without hurry.

At last Nana Ruth lifted a corner of the cloth with two careful fingers. The pinch pot still wearing wet clay slip now looked firmer, the surface no longer shiny with wetness but softly matte and ready for the next gentle step. Pema’s eyes widened with quiet gladness. “It waited for us,” Pema whispered. Nana Ruth nodded. “And you waited for it,” she replied. Ulla smiled and stepped back so Pema could see clearly. The air near the table smelled clean and earthy. Cool evening light pooled across the boards. Pema did not touch the pot yet; they only looked and felt proud of the choice to leave it covered until the right moment arrived on its own.

Later, when the tools had been rinsed and the basket set aside, Pema stood once more beside Nana Ruth at the ceramic studio table. The pinch pot rested uncovered now, calm and finished with its waiting. Pema breathed the last faint sweetness of tomato soup still lingering from the kitchen and felt the day’s quiet work settle into a peaceful tiredness behind their eyes. Nana Ruth rested a warm hand on Pema’s shoulder. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Pema watched the gentle light and knew the clay had taken exactly the time it needed, and that waiting nearby with small kind tasks had been enough.
