Soft late-day light pressed gently against the ceramic studio windows, warming the glass until a mild golden glow spread across the long table where clay slip and new handles waited to firm. Paz walked in slowly and felt that pleasant warmth on their cheeks as they set their small backpack down. The air inside held a quiet steadiness that matched the careful work happening there. Paz rested both hands on the cool edge of the table and looked at the covered shapes resting under damp cloths. Nana Ruth stood a few steps away, smoothing clay with patient fingers, and Paz felt glad to be near her. The light shifted a little as a cloud passed outside, and Paz watched the soft change travel over the wooden surface. They liked how the studio always smelled clean and earthy, and how the temperature stayed even and kind.

Paz turned toward a small folded paper boat that sat near the edge of the table, its tiny dots of glue still shiny and tacky where the folds met. They had made the boat earlier while the clay pieces needed time, folding carefully and pressing each corner, then placing dots of glue so the sides would hold. Now Paz wanted very much to lift the loose cloth covering and check whether the glue had set. The paper looked pale in the warm light, and Paz could almost feel how sticky those little dots would still be if they touched them too soon. Nana Ruth glanced over with a calm smile. “The boat needs its own quiet minutes, just like the handles,” Nana Ruth said softly. Paz nodded, though their fingers twitched once with the wish to peek.

The cloth over the paper boat showed one small wrinkle near the bow, and Paz smoothed that single wrinkle flat with the side of their hand, feeling the soft weave under their skin. They left the covering in place after that and stepped back. Nearby, a row of clay mugs with fresh handles sat drying, and Paz watched a thin edge of slip catch the light. Nana Ruth hummed a little tune under her breath, a sound as gentle as the studio air. “Shall we keep our hands busy with something slow while everything sets?” Nana Ruth asked. Paz answered, “Yes, I can wait with you.” They felt the table’s solid wood beneath their elbows and noticed how the temperature of the room stayed comfortably warm without ever growing hot.

Paz chose a quiet task that fit the space: they took a clean sponge and wiped tiny clay flecks from the far end of the table in slow circles, listening to the soft scrape of the sponge. The motion felt steady and kind. After a while Nana Ruth poured two small bowls of tomato soup from a thermos she had brought, and the warm smell of tomato soup drifted gently through the studio, mingling with the earthy clay scent. Paz breathed it in and smiled. “It smells like supper already,” Paz said. Nana Ruth replied, “We can have a little now and keep working.” They sat side by side on the stools and sipped carefully, feeling the heat of the bowls against their palms while the paper boat and the clay handles continued their quiet setting.

When the bowls were empty, Paz returned to the table and began arranging wooden tools in a neat row by size, feeling each smooth handle as they lined them up. A soft jingle came from the doorway as the studio cat wandered in, and the clear sound of a cat's collar bell rang once, then twice, before the cat settled on a sunny patch of floor. Paz glanced toward the covered paper boat again but kept their hands with the tools. Nana Ruth noticed and spoke in her low, even voice. “Everything finishes when it is ready, Paz. The glue, the slip, the day itself.” Paz answered, “I know. I can do one more quiet thing.” They felt proud of the calm choice settling inside them like a warm blanket.

Paz found a short length of soft cloth meant for polishing finished pieces and began folding it into neat squares, pressing each crease with care. The light through the windows had grown softer and more golden, painting long calm shapes across the floorboards. They could hear Nana Ruth’s quiet footsteps as she checked the clay handles without rushing. Paz kept their attention on the cloth squares and on the steady temperature of the room, which felt just right against their bare arms. Once they almost reached toward the paper boat again, but instead they counted the folds they had made and smiled at how tidy the little stack looked. The cat stretched and the collar bell gave one tiny final ring that faded into the stillness.

Time moved like honey in a jar, thick and unhurried. Paz decided to wipe the window ledge next, feeling the cool glass under their fingertips and watching dust sparkle in the late light. Nana Ruth came beside them and rested a hand lightly on Paz’s shoulder. “You have been so steady today,” she said. Paz looked up and answered, “The boat is still under its cloth, and I haven’t peeked.” They both smiled. Together they returned to the table, and Paz noticed that the clay handles no longer looked shiny-wet; they had begun to hold their shape. The paper boat waited too, and Paz felt a quiet certainty that the glue dots must be closer to ready now, though they still did not lift the cover.

At last Nana Ruth nodded toward the small covered shape. Paz carefully drew the cloth aside and saw that the dots of glue had turned clear and firm. The paper boat sat sturdy on the table, ready for gentle floating later in a shallow dish of water. Paz picked it up by the folded prow and felt how solid the joins had become. Nearby sat a basket that held clean sponges, and Paz closed their fingers for a moment around its wicker handle, feeling the woven texture press lightly against their skin before setting the boat down again with care. Nana Ruth watched with soft eyes. The studio light had grown even gentler, and the air held the last warmth of the afternoon as evening began to lean close.

Paz carried the finished paper boat to a low shelf near the window and placed it where it could rest overnight. They smoothed the cloth one last time across the ceramic table so everything looked ready for tomorrow. Nana Ruth turned down the main lamps until only a small light remained. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Paz stood close to Nana Ruth and felt the quiet of the finished waiting settle around them both like a kind evening hush, full of the soft temperature of the cooling room and the peaceful certainty that every careful thing had been given the time it needed.
