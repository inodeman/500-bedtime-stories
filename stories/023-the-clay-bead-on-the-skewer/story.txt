Auntie June stood at the greenhouse bench already, her hands gently pressing soft earth into a row of little seed pots that caught the warm afternoon light from the south window. The air felt pleasantly mild against Gita’s arms as they stepped closer, and the glass panes held a golden glow that made the soil look rich and dark. Auntie June hummed a quiet tune while she worked, and Gita watched the careful way each pot was settled into place beside the others. The benches smelled faintly of damp potting mix and green growing things, and the whole space felt snug and still. Gita’s fingers brushed the smooth wooden edge of the bench as they leaned in to see better.

“Look what we started this morning,” Auntie June said, nodding toward a long skewer that rested across two small blocks at the far end of the bench. On that skewer sat a neat row of clay beads, each one round and pale, covered lightly with a clean cloth so they could dry without dust settling on them. Gita’s eyes brightened at the sight. The beads looked almost ready, and the cloth felt cool when Gita’s fingertips rested near its corner. Sunlight from the south window warmed the tops of the nearby seed pots, and Gita could feel that gentle heat on their wrists.

Gita wanted very much to lift the cloth right then and see how firm the clay beads had become. They imagined rolling one carefully between their palms and hearing the soft click it might make against another. “May I peek at the beads now, Auntie June?” Gita asked, their voice soft in the quiet greenhouse. The air near the open vent carried a slight coolness that brushed Gita’s cheek, and Auntie June set down her trowel with a small clink against the pot rim.

“They still need more time in the dry air,” Auntie June answered kindly, wiping a bit of earth from her wrist. “If we lift the cloth too soon, the softest sides might catch a fingerprint or lose their round shape. We can leave them covered and do something gentle while we wait.” Gita nodded and let the cloth stay exactly where it was. The wooden bench felt smooth and solid under their palms as they rested there for a moment, thinking. A thin stripe of sunlight lay across the seed pots like a quiet ribbon of gold.

Instead of touching the skewer, Gita chose a quiet task that fit the calm room. They picked up a soft cloth and began to wipe the open part of the greenhouse table in slow circles, moving the dampness away in easy loops. The fresh scent of lemon soap rose from the cloth as they worked, clean and bright without being sharp. Auntie June smiled and continued tucking tiny seeds into the waiting pots. Gita’s circles grew slower and steadier, and the tabletop grew clearer under their careful hands. The south window glass felt cool whenever Gita’s sleeve brushed near it.

Outside the open vent, the sound of wind in the fig leaves drifted in, a soft shushing that rose and fell like a distant lullaby. Gita paused their wiping for a breath and listened, letting the gentle rustle settle into the room. Auntie June poured a little water into one seed pot, and the soil made a quiet drinking sound. “The leaves are talking to the afternoon,” Auntie June said with a small laugh. Gita smiled and started another slow circle on the table, feeling settled and patient as the minutes stretched comfortably. The light through the glass stayed warm and steady on the covered skewer.

After a while Auntie June brought over a floured towel from the side shelf, its surface lightly dusted and soft under Gita’s curious touch when they helped fold it for later bread dough. The flour left a fine, dry powdery feel against Gita’s fingertips, pleasant and familiar. They set the towel aside on a clean spot and returned to watching the cloth that hid the clay beads. Nothing rushed. The seed pots sat in their neat lines, and the south window kept pouring its kind light across everything. Gita rested their hands in their lap and simply breathed the mild greenhouse air.

“I think the beads have had enough quiet time now,” Auntie June said at last, glancing at the angle of the sunlight. Gita’s heart gave a happy little lift. Together they lifted the covering cloth with great care, and the row of clay beads on the skewer looked smooth and finished, each one holding its round shape perfectly. Gita touched one very lightly with a single fingertip and felt that it was firm and ready. “They waited just right,” Gita said softly, almost to themselves. The beads caught a little of the window’s glow and seemed to shine in their modest clay way. Auntie June nodded, and the two of them stood side by side in the warm stillness.

Later, when the seed pots were watered and the beads were set safely on a tray, Gita helped Auntie June tidy the last corners of the bench. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside, evening light turned gentle and blue, and inside the greenhouse the air stayed mild and kind. Gita’s hands smelled faintly clean from the earlier work, and they felt the smooth tray under their palms as they carried the finished beads a few careful steps. Auntie June dimmed the brighter lamps, and the whole space settled into a peaceful hush that invited slow breaths and quiet hearts ready for rest.
