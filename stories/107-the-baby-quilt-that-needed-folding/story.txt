Greta smelled the warm bread drifting from a nearby stall as their small hands already pressed down the lids on two clay jars along the market lane. The jars sat in a neat row beside flower pots filled with bright marigolds, and the late afternoon light turned the clay a soft orange. Greta moved slowly so nothing tipped, feeling the smooth outsides of the jars and listening to the quiet shuffle of basket lids nearby. Grandma Nia stood a few steps away under a short clothesline strung between two posts, where a pale baby quilt had been hung to air. Greta kept their fingers busy stacking empty baskets by size while the sweet scent settled around them like a gentle blanket. The lane felt calm and full of ordinary things that needed only careful hands.

Greta glanced up and watched the baby quilt slide a little farther down the line each time a soft breeze passed. The quilt was thick and square, patterned with tiny blue stars, and one corner already drooped toward a row of flower pots. Grandma Nia reached up on her toes, but the line sat just high enough that catching both slipping corners at once looked awkward for one person. Greta noticed how Grandma Nia’s arms stretched and how the quilt kept easing sideways in slow little slips. The light on the quilt looked gentle and clean, and Greta felt the warm air against their cheeks while they set the last basket straight. No one else seemed to see the problem yet, so Greta simply watched and waited for the right moment to step closer.

Greta walked over without calling out and took hold of the lower corner just as it brushed the rim of a flower pot. The fabric felt soft and a little cool from hanging in the open air, and Greta lifted it carefully so it would not drag. Grandma Nia looked down and smiled with quiet surprise. “Oh, Greta, you found the sliding corner,” Grandma Nia said in a low, pleased voice. Greta nodded and held their side steady while the light shifted across the cloth in pale gold stripes. The two of them stood close together between the baskets and the jars, and Greta felt glad that their hands already knew how to be useful. The market lane stayed peaceful around them, full of ordinary scents and soft sounds that made the work feel easy.

Greta and Grandma Nia began folding the baby quilt together, matching corner to corner in slow, careful motions. Greta kept their half of the fabric smooth and waited each time Grandma Nia adjusted her grip. “If we fold it once more, it will fit the basket,” Grandma Nia said. Greta answered softly, “I can hold this side while you bring yours over.” The texture of the quilt under Greta’s palms was thick and friendly, and the afternoon light made the blue stars look almost like tiny puddles of sky. They worked without hurry, and Greta never asked for thanks or praise, only kept the cloth steady so Grandma Nia did not have to stretch alone. A few baskets sat open nearby, ready to receive the folded quilt when they finished.

Greta felt the breeze move again and heard the wind in the fig leaves that grew just beyond the last row of flower pots. The sound came like a quiet whisper of paper pages turning, and it made the whole lane feel cooler for a moment. Greta held the half-folded quilt firmly so it would not catch air and open again. Grandma Nia smiled and said, “You took the useful half without me even asking, Greta.” Greta only shrugged a little and kept folding, matching the edges until the quilt became a neat soft square. The light under the fig leaves looked dappled and green, and Greta liked the way the shadows danced gently across the jars. They finished the last fold together and set the square into an empty basket lined with clean cloth.

Greta noticed a small bowl that had held extra clothespins resting on a wooden stool near the jars. The bowl was empty now, and Greta picked it up carefully. The cool ceramic rim pressed lightly against Greta’s fingers as they carried it a few steps to the side table where other clean dishes waited. Greta set the bowl down, came back at once, and stood beside Grandma Nia again. “Shall we check the other baskets now?” Greta asked. Grandma Nia nodded and answered, “Yes, Greta, that would be kind.” The two of them moved along the lane, straightening jar lids and tucking flower pots into safer spots while the light grew softer and more golden. Greta’s hands stayed busy and calm, and the empty bowl’s cool touch still lingered in their memory like a small secret of the afternoon.

Greta and Grandma Nia walked the short length of the market lane once more, making certain every basket sat level and every jar lid sat snug. Greta paused beside a cluster of flower pots and brushed a stray leaf from one rim, feeling the grainy clay under their fingertips. The air smelled clean now that the quilt was safely folded, and the light slanted lower between the posts. Grandma Nia rested a hand lightly on Greta’s shoulder for a moment. Greta did not need words of praise; the quiet work itself felt complete and right. They stood together looking at the neat row of baskets, and Greta felt the mild temperature of the late day settling around their arms like a second soft sleeve. Everything looked ready for evening.

Greta helped Grandma Nia gather the last empty baskets and carry them toward the little doorway that led home from the lane. Inside, the rooms smelled faintly of the same bread that had drifted outside earlier, and the light through the windows had turned the color of honey. Greta set one basket down near the wall and smoothed its handles flat. Grandma Nia placed the folded baby quilt on a low shelf where it could wait for the next morning. Greta sat for a moment on a wooden chair and felt the smooth seat under their legs while the house grew quieter. Outside, the fig leaves had stilled, and only a far soft murmur of the market remained. Greta’s hands rested open on their knees, still remembering the weight of the quilt and the helpful work they had shared.

Greta rose after a little while and walked to the window where evening had begun to settle in gentle blues. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Greta stood close enough to feel the mild warmth from the glass and watched the quiet lane grow still beyond the flower pots and jars. Grandma Nia came to stand beside them, and together they looked out at the peaceful darkening shapes of baskets left tidy for the night. Greta felt sleepy and content, the soft light of the lantern resting on their face like a calm goodbye to the day. They turned from the window at last, ready for bed, with the memory of folding the baby quilt still warm and steady inside them.
