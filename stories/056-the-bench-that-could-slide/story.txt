The bench rested on short wooden rails beside the long supper table, its seat boards smooth and slightly cool where the shade of the fig tree touched them. Milo crouched close and traced the shallow grooves that let the whole bench slide outward with a soft wooden whisper, making room for one more person without a fuss. Tiny flecks of old varnish caught the last honey-colored light, and the air near the legs smelled faintly of earth and leaf. Milo pressed their cheek to the edge for a moment and felt the solid weight of it, steady as a boat dock. They liked how the rails kept everything quiet and sure.

Grandpa Sol had already set out the long table under the wide arms of the fig tree, where green fruits hung like little lanterns of their own. Warm plates waited in a neat row, and the courtyard stones still held the gentle heat of the day beneath Milo’s bare feet. Yara arrived with a soft laugh and a basket of bread, the crusts crackling when she set it down. “I brought the round loaves,” Yara said, and Milo answered, “They smell perfect already.” The leaves above rustled in a mild evening breeze that brushed Milo’s arms like a light scarf. Grandpa Sol smiled and nodded toward the sliding bench, inviting them both to settle.

Milo slid the bench out carefully so three could sit side by side without crowding, the rails gliding with a low wooden hum. They sat in the middle, Yara on one side and Grandpa Sol on the other, the fig leaves filtering speckled light across their laps. A damp sweater had been draped over a nearby chair after a brief cool mist earlier, and the smell of wet wool drifted past once before fading into the supper scents. “Shall we begin?” Grandpa Sol asked in his calm voice. Milo looked at the steaming bowls and the empty plates and felt a quiet worry bloom. They wanted every share to feel the same size.

The first ladle of soup hovered over Grandpa Sol’s bowl, and Milo saw at once that starting with only one person would leave the others waiting in a way that felt uneven. The tin clock on the side table ticked a steady soft note that seemed to mark the moment. “Wait,” Milo said gently, holding up one hand. “If we fill one bowl all the way first, it will not feel fair when Yara is right here too.” Yara tilted her head, the light catching the curve of her smile. Grandpa Sol paused with the ladle still raised, steam curling upward in the mild air, and waited to see what Milo would choose.

Milo reached for the smaller bowls stacked near the bread and began to portion the soup into three equal parts, each one a little less deep than a full serving yet matching the others exactly. They moved slowly so nothing spilled, the warm ceramic edges pleasant against their fingers. “This way everyone starts together,” Milo said, sliding a bowl toward Yara and another toward Grandpa Sol before taking their own. The fig tree stirred again, dropping a single soft leaf onto the tablecloth with a faint papery sound. Yara’s eyes shone in the golden light. “That feels right,” she answered, and the courtyard seemed to settle around the three of them.

Grandpa Sol watched without a single correcting word, simply accepting the three smaller shares as they were. He set the ladle down and picked up his spoon, the metal cool for an instant before the soup warmed it. Milo noticed a folded paper napkin by their plate and ran one fingertip along its paper edge, feeling the thin crisp line before unfolding it neatly across their lap. The bread passed from hand to hand, each piece torn with care so the soft insides stayed warm. Evening air cooled the stones under the table, and Milo breathed the peaceful quiet that had grown between them. No one hurried, and the equal bowls glowed with the same soft steam.

They ate slowly while the sky turned the color of ripe figs, purple and soft at the edges. Milo listened to the quiet clink of spoons and the far-off call of a bird settling for night. Grandpa Sol offered a second piece of bread without speaking of portions again, trusting the choice Milo had made. Yara hummed a little tune under her breath that matched the calm of the courtyard. The sliding bench held them steadily, its extra space no longer needed for fairness because the shares themselves had already made room. Milo felt the evening temperature slip lower and pulled their sleeves down over their wrists, content.

After the bowls were empty, Milo helped gather the spoons while the kettle on the small side stove began its quiet tick as it warmed for mint tea. They breathed in time with that gentle sound, shoulders easing, and watched the last light slide across the fig leaves. “Thank you for waiting with us,” Milo told Grandpa Sol, and he only smiled and patted the bench rail once. Yara stacked the plates with careful hands, the ceramic still carrying a trace of warmth. The courtyard held the mild scent of fig wood and cooling stone. Everything felt finished in the best way, small and even and shared.

Later Milo sat on the edge of their bed inside the quiet house, the day’s supper still soft in their memory like a held hand. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the courtyard lay peaceful under the same fig tree, the long table cleared and the sliding bench drawn back into place. Milo closed their eyes and felt the calm temperature of the sheets against their legs, ready for sleep.
