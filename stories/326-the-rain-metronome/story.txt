A soft clink of a cup against a saucer sounded from the kitchen counter, and Dahlia turned toward it while the last gray light of evening settled cool across the floorboards. They wiped their hands on a dish towel and listened again, wondering what small thing had shifted in the quiet house. Mama stood nearby folding a damp cloth, and Faye waited by the back door with patient eyes. “I think the rain has stopped,” Dahlia said softly. The air drifting through the cracked window felt cooler than before, carrying a clean freshness that made Dahlia want to step outside at once. They slipped on their shoes and followed Mama and Faye toward the garden, already curious about whatever the gentle rain might have left behind along the path.

Outside, the garden path gleamed dark and smooth under their feet after the gentle rain, and Dahlia walked slowly so they would not splash the shallow puddles. The wet leaves brushed their sleeves with a slick coolness while Faye stayed close beside them. Mama carried a folded sweater that still held the clean smell of soapy wool from the afternoon wash, and Dahlia breathed it in as they moved between the flower beds. Tiny beads of water hung from every stem, catching the last pale light. “Listen,” Dahlia whispered to Faye, “everything sounds softer now.” They paused near a low stone border and touched one cool wet petal with one careful finger, feeling how the whole garden seemed to hold its breath after the rain.

Farther along the path the porch came into view, its wooden boards still dark with moisture, and Dahlia noticed three separate patterns of water falling from the edge of the roof. One thin stream ticked quickly against an empty tin pot, another dripped at a steady middle pace into a shallow saucer, and a third fell slowly and deeply onto a broad leaf that someone had left there. The rain ticking at three different speeds on the porch made a gentle uneven music that held Dahlia still. Faye crouched lower to hear better. Cool air moved across Dahlia’s cheeks as they stood listening, and the soft light from the house windows reached just far enough to show the shining drops.

Dahlia felt a quick wish to reach out and poke the fastest drops or even scoop a few into a pocket to keep the ticking close, yet they stayed their hand and turned instead toward Faye. “If we look without touching,” Dahlia said, “we can hear all three at once.” Faye nodded and kept both hands folded in their lap. Mama stepped nearer on quiet feet, and the leather lace on one of Dahlia’s shoes brushed lightly against the porch step as they shifted closer to the edge. The three speeds continued side by side, fast then medium then slow, like quiet drums talking to one another in the damp evening air.

Faye leaned in until their shoulder nearly touched Dahlia’s and whispered, “It sounds like the porch is keeping time for us.” Dahlia smiled and pointed gently with one open hand so Faye could follow each stream without moving nearer. “The quick one is like little footsteps,” Dahlia explained, “and the slow one is like a long breath.” Mama rested a warm palm on Dahlia’s shoulder and watched too, saying nothing at first. A faint jingle from a cat’s collar bell drifted from the garden behind them as the household cat wandered past the lavender, yet none of them turned away from the porch. The boards underfoot felt cool and slightly rough, and the air stayed mild enough that no one shivered.

They stayed there while the light grew softer and the three kinds of ticking remained steady and clear. Dahlia showed Faye how to tilt their head one way and then another so each speed stood out by itself for a moment before blending again. “Mama, do you hear the middle one best from here?” Dahlia asked. Mama answered, “Yes, and it feels calm just to stand and listen.” No one reached forward. The wish to pocket or poke the rain ticking faded as Dahlia noticed how the drops kept their own quiet work only when left alone. Faye’s quiet breathing matched the slowest drip for a few seconds, and Dahlia felt the shared stillness settle over all three of them like a light blanket.

After a long shared minute Dahlia straightened and took one small step back from the porch edge. “We can leave it right where it is,” they said, and Faye rose carefully beside them. Mama smiled and turned toward the garden path again. They walked home slowly, shoes making soft wet sounds on the stones, while the three speeds of rain ticking grew fainter behind them yet never fully disappeared from memory. Dahlia glanced back once and saw the tin pot, the saucer, and the broad leaf still catching their separate drops under the porch roof. The evening air had cooled another degree, and a single firefly blinked near the fence, adding its own tiny light to the damp garden.

Inside the kitchen once more, Dahlia hung their jacket and noticed the spoons still drying on a towel. They lined the spoons up by size in a neat silver row while Faye watched and Mama filled the kettle for warm milk. The ordinary clink of metal against cloth felt friendly after the outdoor music of water. Dahlia ran one finger along the largest spoon and then the smallest, enjoying the smooth cool surfaces. “Tomorrow we can listen again if the rain returns,” Faye said. Dahlia nodded and answered, “And we will look the same quiet way.” The house smelled faintly of the milk beginning to warm, and the overhead light cast a gentle yellow circle across the table where they sat together for a little while longer.

Later Dahlia climbed into bed while Mama smoothed the covers and Faye waved good night from the doorway. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Cool sheets settled against Dahlia’s arms, and the distant memory of three ticking speeds mixed with the hush of the house until their eyes grew heavy and sleep arrived like another quiet rain.
