The tray of honey sweets sat still upon the smooth marble, each round piece gleaming like a small sun under the pale cottage light that slipped through the glass. The honey had turned cloudy at the edges while the middles stayed softly clear, and a thin warm sweetness rose from them into the quiet air. Femi leaned close enough to see the faint swirl patterns hardening, their breath fogging a little circle on the cool stone. Outside the window the late day glowed golden across the river, yet inside everything felt hushed and safe. The marble held a gentle chill against the wood of the sill, and Femi’s fingertips hovered just above without touching.

Femi stood on the low stool by the cottage windowsill crowded with jars of jam, empty trays, and long strips of drying wool that hung like soft banners. The sweets were not ready yet, Femi knew, because the ferryman had said so only moments before, yet the wish to peek under the clean cloth cover pulled hard. Soft afternoon light warmed Femi’s cheeks while the wool fringe brushed their wrist with a dry, tickling touch. Jars caught rainbows on their glass sides, and the marble tray waited patiently in the middle of it all. Femi rocked once on the stool and whispered, “Just one little look would tell me.”

The ferryman sat in the wooden chair near the hearth, his river boots set neatly by the door, and he smiled when he heard Femi’s wish. “They need more time to set firm, Femi,” he said in his deep calm voice. “Cover stays on until the tin clock sings six.” Femi nodded slowly, feeling the cool floorboards under bare feet. A faint green scent of basil drifted from the herb pot on the sill, mixing with the honey air. Ren, who had come to visit and help tidy, sat cross-legged nearby sorting buttons and looked up with bright eyes. “We can wait together,” Ren said softly.

Femi drew the light cloth back over the tray so the sweets rested safe and unseen, then stepped away from the marble. The ferryman nodded approval and turned a page in his small notebook. Warm light from the western window striped the floor in long gentle bands. Femi’s hands found a polished pebble that Ren had brought from the river path; it felt cool and perfectly smooth in the palm like a tiny frozen moon. “I will do something quiet until they finish,” Femi told them both. Ren smiled and pointed toward the basket of folded cloths waiting on the side table.

Femi carried the basket to the round table and began to unfold each napkin so the stitched pear design faced upward in neat rows. The fabric felt soft and slightly crisp from drying near the wool strips. Outside a bird called once and then fell silent. The ferryman rose and poured warm milk into two cups, the liquid making a low pouring sound that matched the calm room. “These pears look happy when they face the light,” Femi said, smoothing one corner carefully. Ren handed over the next napkin and answered, “They do, Femi. Everything looks happier when we give it time.”

While the napkins grew into tidy stacks, Femi listened to the steady tick of the tin clock on the shelf above the jars. Each soft metal click marked another moment the sweets spent setting on their marble bed. The air near the window held a gentle coolness that brushed Femi’s arms whenever the cloth moved. Ren hummed a little river tune without words, and the ferryman joined with a quiet hum of his own. Femi placed the last napkin pear-side up and pressed it flat with both hands. The polished pebble rested nearby on the table, catching a spark of light each time Femi glanced over.

The ferryman set the milk cups down and invited Femi and Ren to sip while they waited. The milk tasted mild and left a warm path down Femi’s throat. Shadows lengthened slowly across the drying wool, turning the white strands to soft gray. “The river taught me long ago that boats arrive when the water is ready, never sooner,” the ferryman said. Femi watched the cloth-covered tray and felt the wish to lift it fade into something quieter and easier. Ren reached over and gave Femi’s shoulder a light friendly pat that felt like a small promise of company.

Time stretched gently while Femi returned the empty cups to the sideboard and checked that every napkin still showed its pear. The marble tray under its cover remained undisturbed, and the jars glowed like quiet jewels in the fading light. A soft draft from the window carried the last of the day’s warmth across Femi’s face. The ferryman closed his notebook and stood by the sill, looking out at the calm water. “Almost,” he murmured. Femi stood beside him, close enough to feel the solid presence of his coat, and together they simply watched the light change.

When the tin clock’s hands finally pointed to six, Femi lifted the cloth with careful fingers and saw the honey sweets standing firm and perfect on the marble. Their surfaces shone with a soft finished glow, no longer sticky to the eye. Ren clapped once, quietly, and the ferryman ruffled Femi’s hair with a proud gentle hand. The room smelled clean and sweet. Femi carried one small sweet on a saucer to the table where the napkins waited in their neat pear rows. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
