"Please may I lift the cloth just once, Auntie Luz?" Tala asked in a whisper, their voice almost as quiet as the faint golden light that pooled across the craft corner table. Auntie Luz sat close on a low stool, her hands folded, and the wooden rack behind them held neat stacks of colored sheets ready for gluing. The pickle jar rested there too, round and cool after the brine had been poured, its lid snug and a soft cloth draped over the top so the cucumbers could rest in peace. Warm lamp glow touched the paste pots and the jar alike, and Tala felt the mild evening air on their cheeks while they looked from the covered glass to Auntie Luz and back again, wanting so much to know what waited inside yet staying still for one more breath.

Tala leaned nearer the wooden rack and smelled the clean, bright scent of fresh paper stacked beside the paste brushes, a smell that made the craft corner feel open and ready. Auntie Luz smiled without hurry. The pickle jar sat quiet under its cloth, the brine already poured and the vegetables settling into their slow work, and Tala knew it was not yet time to peek. Light from the small window fell in a gentle stripe across the table edge. Tala traced a fingertip along a sheet’s corner and listened to the tiny rustle it made, then looked at Auntie Luz again, holding the wish to check inside the jar the way a child holds a secret pebble in a pocket.

“The jar needs its own quiet hours, Tala,” Auntie Luz said, her tone warm as the soft heat rising from the floorboards. “We leave the cloth right where it is, and we make something pretty while we wait.” Tala nodded and felt the pleasant coolness of the evening gather around their ankles. They pulled a chair closer to the paper and paste, the wooden rack steady at their shoulder, and the covered pickle jar stayed exactly as it was. Tala chose then, with Auntie Luz nearby, to leave the jar covered and to begin a calm craft instead of lifting the cloth too soon.

Tala picked up a sheet and felt its smooth face under their palms, then dipped a brush into the paste pot where the glue shone like thin milk in the low light. Auntie Luz watched with kind eyes while Tala spread a careful line of paste and pressed a second piece of paper on top to make a simple flower shape for later. The craft corner smelled faintly of paste and wood, and the temperature in the room stayed gentle, neither warm nor cold. Tala worked slowly, glancing once at the cloth-draped jar on the rack and then returning to the paper, trusting the waiting time the way Auntie Luz had shown.

“I can make three flowers before we look,” Tala said, and Auntie Luz answered, “That sounds just right, Tala.” The words floated softly between them while Tala pressed another petal into place. Somewhere near the craft corner the house wall felt different when Tala’s elbow brushed it, a damp clay wall cool and slightly grainy under the sleeve, and the coolness reminded Tala to slow their hands even more. Paste dried in little shiny patches on the table, and the wooden rack held steady, keeping both the papers and the resting pickle jar safe while the evening light turned a shade softer.

Outside, a gate latch clicked once with a small metal sound that drifted in through the open window and then faded, leaving the craft corner quieter than before. Tala paused, brush in hand, and listened to the silence that followed. Auntie Luz hummed a low tune without words. Tala set the finished paper flower on the rack’s lower shelf, well away from the covered jar, and began another shape, folding edges with careful thumbs. The paste felt sticky and cool between their fingers, and the lamp light made tiny stars on the jar’s cloth. Tala kept the cloth untouched, choosing again the quiet task over the early peek.

Tala rested both palms on their knees for a long still moment, feeling the fabric of their trousers and the calm rise and fall of their own breathing, then picked up the next sheet. Auntie Luz reached over and steadied the paste pot so it would not tip. “You are doing fine work, Tala,” she said. Tala smiled and answered, “I like waiting with you.” The words hung gently in the mild air. Paper scraps gathered in a neat pile, and the wooden rack seemed almost like a small shelf of patience itself, holding the pickle jar while the brine finished its quiet changing inside the glass. Light from the window had grown dimmer, yet the craft corner stayed bright enough for careful hands.

Time moved the way soft shadows move across a floor. Tala finished the third paper flower and lined all three in a row beside the paste brushes. Auntie Luz checked the clock on the wall with a glance and then looked at Tala. The craft corner held the faint warmth of two people sitting close, and the covered jar on the wooden rack no longer felt like a secret that needed opening right away. Tala wiped a spot of paste from their wrist on a cloth and sat back, content to let the jar stay covered until the moment Auntie Luz would say it was ready. A breeze brought a cooler ribbon of air across the table, and Tala shivered once with pleasure, not with cold.

When Auntie Luz finally lifted the cloth, the pickle jar gleamed clean and settled, the work inside complete in its own time, and Tala clapped once very softly so the sound would not startle the quiet. They both admired the jar for a peaceful minute before Tala placed one paper flower beside it on the rack as a bright companion. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Tala leaned against Auntie Luz’s shoulder and felt the last of the evening warmth in the room, the paste pots closed, the papers stacked, and the wooden rack holding everything steady while sleep began to call their name in the gentlest way.
