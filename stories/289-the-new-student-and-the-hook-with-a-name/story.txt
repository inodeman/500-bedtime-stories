"Look how the water holds the last gold light," Farah said, resting both elbows on the ferry rail where only two elbows of room remained between the cold metal and the wooden board behind them. The evening air felt mild on Farah’s face, cooler near the water than on the dock, and the deck boards gave a quiet thrum under their shoes. Neighbor Sami stood a step back with a thermos and a small paper packet, smiling but not stepping into the little space. Yuri hovered just outside the fun, near an empty hook screwed into the board, a blank oval of wood where a name could be written if someone chose. Farah kept glancing from the ripples to Yuri’s quiet hands and felt the gentle press of the breeze through their sleeves.

Farah shifted sideways so the narrow strip of deck opened a little more, the way a door eases when someone needs room. The rail was smooth and chalky under their palms from many other travelers. Farah listened to the low engine and the soft hush of water sliding past the hull. Yuri still stood by the empty hook, watching the game of naming shapes in the clouds that two other children had started closer to the middle of the deck. Farah did not call anyone over. Instead they moved a folded jacket from a bench slat and slid the bench a hand’s width toward the rail, making a third seat that faced the same view. The wood of the bench felt dry and warm from the day, still holding a little heat even as dusk settled.

Neighbor Sami unscrewed the thermos, and a soft curl of cinnamon rose into the air, sweet and familiar, as if a kitchen had briefly visited the deck. Farah breathed it in and smiled without making a speech. “There is space right here if you want the same water as us,” Farah said to Yuri, keeping their voice ordinary and low so it sounded like an invitation to share a seat rather than a prize. Yuri’s shoulders loosened. Sami only nodded once and stayed put, letting the children arrange things themselves. Farah patted the open slat twice, a small sound against the wood, and the ferry leaned gently as it rounded a bend in the channel.

Yuri stepped closer and sat. Farah offered the paper packet Sami had set down, and inside lay a walnut-smooth spoon resting on a napkin beside two apple slices. Farah felt the spoon’s polished curve with one finger before handing it over so Yuri could stir the warm drink Sami poured into a spare cup. The spoon made no clatter; it only slid. “I like the way the wake draws white lines,” Yuri said, and Farah answered, “Me too, especially when they fade and come back.” The empty hook hung behind them still blank, quiet and unused, and Farah decided not to write anything on it or point it out. The place beside them was enough.

Farah changed the pace of their cloud game without announcing new rules. Instead of calling turns, Farah simply waited longer between comments so Yuri could speak when ready, and they pointed more with their chin than with loud words. The sky above the far shore turned the color of pale peach jam, a soft light that made every face gentle. Neighbor Sami leaned on the rail a few steps away, humming nothing and watching the children keep their own order. Farah felt glad that no one needed to crow about fairness. The deck’s small width meant knees almost touched, and that nearness felt friendly rather than crowded.

A gate latch clicked somewhere near the stairwell to the lower cabin, a short metal sound that rose and settled again. Farah glanced that way, then back to the water. “We can take turns holding the rail if the boat sways,” Farah said, and Yuri nodded. Farah showed how to plant feet a little apart so the sway felt like a slow dance. They did not rush. When a gull coasted past, white against the dimming blue, Farah only tipped their head, and Yuri tipped theirs too. The empty hook stayed empty on purpose, not lonely, just unused, because the real place was the bench and the shared view. Sami’s quiet presence told Farah that solving it themselves was welcome.

Farah hummed four notes, soft as breath, and then left a rest so the ferry’s own sounds could fill it. The air along the rail grew cooler as the sun slipped lower, a clean chill that made Farah tuck their hands into their sleeves for a moment before resting them again on the metal. Yuri copied the habit without being told. “Neighbor Sami, may we keep this little bench until the landing?” Farah asked, and Sami answered, “You three already made it work.” Farah grinned at the word three, which included Yuri without fanfare. The wake kept drawing its white lines, and Farah watched them stretch and softenthe way chalk lines do in rain, though no rain came.

When the ferry slowed, Farah helped gather the cups and the walnut spoon’s napkin, though the spoon itself had already gone back into Sami’s bag. The light on the water had turned silver and calm. Farah stood, left the empty hook as it was, and walked with Yuri toward the stair, matching steps so neither hurried. Neighbor Sami followed at an easy distance. Farah felt the slight rise of the deck as the boat met the dock pilings, a mild lift under their shoes, and they waited for the ropes rather than pushing forward. In the closer quiet, Farah said, “Next time we can bring crayons for doodles, not for names if we do not need them,” and Yuri’s small laugh was quick and warm. The two elbows of room by the rail had been enough all along.

At home later, Farah sat by their own window while the ferry day settled into memory like a folded blanket. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Farah remembered the cool rail, the shared bench, and Yuri’s shoulder near theirs, and they listened to the house tick and hush as sleep came close, the cinnamon ghost of the thermos still pleasant in mind, the day complete without any loud declaration, only the steady comfort of room made kindly and kept.
