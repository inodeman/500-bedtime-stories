A tiny click sounded from the shelf clock as dusk settled over the cottage, and Idris noticed it right away while sitting on the braided rug. Warm lamplight painted soft circles on the floorboards and made the air feel cozy against Idris’s bare arms. Idris stood and walked toward the cottage windowsill crowded with glass jars of seeds, shallow trays of damp earth, and loops of drying wool that hung like pale clouds. The wool gave off a clean soapy scent that mixed with the cool evening air drifting through a cracked pane. Idris ran one finger along the sill’s smooth wood and paused beside a small clay pot where tender pea shoots waited beneath a folded paper hood. The ferryman sat nearby on a low stool, his coat still smelling faintly of river water, and he looked up with a gentle smile as Idris approached.

Idris leaned closer to the paper hood and felt a strong wish to lift its edge just a little, because the pale green tips seemed to call for a peek. The light from the window turned the paper almost translucent, and Idris could almost imagine the tiny leaves stretching underneath. A soft warmth rose from the tray of earth beside the pot, touching Idris’s wrists like a quiet breath. “I want to see if the pea shoots have grown taller yet,” Idris said, voice hushed so the quiet room would stay peaceful. The ferryman set down the bit of rope he had been coiling and rested his hands on his knees. Outside the glass the sky held the last soft blue of day, and inside everything felt still and safe.

The ferryman spoke in a low, even tone that matched the calm of the drying wool. “The pea shoots like their paper hood a while longer, Idris,” he said. “They finish when they are ready.” Idris heard the words and nodded slowly, fingers hovering near the clay pot but not quite touching the hood. A cool draft slipped past the window latch and brushed Idris’s cheek, carrying the distant hush of the river the ferryman knew so well. Felix, who had come to visit and now sat cross-legged on a cushion, watched with bright eyes and offered a small nod of agreement. Idris took a careful step back from the windowsill so the paper hood stayed exactly where it belonged.

“What can we do while the pea shoots take their time?” Idris asked, turning toward the ferryman and Felix. The ferryman reached into a shallow basket and drew out a handful of smooth stones and one clear glass bead that caught the lamplight. Idris picked up the glass bead and rolled its cool, rounded surface between thumb and finger, feeling how perfectly round and satiny it was. Felix smiled and said, “We could sort the pretty things by color and size.” The three of them settled nearer the window where the jars and trays kept quiet company, and the drying wool hung motionless in the mild air. Idris placed the glass bead into a small wooden dish and began arranging the stones beside it with slow, careful movements.

A faint jingle of a cat’s collar bell floated in from the hallway as the cottage cat wandered closer and settled on a sunny patch of floor still holding the day’s leftover warmth. Idris glanced over and smiled at the soft sound before returning to the quiet task of lining up the stones. The ferryman hummed a short river tune under his breath, the notes drifting like gentle water. “These jars hold so many beginnings,” the ferryman remarked, nodding toward the windowsill. Idris answered, “I like how the trays smell of fresh earth after rain.” Felix added a quiet laugh and slid another stone into place, and the whole room felt wrapped in patience without anyone needing to name it.

Idris decided to name the scents that floated near the windowsill while their hands kept busy with the stones. “I smell mint leaves from the little bunch in that jar,” Idris said softly, “and the clean wool, and the sweet soap still clinging to the cloths.” The green mint leaves released their bright, cool fragrance each time the air stirred, and Idris breathed it in with a small happy sigh. No one spoke of wanting to lift the paper hood; instead the three of them simply stayed close to the light and the jars. The temperature near the glass stayed pleasantly mild, neither chilly nor warm, perfect for lingering. Felix passed Idris another smooth stone, and the quiet sorting continued in easy rhythm.

Time stretched like the soft wool hanging above the trays, and Idris kept their attention on the small wooden dish that now held a neat row of stones and the single gleaming bead. The ferryman told a short story about a calm crossing at dawn when the water had mirrored the sky so perfectly that birds seemed to fly twice. Idris listened and felt the wooden floor solid and steady under their folded legs. “Did the boat rock at all?” Idris wondered aloud. “Only a little, like a cradle,” the ferryman replied with a kind crinkle around his eyes. The paper hood over the pea shoots remained undisturbed, its edges casting a faint shadow across the clay pot in the fading light.

Felix stood for a moment and stretched, then returned to sit beside Idris again as the last orange glow slipped from the windowpanes. Idris noticed how the jars now reflected tiny points of lamplight and how the drying wool looked almost silver. The air held a gentle hush broken only by the occasional soft shift of the cat’s paws somewhere behind them. Idris’s shoulders felt loose and easy, and their breathing matched the slow pace of the room. “I think the pea shoots must be nearly finished growing under there,” Idris whispered, yet made no move to touch the hood. The ferryman simply smiled and kept his hands folded, trusting the quiet passage of minutes.

Idris finished placing the final stone and sat back on their heels, content to watch the clay pot without lifting anything. A soft green tip finally nudged the edge of the paper hood all by itself, proving the shoots had taken exactly the time they needed. The ferryman gave a quiet nod of approval, and Felix clapped once very softly. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Idris breathed in the lingering mint and wool and felt the whole cottage settle into night around them like a familiar blanket.
