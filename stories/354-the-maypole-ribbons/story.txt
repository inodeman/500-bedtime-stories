The late afternoon air brushed the wooden planks of the harbor dock with a gentle coolness that smelled faintly of salt and wood, and Dori stepped carefully along the edge where the baskets waited in neat rows. Soft golden light slipped between the tall posts and warmed the tops of Dori’s shoes while they watched the quiet water rock against the pilings. Dori reached out and touched one of the waiting baskets, feeling how steady everything felt under the kind sky. Farther along the dock a small flag fluttered without hurry, and Dori smiled at the peaceful way the whole place seemed to breathe.

Dori noticed the long maypole ribbons coiled inside several of the open baskets, bright streamers of red, yellow, and blue that had been brought ashore to dress the tall maypole standing near the warehouse for the spring gathering. A warm drift of tomato soup rose from a covered pot resting on a nearby crate, where someone had set supper for later, and the comforting scent mixed with the harbor air as Dori leaned closer. The ribbons looked ready to climb the pole once they were free of their coils, yet Dori could already see how easily the long lengths might twist together if anyone tugged too quickly. Dori called softly for help and waited.

Oren arrived first, shoes quiet on the boards, and right behind came Uncle Idris carrying an empty basket of his own. A small dock cat trotted past them, and the clear tinkle of a cat’s collar bell rang once in the calm air before the cat settled on a sun-warmed rope. “We need these ribbons up to the maypole without a single snarl,” Dori said, looking from the baskets to the tall painted pole. Oren nodded and touched one ribbon end with careful fingers. Uncle Idris set his basket down and rested a hand on Dori’s shoulder while the three of them studied the colorful coils together.

Dori lifted the nearest basket by its side and felt the smooth curve of a wicker handle press firmly against their palm, solid and familiar. The ribbons inside shifted just a little, already beginning to lean toward one another the way they always did when anyone moved too fast. “If we hurry they will tangle for sure,” Dori said, voice low and thoughtful. Oren peered into the next basket and agreed with a quiet hum. Uncle Idris only smiled and waited, ready to help but never pushing. The light on the water flickered gently, and Dori felt the plan begin to form inside their mind like a slow-moving tide.

Dori and Oren decided right then to divide the work into two unhurried jobs so nothing would rush. Dori would loosen each coil one by one while Oren steadied the free ends and guided them toward the base of the maypole. “I will take the red and yellow first, very slowly,” Dori said. Oren answered, “And I will walk them over without pulling.” Uncle Idris offered only a soft count when they needed it, saying the numbers like a friendly song. The boards under their feet held the leftover warmth of the day, and every movement stayed easy and even.

Dori began with the first long ribbon, easing it free of its neighbors the way one might unfold a sleeping blanket. The cloth of the ribbon felt cool and silky between Dori’s fingers as they smoothed one small wrinkle out of the cloth before handing the end to Oren. Oren took it with both hands and stepped backward in tiny careful paces, never jerking the line. Uncle Idris watched and then spoke the name of the simple loop they would need later: “That soft circle knot will hold it fine.” Dori repeated the name under their breath and kept the pace steady while a gull glided overhead without a single cry.

Oren finished walking the first set of ribbons to the maypole foot and returned for the next colors, still moving like a quiet shadow. Dori loosened the blue streamers next, pausing whenever the strands tried to lean together, and Uncle Idris counted softly, “One breath, two breaths, three,” so the rhythm stayed gentle. The harbor water lapped the dock in a slow rhythm that matched their hands. Dori felt proud of how the ribbons stayed free and bright. “We are almost there,” Dori whispered, and Oren grinned across the space between them.

Together they lifted the last basket and passed the final ribbons hand to hand, Dori loosening, Oren guiding, Uncle Idris naming the finishing knot once more so everyone remembered. The three of them wound the free ends loosely around the base of the maypole where they would wait for morning. No tangle had formed because no one had rushed. The air had grown cooler and the first evening shadows stretched long and friendly across the dock. Dori rested both hands on the smooth pole and felt how everything had settled into place exactly as they had hoped.

Dori, Oren, and Uncle Idris sat for a moment on the edge of the dock with the empty baskets stacked neatly beside them, listening to the quiet water and the distant creak of rope. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Dori leaned against Uncle Idris’s side and watched the last pink light fade from the sky while the ribbons waited calmly for tomorrow, every bright length free and ready, and the whole harbor felt peaceful and complete under the coming night.
