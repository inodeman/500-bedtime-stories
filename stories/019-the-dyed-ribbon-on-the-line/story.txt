The sweet smell of pear skins rose from a small bowl near the sink while Opal’s hands already moved along the ceramic studio table, pressing a soft handle onto a cup that still needed time to firm. Cool slip clung to their fingertips as they smoothed the join with slow circles, careful not to hurry the clay. Afternoon light slanted across the wooden surface where other pieces rested under damp cloths, and the air felt pleasantly warm against Opal’s bare arms. Nana Ruth sat close by on a low stool, her own hands steady as she checked a plate rim. Opal glanced once toward the indoor drying line strung near the window, where a length of dyed ribbon hung dripping in soft purple drops onto a folded towel below.

Opal’s fingers paused on the handle. The ribbon looked almost dry at the edges, and they wanted very much to lift the cloth cover Nana Ruth had draped over part of the line and touch the bright fabric right then. “Nana Ruth, may I check the ribbon now?” Opal asked in a quiet voice. The clay under their palms stayed cool and smooth, reminding them that everything on the table also needed stillness. Nana Ruth looked up with a gentle smile and wiped a speck of slip from her wrist. Outside the open window the soft sound of wind in the fig leaves drifted in, mixing with the quiet of the room.

“Not quite yet, Opal,” Nana Ruth said. “The ribbon is still giving up its water, just the way these handles need to set without being poked. We can leave the cover down a little longer.” Opal nodded and felt the low hum of the studio settle around them again. They pressed one last gentle curve into the handle, then rinsed their hands in the basin where the water felt lukewarm and silky. The towel they used was nubby against their skin, and they folded it neatly before returning to the table. Quinn, who had come to help wash brushes, stood nearby sorting soft bristles into a jar that clicked faintly when set down.

Opal decided to leave the ribbon covered and find a quiet task instead. They picked up a soft sponge and began wiping stray slip from the edges of the table in long, calm strokes. The wood felt smooth and slightly damp under the sponge, and the motion kept their hands busy without disturbing anything that needed rest. “Quinn, will you help me clear these extra tools?” Opal said. Quinn nodded and passed a wooden rib that smelled faintly of soap. Together they set the tools in a neat row, and Opal felt glad the ribbon could drip in peace while the clay pieces stayed undisturbed under their cloths.

Nana Ruth rose and placed a small warm coin into Opal’s palm for safekeeping while she stepped aside to fetch fresh water. The metal sat smooth and comforting against Opal’s skin, holding the heat of Nana Ruth’s hand for a long moment. Opal closed their fingers around it and stood still, listening to the quiet drip of the ribbon somewhere behind its cover. They did not lift the cloth. Instead they slid the coin into their pocket and turned back to the table, where one cup handle looked nearly ready but still required patience. The light in the room had grown softer, golden along the shelves of finished bowls.

Opal used the side of their foot to straighten a curled corner of the woven rug that lay near the table legs. The rug fibers felt thick and dry against their bare toes, and the small action settled the floor so no one would catch a toe later. “The handles look peaceful now,” Opal said to Nana Ruth when she returned. Nana Ruth smiled and set the fresh pitcher down with a soft clink. “They do,” she answered. “Everything finishes when it is ready, Opal. The ribbon will tell us by feeling dry all the way through.” Opal breathed in the calm air and kept their hands away from the line, choosing instead to watch a bead of water slide slowly down the outside of a water jar.

Quinn brought over a clean cloth and helped Opal dab the last traces of slip from a mug base. The cloth was soft and slightly fuzzy, and it left the clay looking smooth and pale. Opal worked beside Quinn without speaking for a while, enjoying the quiet scrape of cloth on clay and the distant hush of leaves beyond the glass. They thought about the purple ribbon hanging still and covered, dripping at its own slow pace. No one rushed it. Nana Ruth hummed a low tune that matched the gentle rhythm of the room, and Opal felt the warm coin in their pocket shift when they leaned forward to reach a farther spot on the table.

At last Nana Ruth walked to the indoor line and lifted the cover with careful fingers. “Opal, come see,” she said. The ribbon no longer dripped. It hung in easy folds, the purple even and dry to the touch. Opal stepped close and ran one fingertip along the edge; the fabric felt cool and smooth now, ready for whatever came next. Quinn clapped once, softly, and the three of them smiled at the finished ribbon and the firm handles resting on the table. Opal slipped the warm coin back into Nana Ruth’s hand with a grateful squeeze. The clay pieces would stay overnight to harden fully, and the ribbon could be coiled into a neat loop whenever they chose.

Opal washed their hands one final time in water that smelled faintly clean and cool, then dried them on the nubby towel again. They looked once more at the quiet table where slip and handles had been allowed to set without hurry, and at the ribbon that had finished dripping in its own time. Nana Ruth rested a light hand on Opal’s shoulder as the room settled into evening stillness. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Opal leaned against Nana Ruth and felt the calm of the finished work settle around them like a familiar blanket, ready for sleep whenever the day was done.
