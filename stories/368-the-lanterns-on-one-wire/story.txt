The wire of lanterns hung in a long gentle droop over the garden row, each small glass shade cool and smooth under the last peach light of day, the far hook empty so the whole line dipped toward the path like a soft frown of metal and glass. Zola stood close enough to see the tiny scratches on the wire and the way one lantern tilted lower than the rest, the evening air cool against their wrists as they traced the curve without lifting it. Dew had begun to gather on the rims and made them shine faintly. Two watering cans rested side by side on the packed earth, still beaded from earlier use, and a coil of garden string lay near their spouts. Zola kept their eyes on that sagging wire and felt how it waited for careful hands.

Zola stepped back one pace and drew in the sweet smell of lavender rising from the purple border that edged the garden row, the scent soft and clean in the cooling dusk. Maude waited beside them with the string ready in her palms, and Miss Dalca stood a little apart near the watering cans, her voice calm when she spoke. “We can raise them if everyone moves slowly,” Miss Dalca said. Zola answered, “The far hook needs a helper or the wire will stay low.” Maude nodded and added, “Then we split the work into two quiet jobs.” The lavender drifted around Zola’s shoulders while the last light warmed the glass shades just enough to glow pale gold.

Zola and Maude looked at the empty far hook and at the middle of the wire where it sagged deepest. Zola said, “I can walk the lanterns up one by one if you hold the far end steady.” Maude replied, “I will keep the hook high and still so nothing slips.” Miss Dalca only offered a name for the knot they would need later and said, “Call it the simple loop when the string meets the wire.” The air held a mild warmth near the soil even as the sky deepened, and Zola felt the grain of the path under their shoes while they planned the slow steps. No one rushed. The two watering cans sat like quiet markers of the row’s beginning.

Zola began at the near end, lifting each lantern only a thumb’s width at a time while Maude braced the distant hook with both hands so the wire rose evenly. The glass made a faint chiming sound when it touched its neighbor, and Zola listened carefully before moving again. Miss Dalca counted soft numbers when the string was ready, saying, “One loop, then through.” Zola threaded the garden string through the first ring with patient fingers and felt how the fibers were slightly rough yet kind. Maude held firm at her post, and the lanterns climbed higher without a single jerk. Cooler air brushed Zola’s neck as the light faded further.

Zola kept the pace gentle while they worked along the row, pausing after every third lantern so Maude could adjust her grip on the far hook. The wire no longer dipped toward the dirt. Miss Dalca stood near the watering cans and offered only the count when asked, her voice low. “Two more loops will hold it,” she said. Zola repeated the motion, sliding the string into place and drawing it snug without pulling hard. Maude called across, “It feels steady now.” A tin clock ticked once from the open porch window, its clear metallic note carrying across the quiet garden and reminding Zola that evening moved without hurry. They smiled at the sound and continued.

Zola reached the middle of the wire and felt the quilt stitch texture of the folded blanket someone had left on the nearby bench, its raised threads soft under their resting hand while they caught their breath. The blanket’s careful seams pressed lightly against Zola’s palm like tiny paths. Maude kept the far hook level, and together they raised the remaining lanterns until the entire line floated straight and true above the garden row. Miss Dalca named the final knot again so Zola could finish it. “Simple loop and tuck,” she murmured. Zola tucked the end and felt the wire settle into place with a small secure give. The watering cans reflected the new higher glow of the lanterns.

Zola and Maude stepped back side by side to look at the finished wire, the lanterns now hanging at even height and beginning to hold soft yellow light from within. Zola said, “We did the two jobs slowly and it stayed kind.” Maude answered, “Holding the far hook was easy when no one rushed.” Miss Dalca smiled but spoke no more than a quiet “Well done together.” Warm light from the lanterns touched Zola’s face and made the lavender border look silver for a moment. They watched a shadow on the path move the width of a thumb as one lantern swayed once and then grew still. The garden row felt peaceful under the new glow.

Zola helped coil the leftover string and set it beside the two watering cans while Maude released the far hook for the last time and joined them. The air had cooled enough that Zola rubbed their arms lightly and felt the pleasant night chill settle. All three walked toward the house steps where the porch light waited. Zola glanced back once at the straight wire of lanterns shining over the garden row and felt glad the work had been shared. Inside the doorway the rooms smelled faintly of supper long finished, and the floors were smooth under tired feet. Zola moved slowly down the short hall with Maude close by.

Zola climbed into the familiar bed near the window and pulled the covers up while Maude settled on the nearby chair for a short while and Miss Dalca dimmed the brighter lamps. The room held a gentle warmth from the day still stored in the walls. Zola listened to the quiet house and felt their breathing slow. Outside the garden lanterns continued their soft work above the row with its string and watering cans. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Zola closed their eyes and rested in that calm light, the day folded neatly behind them like the finished wire.
