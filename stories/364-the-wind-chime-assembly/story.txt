The tin clock on the shelf made a small steady tick, and Farah noticed it at once as the sound threaded through the quiet bedroom air. Warm lamplight pooled on the rug near Farah’s bare feet, and the air felt soft against their arms. Farah sat on the edge of the bed and listened a moment longer, smiling because the tick reminded them of the careful rhythm they had used that afternoon. Outside the window the sky held a pale peach glow that cooled the room without a draft. Farah rubbed the blanket’s edge between their fingers and thought of the harbor dock where baskets were passed up from the boats, and of the wind chime whose strings had needed one person to hold and one to knot. The memory settled gently, and Farah breathed in the calm of the house before letting the day return in order.

Down at the dock the planks had felt warm under Farah’s shoes, and the water slapped lightly against the pilings in a slow repeating hush. Farah stood beside Juno while Uncle Idris set a shallow box on a crate between them. Inside lay bright tubes, thin strings, and little discs that caught the late sun. Baskets rose from the boats in careful hands, and the air smelled clean and open. Farah touched one cool metal tube and heard it chime a soft note against another. “We should not hurry,” Farah said, looking from the strings to Juno’s open hands. Juno nodded, and Uncle Idris only rested his palms on his knees, ready to help with a word if they asked, nothing more.

Farah lifted the longest strings and held them straight so they hung even, feeling the slight pull as the breeze tried to twist them. Juno took the free ends and began the first knot with slow fingers. “You hold, and I will knot,” Juno said, voice quiet enough that the water sounds stayed clear. Farah kept their wrists steady and watched the loops form. Uncle Idris leaned a little closer and said, “That knot is called a double loop when both turns sit side by side.” Farah repeated the name under their breath and felt the dock’s warmth rise through the crate into their legs. The baskets continued their patient climb nearby, and no one called for speed.

Halfway through the second string Farah felt a small urge to finish faster so the chime could hang sooner, yet they remembered how uneven knots would make the tubes knock instead of sing. Farah loosened their grip just enough to keep the lines smooth and said, “Slow jobs feel better when we share them.” Juno smiled and tied the next turn without tugging. The metal tubes rested against Farah’s knee with a cool smooth touch, and sunlight flickered on the water in bright coins. Uncle Idris counted softly when they asked, “One, two, three,” so the loops matched. Farah’s shoulders stayed easy, and the dock felt wide and safe around them.

When a tin lid from an empty tea tin rolled gently against Farah’s shoe, Farah picked it up and set it aside on the crate, noticing its thin ridged texture under their thumb before returning both hands to the strings. Juno waited without fuss. “Ready for the middle disc,” Juno said. Farah guided the disc into place while Juno knotted below it, and the pair worked like two halves of one careful motion. Uncle Idris named the final knot once and then fell quiet again, offering only the calm of his presence. Farah smelled a faint drift of chamomile from a thermos Uncle Idris had opened for later, warm and mild in the salt air, and the scent made the work feel even gentler.

The last strings needed the same patience as the first. Farah held them high so they would not tangle with the finished ones, and Juno’s fingers moved in small sure circles. A gull called once far out over the water, then silence returned except for the soft clink of a finished tube. “We are almost there,” Farah said, and the words felt like a shared breath. Baskets still rose and settled at the far end of the dock, their woven sides whispering. Farah’s arms grew a little tired, yet the tiredness felt proud and light. Uncle Idris smiled and tapped the crate twice in a friendly rhythm that matched their careful pace.

Together they lifted the finished wind chime, and the tubes answered with a clear bright scatter of notes that made Farah laugh under their breath. Juno held one side and Farah the other while Uncle Idris found a hook on a post near the waiting boats. The three of them raised it until it hung free, and the light wind turned the discs so colors slid across the metal. Farah stood close enough to feel the faint vibration when a tube brushed another. No one had rushed, and nothing had gone wrong. The dock smelled of sun-warmed wood, and Farah’s hands still remembered the shape of the strings.

On the walk home the sky cooled to lavender, and Farah carried the empty box while Juno swung an empty basket by its handle. Uncle Idris walked between them and hummed a low tune without words. At the house Farah washed their hands in water that felt pleasantly cool, then found a warm heap of laundry near the chair. Farah matched two soft socks and set them neatly on the seat, the small action settling the last busy feeling of the day. Juno waved goodnight from the door, and Uncle Idris poured a little warm drink that tasted mild. Farah climbed the stairs with slow steps, listening to the familiar sounds of the settling house.

In the bedroom Farah slipped under the blanket and felt the sheets smooth and cool at first, then slowly warming. The tin clock kept its gentle tick from the shelf, steady as the knots they had tied. Farah closed their eyes and saw again the hanging chime turning above the dock, the baskets rising, and Juno’s careful hands working in time with their own. Uncle Idris’s quiet count still echoed kindly in Farah’s mind. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Farah breathed once more, long and easy, and let sleep arrive like a kind tide.
