The soft tick of a tin clock filled the quiet harbor shelter first, and Opal noticed it right away as they sat on the smooth wooden bench near the open doorway. Evening light slanted in pale gold across the floorboards, warm on Opal’s bare ankles, while the lee side of the wind kept the air still and kind instead of sharp. Opal breathed in and out with the steady ticks and felt the shelter settle around them like a familiar coat. Outside the water made only small slapping sounds against the pilings, far softer than any open-sea roar. Neighbor Sami crossed the room with quiet steps, and Opal watched the way Sami’s shadow stretched long and gentle across the planks. Opal’s fingers rested on the bench grain, feeling every ridge while the tin clock kept its calm measure.

Opal turned their head and saw the folded paper resting on the little side table where Sami had already smoothed its edges and set a smooth stone atop it so no stray breeze could lift it. The note that said only that someone saw you try lay neat and safe, its corners no longer curled. Opal’s chest felt warm at the sight. The shelter smelled faintly of dried rope and clean wood, and the temperature stayed mild enough that Opal did not need a heavier sweater. Sami had taken care of the note without being asked, tucking it where it would stay dry and easy to find later. Opal stood and walked closer, shoes making soft scrapes on the boards, and looked at the careful placement with open eyes.

“I saw what you did with the note,” Opal said, voice low so it would not startle the quiet. Sami glanced over and offered a small nod, hands still busy folding a length of spare canvas. The light from the doorway had turned softer blue now, painting cool shadows on the walls. Opal reached toward a nearby tin that held spare buttons and felt the cool ridged edge of its tin lid under their fingertips, the metal smooth and slightly chalky from salt air. That texture stayed with Opal as they thought about how thin a plain thank-you might sound after such steady care. The tin clock continued its gentle counting somewhere behind them, marking the peaceful minutes.

Opal decided then to do one useful thing Neighbor Sami had not asked for at all. Near the back bench sat a warm heap of clean socks and small towels that had been brought in from the drying line earlier, still holding the last of the afternoon heat. Opal knelt on the soft woven mat and began to match two socks from the warm heap, pairing the blue ones with the tiny white stripes and setting them neatly aside. The wool felt springy and dry against Opal’s palms. A kettle on the little stove released a thin ribbon of steam that carried the smell of chamomile into the room, gentle and apple-sweet. Opal smiled at the scent while their hands kept working, lining up another pair so the heap grew tidy instead of tangled.

Sami noticed the neat pairs and paused. “You did not have to sort those,” Sami said, tone easy and surprised. Opal kept their eyes on the socks a moment longer, feeling the last bit of warmth leave the wool as the air cooled. The harbor shelter stayed peaceful on its lee side, the wind sliding harmlessly over the roof while lantern light began to pool in the corners. Opal finished the second pair and stacked them with care so they would be ready for whoever needed dry feet later. The chamomile scent drifted thicker for a breath, mixing with the faint salt that always lived near the water. Opal stood slowly, knees leaving two pale circles on the mat, and faced Sami fully.

“I noticed you took care of the note first,” Opal said plainly, hands resting at their sides. “It looked important, and you made sure it stayed safe.” Sami’s face softened in the low light. The tin clock ticked once more, clear and round, while Opal waited. Outside a gull called once and then fell silent, leaving only the mild hush of protected water. Opal felt the cool evening air brush their cheeks through the doorway yet never turn sharp. The matched socks sat in a small proud pile, and the note remained exactly where Sami had settled it, stone and all.

Dina appeared in the doorway with a basket of fresh bread rounds, her steps light on the threshold boards. “The shelter feels nice tonight,” Dina said, setting the basket down so the warm crust smell joined the chamomile. Opal waved her closer and showed the tidy socks without fuss. The three of them stood together for a quiet stretch while the lanterns along the wall threw steady circles of gold across the floor. Opal’s shoulders loosened. The tin lid still rested cool in memory on Opal’s fingertips, a small solid fact among softer ones. Sami poured a little of the chamomile steam into three cups, and the sound of liquid meeting ceramic was soft as rain on canvas.

Opal carried one cup to the bench by the window and sat again, letting the heat of the mug soak into both palms. The bread tasted mild and good when they tore a small piece. Neighbor Sami settled nearby with their own cup, and Dina curled on the opposite bench with hers. No one hurried. The lee side of the wind kept every edge of the evening smooth, and the harbor water reflected the first early stars like scattered coins. Opal glanced once more at the note held safe under its stone and felt a quiet gladness settle deep. The tin clock marked the slow passing of minutes without ever growing loud. Opal’s eyelids grew heavier while the chamomile warmth rose with each careful sip.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Opal rested their head against the bench back and listened to the last kind ticks of the tin clock while the shelter held its calm temperature around them all. Sami’s breathing slowed. Dina’s basket lid stayed half open, bread scent fading gently into the night air. Opal’s matched socks waited in their neat row, and the note that someone saw you try remained exactly as Sami had arranged it, safe and seen. The water outside made its smallest possible sounds against the pilings. Opal closed their eyes fully and let the mild light and the quiet room carry them toward sleep, every corner of the harbor shelter peaceful on the lee side of the wind.
