Soft evening light brushed the workshop window and warmed the wooden bench where little jars of glue and stacks of paper labels waited in tidy rows. Paz walked in from the mild dusk and felt the gentle change from outdoor coolness to the room’s calm indoor warmth on their cheeks and hands. The air held a quiet stillness that settled over the tools and scrap wood. Paz’s fingers brushed the outside of their pocket and found the stiff, folded edge of the map they had carried all afternoon. Something about that corner felt wrong, bent out of true, and Paz paused near the bench, listening to the low creak of the old floorboards under their sneakers.

Paz stood close to the bench and almost tucked the map deeper out of sight behind a box of labels, the way a person might hide a small mistake when no one was looking. The pocket fabric rubbed against their palm with a dry whisper. Uncle Idris looked up from sorting thin strips of wood and smiled without hurry. “Hello, Paz,” he said in his easy voice. “I saved you a seat right here if you want to help with the labels.” Paz felt the bent corner again and hesitated, the decision sitting heavy and light at the same time in their chest. The lamp above the bench cast a steady golden pool across the grain of the wood.

Paz drew out the map slowly and laid it flat so the bent corner showed clear under the light. “Uncle Idris, I bent the corner while it was in my pocket,” Paz said. “I almost put it away without telling you.” The words came out plain and soft. Uncle Idris nodded once and rested a gentle hand on the edge of the bench. “Thank you for saying it straight, Paz,” he answered. “We can mend what can be mended.” A faint, comforting smell of tomato soup drifted from the kitchen doorway and mixed with the clean scent of wood shavings. Paz let out a quiet breath and felt their shoulders loosen.

Together they smoothed the map across the center of the bench, careful not to tear the thin paper. Paz held one side steady while Uncle Idris warmed a small dab of glue between his fingers until it turned clear and mild. “You hold the corner just so, Paz,” he said, “and I will press from the other side.” Paz’s fingertips met the paper’s cool surface and felt every tiny ridge of the printed lines. The workshop stayed peaceful around them. Outside the window the last pale sky faded into deeper blue, and the bench itself felt solid and kind under their elbows.

Paz watched the glue set in a thin, even line while Uncle Idris fetched a clean cloth from a nearby shelf. The cloth had a soft fold across its middle, and Paz reached over to smooth one wrinkle out of the cloth before they used it. The motion felt small and good. Uncle Idris pressed the cloth lightly over the mend and counted quietly under his breath. “Almost ready,” he murmured. Paz listened to the soft count and kept both hands still. A moment later a clear, tiny sound came from the open doorway—the single ring of a cat’s collar bell as the household cat wandered past and then settled somewhere out of sight.

When the glue held firm, Paz and Uncle Idris lifted the map together and checked the corner under the lamp. The bend was nearly gone, only a faint crease left like a quiet memory of the pocket. “It looks better now,” Paz said, and their voice carried a warm note of relief. Uncle Idris agreed with a small nod. “We did good work, Paz.” On the far end of the bench sat an old basket with a wicker handle that felt dry and woven under Paz’s curious fingers when they rested a hand there for a second. The texture was familiar and steady, like something made to be held.

Paz’s friend Sami had stopped by earlier with a borrowed pencil and left it near the labels, so Paz slid the pencil back into its place and thought of how Sami would like seeing the mended map. Uncle Idris poured a little more glue into a shallow dish for tomorrow’s projects. The dish made a soft clink against the wood. Paz wiped a speck of dried glue from the bench edge with the cloth and felt the surface grow smooth again under the cloth’s pressure. Everything around them stayed quiet and ordinary in the best way.

Uncle Idris reached for two clean labels and wrote the word “map” on each in careful letters so they would know which drawer held the finished piece. Paz helped stick one label on a flat wooden tray and pressed the edges down until they lay flat. “Shall we put it away until morning?” Uncle Idris asked. Paz answered, “Yes, please,” and together they slid the map into the tray. The paper made the softest rustle as it settled. Paz noticed how the glue jars caught little sparks of light and how the whole bench smelled faintly of paper and wood now that the soup scent had faded.

Paz and Uncle Idris stood side by side for one more calm minute, looking at the neat row of tools and the mended corner resting safe in its tray. The room felt warmer than when Paz first arrived, and the quiet settled deeper around their shoulders. Paz touched the edge of the bench once more and felt glad the truth had been easy to say. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
