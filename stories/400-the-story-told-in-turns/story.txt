A soft drip-drip sounded from the kitchen tap in the corner of the cottage room. Jorie noticed the small steady sound at once and tilted their head, listening while the pale gold light of late day slipped through the paper stars strung overhead and laid quiet patterns across the long tablecloth. The air felt pleasantly cool against Jorie’s bare arms as they slid onto the bench. Eitan settled beside them with a gentle thump of cushions. Jorie said, “I hear the drips, Eitan. Shall we count them later without looking?” The baker wiped floury hands on an apron and smiled from the counter, filling the space with a calm presence that made the room feel held.

Jorie reached for a clean linen napkin resting near the plates and rubbed its soft woven texture between their fingers while the paper stars turned slowly in a draft. The long tablecloth stretched smooth and white beneath their elbows, smelling faintly of soap and fresh bread. Eitan watched the stars sway and whispered that the whole room looked ready for something gentle. Jorie nodded and kept stroking the napkin, enjoying how the threads felt cool and even. The baker set a bowl of fruit on the table, and the quiet light grew warmer as clouds shifted outside the cottage window.

The baker peeled an orange with careful fingers, and the bright smell of orange peel rose into the air around the long tablecloth and paper stars. Jorie breathed it in deeply and felt their shoulders loosen. Eitan leaned closer and said the scent made everything sweeter. Jorie agreed and suggested they invent a story together right there at the table, taking turns of two sentences each so the tale would stay neat. The baker only nodded and kept peeling, letting the children lead while the orange scent drifted softly through the cottage room.

Jorie began the story in a slow voice, speaking two careful sentences about a small boat that floated on a quiet pond under paper-star skies. Eitan answered with two more sentences that added a friendly duck paddling nearby, but when Eitan spoke a little too quickly the boat and the duck bumped into each other in a muddle of words. Jorie felt the cool air again and said, “If we rush, the story goes wrong. Let’s split it into two slow jobs.” Eitan nodded at once. The tin clock on the shelf gave one clear tick that marked the pause, and both children listened until the sound faded.

Jorie took the first slow job of choosing every opening phrase and saying it with long soft breaths while the paper stars cast speckled light over the long tablecloth. Eitan took the second slow job of answering with matching calm sentences that never hurried ahead. They practiced once in whispers, feeling the steady temperature of the room wrap around them like a blanket. The baker watched without interrupting and only offered a quiet count when they needed to know how many sentences had passed. Jorie smiled at the help and kept their part unhurried.

Halfway through the tale the story needed a gentle joining place, a knot that would hold the boat and the duck together in friendship. Jorie looked toward the baker and asked what they might call that knot. The baker answered softly, “Name it the Friendship Loop, and it will stay kind.” Eitan repeated the name once, and Jorie wove the Friendship Loop into their next two sentences so the boat and duck rested side by side on the pond. The light from the paper stars seemed brighter for a moment, and the long tablecloth stayed smooth under their hands.

They continued the slow jobs without any hurry, Jorie offering two measured sentences about evening coming to the pond and Eitan answering with two more about the duck tucking its head under a wing. The baker counted softly to twelve when they paused, matching the earlier idea of drips, and Jorie closed their eyes to listen without peeking at the tap. Cool air drifted in again, carrying the last faint trace of fruit, and both children felt proud of how steady their turns had become. No words tumbled or crashed; every sentence settled like a feather.

At last the story reached its quiet close while the paper stars hung motionless above the long tablecloth. Jorie spoke the final two sentences about the boat rocking gently to sleep, and Eitan finished with two sentences that let the duck dream of warm bread. The baker set down a plate of orange slices and said only, “Well told together.” Jorie touched the linen napkin one more time for its familiar softness, then shared the fruit with Eitan. The whole cottage room felt settled and complete as the children leaned back against the bench.

Jorie and Eitan rested side by side, listening to the last soft sounds of the cottage while the baker cleared the bowls with careful hands. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Warm light pooled across the long tablecloth and the paper stars, and Jorie felt the peaceful temperature of the room settle over them like a shared quilt as their eyelids grew heavy together.
