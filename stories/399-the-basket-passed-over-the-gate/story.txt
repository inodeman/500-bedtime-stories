The sweet smell of pear skins rose from an open crate as Delia’s busy hands pressed soft folded cloths deeper into the wide woven basket on the harbor dock. Warm evening light slid across the planks and turned the water beyond the pilings the color of honey. Delia’s fingers moved carefully, settling each jar so nothing would rattle, and the wood under their knees felt smooth and slightly damp from the tide. Ilaria sat close by, sorting smaller parcels, while Uncle Idris checked the ropes that looped from the dock toward the low green gate that stood between the loading spot and the waiting cart. Delia breathed in the fruity scent again and smiled because the work felt calm and steady in their palms.

Delia looked up at the gate and saw that it was only waist-high, painted the soft blue of early morning, and it did not need any climbing at all. “We can just lift the basket over together,” Delia said, voice quiet so the words stayed gentle. The air carried a cool breath from the water that brushed Delia’s cheeks and made the lantern light farther down the pier flicker. Ilaria nodded and touched the basket handle with careful fingers. Uncle Idris rested one hand on the gate post and watched without hurry. Delia felt the salt air settle on their arms like a light shawl while they kept arranging the last cloth, making certain every corner lay flat.

Ilaria spoke next, soft as the water lapping the pilings. “If we both grab the same side too fast, it will tip,” Ilaria said. Delia agreed at once and felt the warm evening settle deeper into the boards. The basket sat heavy but balanced, filled with jars and cloths that smelled faintly of fruit still, and Delia’s hands stayed on the rim so nothing shifted. A gull called once far out and then grew quiet. Uncle Idris only smiled and waited. Delia noticed how the light turned the rope coils the color of toasted bread and how the dock itself held a quiet heat that rose through their bare feet.

They decided together that rushing would spill the jars, so Delia and Ilaria split the work into two slow jobs. Delia would steady the near side of the basket and guide it upward while Ilaria would receive it on the cart side of the gate and lower it gently. The plan felt right in Delia’s chest like a held breath released. Cool mist drifted low over the water and touched Delia’s ankles with a soft chill that made them work even more carefully. Uncle Idris stayed near the gate hinge, ready if they needed a word. Delia’s fingers found the woven grip again and felt every strand under the pads of their hands.

“What knot do we need for the short rope that keeps the lid snug?” Delia asked, looking toward Uncle Idris. He answered in a low voice that matched the hush of the harbor. “Call it the double loop, and count three slow tugs,” Uncle Idris said. Delia repeated the name once under their breath and felt the evening air turn a shade cooler against their neck. Ilaria already held the short rope ready. The boards creaked softly when Delia shifted weight, and the sound was friendly rather than sharp. Delia kept both hands on the basket rim so the jars inside stayed silent and still.

Delia began the first slow job while Ilaria waited on the far side of the blue gate. Together they lifted only a little at a time, and Delia felt the weight settle evenly across both sets of hands. The wind in the fig leaves rustled from the tree that grew near the warehouse wall, a dry gentle whisper that seemed to approve of their patience. Warm light from the low sun still painted the gate posts gold. Delia breathed steadily and did not hurry the rise of the basket. Ilaria’s fingers met the far handles the moment they cleared the top rail, and nothing tipped or scraped.

“One more gentle lift and then the count,” Delia said. Ilaria answered, “I am ready when you are.” The short rope lay soft in Delia’s free hand, and they remembered the double loop while Uncle Idris watched with quiet eyes. Delia’s knuckles brushed the warm coin that Uncle Idris had earlier pressed into their pocket for luck; the metal felt smooth and comforting against the cloth. They finished the three slow tugs together, each tug measured so the lid stayed snug without pulling the basket off balance. The dock smelled of clean water and old wood, and the temperature of the air stayed mild against Delia’s arms.

With the basket safely over the gate and resting on the cart, Delia and Ilaria stepped back and shared a small tired smile. Uncle Idris named the knot once more just to seal the moment, then helped them tuck the rope ends neatly. Delia felt the last of the day’s warmth leave the planks as evening settled fully, and the water below sounded like quiet breathing. Ilaria brushed a stray fig leaf from the cart rail. Delia’s hands, no longer busy with jars, rested open at their sides and felt light. Everything they had carried now sat steady and ready for the morning road, and the three of them stood close without needing any more words.

Later, back in the small room above the harbor path, Delia sat on the edge of the bed while the soft night air drifted through the open shutters. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Delia’s toes found the folded corner of the rug and straightened it with one slow push so the floor felt even under bare feet. Outside the fig leaves had gone still, and the pear-skin scent had faded into memory. Uncle Idris hummed from the next room, and Ilaria’s quiet footsteps moved down the hall. Delia pulled the blanket up and felt its familiar weight settle across their shoulders like a kind hand, ready for sleep.
