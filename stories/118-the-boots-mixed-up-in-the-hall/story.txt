The boots sat in a damp tangle near the wall, toes pointing every way at once, one yellow cuff folded inside a dark rubber heel while a smaller pair leaned crookedly against a taller pair, their wet laces knotted together like soft vines after the rain. Cool water beaded on the scuffed toes and slipped slowly onto the mat, making tiny dark spots that spread like quiet ink. The hall smelled faintly of wet rubber and old wood, and the light from the glass door fell in pale stripes across the jumble, showing every mud freckle and every twisted strap. Kavi crouched low so the camera of their eyes could study the mix-up without hurry, fingers hovering just above a floppy tongue that felt clammy in the air. From the rainy steps outside came the soft drum of leftover drops sliding off the overhang, a steady, gentle rhythm that matched the drip from the boots themselves.

Mr. Ade stood by the library return desk with an armload of picture books pressed to his coat, peering down the hall at the scattered boots as if they had become a puzzle too wide for one person. The desk lamp cast a warm circle on the polished counter, and the air inside felt a little warmer than the chill that drifted in whenever the door opened. Kavi watched Mr. Ade shift his weight, books sliding, one elbow trying to keep a tall volume from escaping while he reached toward the nearest boot with the free hand. Rain still ticked against the steps outside, a light silver sound that made the whole entrance feel soft and sleepy. Kavi’s own socks were dry and thick, and they curled their toes against the floorboards that felt smooth and cool underfoot.

Kavi walked closer without calling out, noticing how Mr. Ade’s shoulders stayed hunched as he tried to keep the books safe and still untangle the mess. A thin ribbon of cocoa drifted from a mug left on the corner of the return desk, rich and sweet, curling into the quiet air like a friendly hello. Kavi breathed it in and felt the warmth settle behind their ribs. “I can hold those books for a minute,” Kavi said, voice low so it would not bounce too hard off the shelves. Mr. Ade turned, surprise gentle in his face, and the books tilted again. “That would help more than you know,” he answered, and the words floated soft between them while rain continued its quiet tapping on the steps beyond the glass.

Kavi took the stack carefully, feeling the paper edges press lightly against their palms, and set the books in a neat pile on the return desk where the lamp light made the covers glow like little windows. Then Kavi knelt again beside the mixed boots and began to loosen the knotted laces one slow pull at a time. The laces were stiff with damp, and each tug released a tiny squeak of wet string. Mr. Ade knelt too, his knees making a soft sound on the mat, and together they sorted the pairs by size and color without speaking for a stretch of calm breaths. Outside, the rainy steps shone under the porch light, silver and clean, and the hall air cooled a degree when another drop of night air slipped under the door.

“These two match, I think,” Kavi said, lifting a pair of brown boots that still held the shape of someone’s feet. Mr. Ade nodded and set them upright against the wall so water could drain downward in thin clear lines. The wool sleeve of Mr. Ade’s coat brushed Kavi’s wrist as he reached for the next pair, and the sleeve felt soft and slightly fuzzy, like a gentle cloud against bare skin. Kavi smiled at the texture and kept working, lining up heels and straightening cuffs until the tangle became two neat rows. A cat’s collar bell chimed once from somewhere deeper in the library stacks, a bright tiny note that rang and then faded into the hush of pages and carpet.

Kavi stood and brushed a speck of mud from their own knee, then carried one last pair of small boots over to the mat by the rainy steps so they could dry facing the cool night air. Mr. Ade rose more slowly, joints quiet, and rested a hand on the return desk as if the wood itself offered steady company. “You made the whole job lighter,” Mr. Ade said, and Kavi only nodded, already turning toward the books again so the praise would not need an answer. The desk felt solid under Kavi’s fingers when they slid the returned volumes into the slot one by one, listening to the soft thunk each book made as it landed inside the bin. Warm lamp light painted Kavi’s hands gold while the rainy steps outside grew quieter, the last drops slowing to a hush.

They walked a few steps deeper into the library where a reading chair waited near a low shelf of sleepy stories, and Kavi sat for a moment, rocking once in the chair and letting it stop by itself with a gentle creak that settled into silence. The chair cushions held a faint warmth from earlier visitors, and Kavi’s back sank into that leftover heat like a blanket that already knew their shape. Mr. Ade sorted the last bootlaces into loops and stood them straight, then joined Kavi with a quiet smile that needed no big words. Far windows showed the night turning deeper blue, and the library lights glowed low and honey-colored across the carpet that felt plush under Kavi’s socks.

Kavi rose again and helped Mr. Ade gather the dry coats from the hooks so no one would leave anything behind when the evening ended. “Shall we put the yellow pair by the door?” Kavi asked, and Mr. Ade replied, “Yes, right where the mat meets the light.” Their voices stayed small and kind, matching the hush of the tall shelves. The air near the windows carried a cooler breath now, touching Kavi’s cheeks like the softest cloth, and the smell of old paper mixed gently with the memory of cocoa still lingering near the desk. Everything felt finished and peaceful, the boots standing ready in their places like friends waiting for morning.

Later, when Kavi rested under a quilt at home with the day folded carefully away, the room held the same calm that had lived in the library hall. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside, night rain had become a distant memory of silver steps, and inside, the air felt still and warm against Kavi’s closed eyes while the quiet of sorted boots and shared work drifted through their thoughts like a slow, kind river carrying them toward sleep.
