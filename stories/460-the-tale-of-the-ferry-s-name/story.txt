The mild evening air drifted through the open window of the bedroom and rested cool against the curtains, making them sway like slow wings. Eko sat cross-legged on the bed in the gentle pool of lamp light, their pajamas soft against their skin and their hands resting on the quilt that still held a bit of daytime warmth. Auntie Luz lowered herself into the wooden chair beside the bed, her voice already quiet as if the room itself had asked for softness. The story had not begun, yet Eko felt a bright rush of bigger news waiting behind their teeth, something they wanted to spill right away. Instead they watched Auntie Luz set a small plate nearby and breathe in the faint sweetness rising from it. Outside the last pale light of dusk faded, and inside the lamp painted everything gold.

Auntie Luz unfolded a linen napkin with a careful rustle and laid it across her knee so the plate would not slip. “Tonight I will tell you the tale of how the ferry got its name,” she said, and the words floated out low and steady. Eko’s eyes widened; the bigger news pressed forward again, but they pressed their lips together and let the first part of the story settle. The napkin felt smooth and cool under Auntie Luz’s fingers as she smoothed one corner. A soft orange-peel smell drifted up from the little plate where thin curls of rind rested beside two bright segments. Eko noticed the scent and almost spoke, yet they only shifted their weight so the mattress sighed gently under them. The lamp’s circle of light stayed calm on the wall.

“Long ago the ferry was only a plain wooden boat with no name at all,” Auntie Luz continued, her voice matching the hush of the room. “It carried families and baskets and sleepy dogs across the wide quiet water every morning and every evening.” Eko imagined the boat and the water and wanted to leap in with their own news, yet they kept listening while the orange-peel smell lingered just a little longer near the plate. The bedroom air felt pleasantly cool on Eko’s cheeks as they leaned forward. Auntie Luz paused to let the picture grow, and Eko heard their own breathing slow to match hers. The quilt under Eko’s legs held a cozy texture of worn cotton that invited stillness.

“One clear afternoon a child left a small silver bell on a seat,” Auntie Luz said, “and when the ferry crossed the water the bell rang once, soft as a thought. The captain smiled and said the sound felt like the boat itself was humming. After that people began to call it the Humming Ferry, and the name stayed because it fit the gentle way the boat moved.” Eko’s shoulders loosened; the urge to interrupt faded a little more with each new sentence. Somewhere on the shelf a tin clock gave a single quiet tick that marked the passing second without hurry. The sound threaded through the story like a tiny heartbeat. Warm lamp light rested on Auntie Luz’s hands as she spoke, and Eko watched those hands rest open on the linen napkin.

The tale went on while Eko stayed close and quiet. “Years later the same ferry carried a whole picnic of neighbors who sang soft songs all the way across,” Auntie Luz told them. “The songs mixed with the water sounds until everyone agreed the boat had earned its name twice over. It still answers to Humming Ferry, and every passenger feels a little calmer just hearing it.” Eko’s fingers traced the quilt pattern, feeling the raised seams, and they almost forgot the bigger news for a stretch of time. The air near the window carried a cooler thread now, touching Eko’s ankles where the blanket had slipped. Auntie Luz’s voice never rose; it simply carried the next piece forward like the ferry itself.

“Even now the ferryman rings that same small bell once at the middle of the crossing,” she said, “just to remember how the name began.” Eko nodded without speaking, their eyes steady on Auntie Luz’s face in the steady lamp glow. The bigger news still waited, bright and important, yet Eko chose to hold it a little longer so none of the story would be missed. The bedroom smelled clean and faintly sweet from the earlier orange peel, though the scent had grown softer. Temperature stayed mild, neither warm nor cold, wrapping the whole room in comfort. Auntie Luz smiled as if she could see Eko listening with their whole self.

“And that is how the ferry received the name it still carries today, a name made of one quiet bell and many kind crossings,” Auntie Luz finished, her last sentence landing gently. Eko waited until the final word faded completely into the quiet. Only then did they draw a slow breath and ask one real question. “Did the child who left the bell ever ride the ferry again?” Eko said, their voice soft as the lamp light. Auntie Luz answered at once, “Yes, many times, and each time the bell rang they smiled like someone greeting an old friend.” The tin clock ticked once more in the pause that followed, a small clear sound that belonged only to this room.

Eko felt the bigger news settle into something smaller and easier to share. “I found a smooth white stone by the garden path today,” they said, “and I put it on the windowsill so it can watch the moon with me.” Auntie Luz reached over and squeezed Eko’s hand, the skin of her palm warm against theirs. “That sounds like a perfect stone for moon-watching,” she replied. Eko smiled and let their shoulders drop fully into the pillows. The air had grown a touch cooler near the open window, brushing Eko’s hair like a good-night touch. Everything in the bedroom felt unhurried and safe.

Eko wriggled lower under the quilt until only their face showed in the lamp’s circle, the fabric cool at first then warming quickly against their arms. Auntie Luz turned the lamp down one gentle click so the light became a softer gold. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the night stayed mild and still, and inside the quiet settled around Eko like another blanket while their breathing slowed toward sleep.
