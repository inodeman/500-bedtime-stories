"Idris, here is the joke you told me yesterday about the cat who borrowed the moon's socks, and I remembered every word so I could hand it straight back to you tonight," Grandma Nia said, her voice low and even while the pale hall light spilled across the bedroom floor in a gentle stripe.

Idris sat up against the pillows and felt the soft quilt settle around their legs as Grandma Nia repeated the whole silly joke, pausing just where the punch line waited, and the warm smell of bread still lingered from the kitchen even though supper had ended hours before. Idris listened with a small smile growing, because the joke felt whole again, and Grandma Nia's hands rested quiet on the edge of the bed while the hall light kept the corners from going fully dark. They asked her to say the middle part one more time, just to hear the cat stretch and yawn in the words, and Grandma Nia did so without hurry. Outside the open window the night air moved slow and mild. Idris tucked a corner of the quilt under their chin and thought how carefully she must have held onto those words all day.

Grandma Nia finished the joke and laughed once, a short happy sound that matched the quiet room, and Idris noticed right then that she had already taken care of the joke remembered and handed back today without being asked. The air near the bed felt pleasantly cool against Idris's bare arms as they sat thinking. They watched Grandma Nia smooth the blanket once more and realized the joke had come back complete, not missing a single sock or moonbeam. Idris wanted to say something right away yet felt the ordinary thank-you would sit too lightly after such careful remembering. They looked at the empty bowl still resting on the little table beside the bed, the one that had held warm milk earlier, and decided a better answer could be something useful she had never requested.

Idris swung their legs over the side of the bed and stood on the rug that felt thick under their feet, then picked up the bowl with both hands while Grandma Nia watched from the chair without speaking. The cool ceramic rim pressed smooth and solid against Idris's fingers as they carried the empty bowl carefully out of the bedroom, down the short hall where the light still glowed, and into the kitchen. They set the bowl in the sink, rinsed it with a quiet trickle of water so it would be ready for morning, and dried their hands on the soft towel hanging nearby. Then Idris walked back the same way, feet quiet on the floorboards, and returned to the bedroom where Grandma Nia waited with the same calm face. The hall light followed them partway through the doorway like a friendly companion.

Back beside the bed Idris climbed under the quilt again and heard the soft rush of wind in the fig leaves outside the window, a gentle brushing sound that came and went like slow breathing. Grandma Nia reached over and adjusted the pillow once so Idris's head rested just right. Idris looked at her and felt the day settling into place around them both. The joke sat safe between them now, returned whole, and the small trip with the bowl had given Idris a way to answer without using thin words. They breathed in and out and watched the way the hall light made a warm path across the floorboards. Grandma Nia hummed a tiny scrap of tune that barely rose above the night sounds.

"I noticed you remembered the whole joke and brought it back to me today," Idris said plainly, looking straight at Grandma Nia while the quilt held its gentle weight across their lap. Grandma Nia smiled and answered, "I kept it safe because it made you laugh yesterday, and I wanted you to have it again." Idris nodded and added, "I carried the bowl to the sink so it would be clean for you in the morning." Grandma Nia said, "That was kind, Idris, and I see it," her voice matching the quiet of the room. The two of them sat without rush while the leaves continued their soft motion beyond the glass. Idris felt the day fold itself neatly away.

Idris leaned their head against the pillow and listened to the faint creak of the house settling, a sound so small it almost hid inside the quieter night. Grandma Nia stayed in the chair a little longer, her presence steady and warm like a lamp left on purpose. They talked a bit more about the cat in the joke and how the moon's socks might have felt fuzzy and too big, inventing no new worries, only the same gentle silliness. Idris's eyelids grew heavier yet they kept the conversation going in short soft sentences until the words themselves began to slow. The temperature in the room stayed mild and even, neither too warm nor chilly. Grandma Nia reached out once more and brushed a stray lock of hair from Idris's forehead with the lightest touch.

Idris let their eyes close most of the way while still hearing Grandma Nia's quiet breathing nearby and the distant hush of the neighborhood settling into sleep. They thought again of the joke that had come home safely and of the bowl now resting clean in the sink, both things held carefully between them. The hall light continued its soft spill across the threshold, never bright enough to keep anyone awake, only bright enough to show the way if needed. Idris shifted once under the quilt and felt the fabric smooth against their cheek. Grandma Nia spoke one last low sentence about tomorrow's breakfast and how the bread might taste even better then. Idris answered with a sleepy sound that meant yes and agreement and comfort all at once.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Idris drifted toward sleep with Grandma Nia's chair still nearby and the hall light keeping its calm watch, the remembered joke tucked safely inside the quiet room like a small bright coin no one would lose again.
