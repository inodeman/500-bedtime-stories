In the quiet corner stall that felt like a little room along the market lane, a soft tick reached Greta first. Greta turned their head and noticed the small sound coming from somewhere nearby while warm stripes of late light lay across the wooden boards. The air felt pleasantly cool on their arms as they stood still and listened. Tall baskets of fruit sat in rows, and glass jars gleamed beside clay flower pots filled with green leaves. Greta took a calm step forward, feeling the smooth floor under their shoes, and watched dust motes drift in the golden glow. They liked how the stall held its own gentle hush even with the lane just outside. Grandma Nia sat at a low table with papers open wide, and Greta moved nearer without hurry.

Greta breathed in the fresh scent of basil rising from the leafy pots clustered by the table edge. The green smell mixed with the mild warmth of the afternoon and made the stall feel friendly and alive. They looked at the long sheet of paper Grandma Nia was filling with careful loops of ink. A cup of water rested near her elbow, yet every time she reached for her pen the cup wobbled a little on the crowded surface. Greta saw the jars of honey and the baskets of folded cloths pressed close around the writing space. Flower pots lined the open side of the stall so the lane outside looked like a garden path. Greta rested one hand on the table edge and felt how solid the wood stayed under their palm.

“This letter is growing longer than I planned,” Grandma Nia said softly while dipping her pen again. Greta watched the cup of water sit too near the moving papers and noticed how awkward it was for one person to keep everything steady. The cool ceramic of a nearby jar touched Greta’s wrist when they leaned in. Light from the open lane painted soft patterns on the letter pages. Greta stayed close and quiet, listening to the gentle scratch of the pen. They counted the flower pots again and saw one held a smooth round stone among the soil. The market sounds outside stayed low and distant, like a background hum that never rushed them.

Greta noticed Grandma Nia trying to hold the paper flat with one hand while reaching for the cup with the other. Without a word Greta slid the cup a safer distance away and kept one careful finger on its side so it would not tip. The polished pebble that had rested in the flower pot felt cool and glassy when Greta picked it up and set it gently on the corner of the letter to keep the page from curling. Warm light still poured through the stall opening and made the pebble shine. Greta stood beside the table and watched the ink dry in neat lines. They felt proud inside yet said nothing about it, simply staying ready to help again if needed.

“Thank you for catching that, Greta,” Grandma Nia said with a small smile as she kept writing. Greta answered, “I can hold the cup while you finish the next part.” The tin clock on the shelf gave one clear bright chime that floated through the stall like a friendly bell. Greta listened to the single note fade and then held the cup of water steady so Grandma Nia could sip between sentences. The water felt cool through the cup wall against Greta’s fingers. Outside the lane the baskets and jars waited in peaceful rows under the soft sky. Greta kept their shoulders relaxed and their breathing slow while the letter grew longer still.

Suri appeared at the open side of the stall carrying a small cloth bundle and spoke in a gentle voice. “May I sit with you both for a minute?” Greta nodded and made room on the bench so Suri could join them. The wooden seat felt smooth and slightly warm from the afternoon light. Greta kept one hand near the cup of water and the other ready to smooth the letter paper whenever a corner lifted. Grandma Nia wrote another full page while the three of them stayed quiet and easy together. The smell of the market lane drifted in mildly, and Greta felt glad the cup no longer threatened the ink. They glanced at Suri and shared a calm look that needed no extra words.

“Would you like me to fold these cloths while the letter rests?” Greta asked after Grandma Nia set her pen down for a moment. Grandma Nia replied, “That would be lovely, and please turn the stitched pears so they face upward.” Greta lifted the square napkins from a nearby basket and folded each one so the little embroidered pear showed clearly on top. The fabric felt soft and lightly textured under their careful fingers. Warm light from the lane made the yellow thread of the pears glow. Suri watched and smiled without speaking. Greta stacked the neat napkins beside the jars and felt the quiet pleasure of the small useful task. The cup of water stayed safe in its new spot, and the long letter lay flat and finished at last.

Greta helped gather the pages into a tidy stack while the air in the stall grew cooler as evening came closer. They ran one fingertip along the edge of a flower pot and felt the slight grit of dried soil there. Grandma Nia sealed the letter with a slow press of her hand. Greta carried the cup of water back to a clear corner of the table so nothing could bump it. Soft shadows stretched longer across the baskets and jars outside. Suri hummed a tiny tune that matched the peaceful mood. Greta sat back on the bench and let their hands rest open in their lap, listening to the quiet lane and feeling how steady everything had become after the helpful sharing of the work.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Greta watched the gentle light settle over the folded napkins and the finished letter while the last cool air of day brushed their cheeks. Grandma Nia and Suri sat nearby in the same calm hush, and Greta felt the smooth wood of the bench beneath them as the market lane grew still and kind.
