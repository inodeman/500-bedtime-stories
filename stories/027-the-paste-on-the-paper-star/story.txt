"Idris, leave the paper star under its cloth a little longer," the ferryman said in a low voice that matched the hush of the room.

Idris stood close to the cottage windowsill crowded with glass jars of buttons, shallow trays of colored paper scraps, and soft loops of drying wool that still held the afternoon warmth. The paper star rested in the middle tray, its paste still shining wet and sticky in the honey-colored light that slipped through the open pane. Idris could hear the wind in the fig leaves outside, a gentle rustling that made the wool stir just a little. They wanted so much to lift the corner of the cloth and touch one pointed tip to see if it had begun to stiffen. The air near the sill felt cooler than the rest of the kitchen, and Idris rested one finger against a cool ceramic rim of an empty bowl while they thought.

Bodhi sat on a low stool nearby, humming while sorting tiny shells into another jar. "You can wait with me, Idris," Bodhi offered, their voice calm as the slow drift of dust motes. Idris glanced at the ferryman, who stood by the table wiping his hands on a cloth that smelled faintly of river water and soap. The ferryman nodded once, encouraging, and the warm bread cooling on the counter sent its yeasty scent drifting toward the window. Idris pulled their hand back from the cloth covering the star. The paste still gleamed under the thin fabric like a little moon, and Idris knew it was not ready yet.

"I will leave it covered," Idris said at last, turning toward the empty bowl. The ferryman smiled and stepped aside so the path to the sink stayed clear. Idris lifted the bowl carefully; its cool ceramic rim pressed smooth and solid against their palms. They carried it across the wooden floor that felt faintly warm under bare feet from the earlier sun. At the sink the water ran soft and quiet when they rinsed the bowl, and the sound mixed with the continuing hush of leaves beyond the glass. Idris set the clean bowl upside down on the drying board and walked back to the windowsill, where the jars caught pink light now that the sun had slipped lower.

Bodhi held out a length of pale wool. "We can braid this while the star rests," Bodhi suggested. Idris took one end and felt the wool's dry, springy texture slip between their fingers. Together they twisted three strands slowly, the motion steady and repeating. The ferryman poured warm milk into two small cups and set them near the sill so Idris and Bodhi could sip without leaving their work. Steam rose in thin curls, and Idris breathed the mild heat while watching the covered tray. Outside, a single fig leaf tapped the glass once, then fell quiet again. Idris kept their eyes on the braid rather than the star, counting the slow turns of wool in their mind.

"Does the paste take long?" Idris asked after several quiet minutes. The ferryman answered without hurry, "It finishes in its own time, the way the river finds the bank." Idris nodded and returned to the braid, feeling the wool grow warmer from the heat of their hands. The trays along the sill held beads that glittered softly when the light shifted. Idris noticed how the drying wool had loosened its curls in the open air, becoming softer than when it first hung there. They pressed one finished braid flat against the wood and smoothed it with the side of a thumb. The room smelled comfortingly of the warm bread still sitting under its cloth on the counter, and that smell made Idris feel settled and patient inside.

Bodhi finished their own short braid and laid it beside Idris's. "We can make little loops next," Bodhi said. Idris agreed and began curling the end of the wool into a circle no bigger than a thumbnail. The light on the windowsill turned deeper gold, then began to fade toward blue. Idris felt the temperature of the air drop a degree at a time, cool enough that the jars no longer felt sun-warmed when brushed by a knuckle. They kept their hands busy with the wool so they would not reach for the paper star too soon. The ferryman moved quietly about the cottage, folding a blanket and setting two chairs closer to the window where the view of the fig tree stayed gentle and green.

Idris finished three small loops and set them in an empty tray. Their shoulders felt loose now, and the earlier wish to peek under the cloth had grown lighter, almost like a soap bubble that drifted away on its own. They listened again to the soft outdoor sounds and watched how the last daylight painted the drying wool in silver edges. The paper star remained still and covered, its paste no longer calling quite so loudly. Idris picked up the empty milk cups, noticing how the ceramic had cooled further, and carried them the short distance back to the sink without spilling a drop. When they returned, Bodhi had already begun a fourth loop, and Idris joined in with easy hands.

"I think it may be ready soon," the ferryman said from his chair, voice as steady as the slow evening. Idris looked toward the middle tray but did not touch the cloth yet. Instead they rested both palms flat on the windowsill wood, feeling its smooth grain and the leftover warmth that had not quite left. Bodhi smiled across the space between them. Outside the fig leaves moved once more in a breath of air that carried no chill, only the soft evening quiet. Idris counted ten slow breaths, each one matching the gentle sway of wool hanging nearby. The jars reflected the first tiny glimmers of indoor light as the day finished its long fade.

Idris finally lifted the cloth with two careful fingers. The paste on the paper star had dried clear and firm, no longer shining wet but holding a soft matte glow that caught the new lamplight. They lifted the star free of the tray and held it up so Bodhi and the ferryman could see the five even points. "It waited just long enough," Idris whispered, turning the star so every side caught the room's calm brightness. The ferryman nodded his approval while Bodhi clapped once, quietly. Idris placed the finished star in the center of the windowsill among the jars and trays, where the drying wool framed it like a gentle nest. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
