The warm smell of toasted sesame drifted from the open kitchen window as Zola’s small hands already smoothed the soft edges of a pale yellow paper star. Afternoon light rested gently on the garden path, and the air felt pleasantly cool against their cheeks while they turned the star over and over. Zola could hear the quiet clink of metal nearby and knew the two watering cans waited by the neat garden row. They pressed the star flat against their palm, feeling its thin crisp surface, and smiled at the way the paper caught a soft glow. Miss Dalca had set out a long loop of string earlier, and Zola wanted every star ready before the light changed. Greta knelt close, her own hands careful with another star the color of cream. Zola whispered, “These will look lovely once they hang,” and Greta nodded without hurry.

Along the garden row the string stretched between two low posts so that anyone walking past would not bump their heads on the paper stars once they were fixed in place. The soil felt damp and cool under Zola’s bare toes, and a light breeze carried the leafy scent of tomato plants. Two watering cans sat side by side, their handles warm from the earlier sun, ready if anyone needed a sip for the roots later. Zola held up a star and measured the space above the string with careful eyes. Miss Dalca stood a short distance away in her cotton apron, the fabric soft and slightly worn where she had wiped her hands many times. She watched without rushing them. Greta said, “If we climb the step stool too fast the stars might tangle,” and Zola agreed at once.

Zola and Greta looked at the pile of paper stars and the waiting string and understood that one person hurrying would make the whole row twist. The light slanted gold across the leaves, and a sparrow chirped once from the fence. Zola felt the smooth paper again and decided they would not rush. They set the stars in two neat stacks on a flat stone so nothing would blow away. Greta touched the string lightly and felt how it hummed when the breeze moved it. Miss Dalca only smiled and stayed quiet, giving them room to think. Zola said softly, “We can split the work into two slow jobs,” and Greta answered, “Yes, that feels right.” The plan settled between them like a calm breath.

Zola took the job of clipping each paper star to a short piece of spare string so the stars could dangle freely without swinging into anyone’s face. Greta chose the job of tying those short pieces onto the long garden string, keeping every knot the same size. The afternoon air stayed mild, and the watering cans cast twin shadows that slowly lengthened. Zola worked with steady fingers, listening to the tiny snip of scissors and smelling the green tomato leaves each time the breeze shifted. Greta’s knots stayed gentle and even. Neither of them spoke loudly; the garden itself seemed to listen. Miss Dalca stepped closer only when they both looked up, her cotton apron brushing the low plants with a soft hush.

When Zola’s first short string was ready, Greta held the long garden line steady so the paper star could rise without spinning. The light had turned softer now, almost peach-colored on the path. Zola felt the cool metal of a watering-can rim under one hand while they balanced. Greta whispered the count of spaces between stars so each one hung free. Miss Dalca offered only a quiet name for the simple knot they needed. “That is a reef knot,” she said, “the kind that lies flat and kind.” Zola practiced the knot once on a spare scrap, feeling the string slide smoothly, then passed the finished star to Greta. They moved like that, one slow gift after another.

A soft click of a gate latch sounded from the far end of the yard as someone closed it gently after bringing in a basket of herbs. Zola paused only long enough to notice the sound, then returned to the next paper star. The toasted sesame smell still drifted faintly from the house, mixing with the earthy garden air. Greta’s hands never jerked the string; she waited until Zola’s clip was firm. Miss Dalca counted the open spaces aloud once when they asked, her voice low and even: “Three finger-widths, then the next.” Zola repeated the measure with their own fingers against the string and smiled. The paper stars began to float above the row like quiet little moons that would never bump a forehead or shoulder.

They kept the two jobs separate yet shared every finished star so the work stayed even. Zola clipped and prepared while Greta lifted and tied, and whenever one needed a steadier hand the other simply offered it without words. The temperature cooled another degree, and the watering cans felt almost cold now against Zola’s knuckles. Miss Dalca named the reef knot again when Greta asked, then stepped back so the children could finish. Zola said, “We are nearly there,” and Greta answered, “Together feels best.” The last few stars rose into place, each one high enough and spaced so the path stayed open and kind for anyone walking through at dusk.

When the final paper star settled into its spot, Zola and Greta stood side by side and looked along the whole gentle row. The string held steady, the stars turned slowly in the soft air, and neither child had rushed a single step. Miss Dalca rested one hand on the nearest watering can and simply watched the quiet hanging lights of paper. Zola reached out and touched Greta’s sleeve in thanks. The garden smelled green and peaceful. They gathered the empty clips and the spare string into a small basket, moving as slowly as they had begun. No star swung low; every head would pass safely underneath. The two watering cans waited in their places like friendly guards, ready for morning.

Later, after they rinsed their hands at the outdoor tap and the cool water trickled over their wrists, Zola and Greta carried the basket indoors while Miss Dalca followed with quiet steps. Inside the kitchen the light had grown dim and golden. Zola set a small wooden chair square to the table so everything felt tidy and ready for tomorrow. Greta smoothed the cotton apron Miss Dalca had hung on its hook. The paper stars still danced faintly outside above the garden row, safe and high. Zola leaned against the table edge and felt the smooth wood under their palms. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
