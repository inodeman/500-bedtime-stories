Grandma Nia knelt on the library rug under the round window, already halfway through stacking picture books into a soft spiral so the glossy covers caught the late afternoon light like quiet moons. The wool felt warm under her knees, and she hummed while she worked, the sound low and steady. Sora stepped closer and watched the spiral grow, then sat down so their knees touched the edge of the books. Grandma Nia glanced up with a small smile and kept stacking, leaving the choice of where to settle entirely to the children. Outside, a gate latch clicked once and then fell still, the brief metal sound fading into the calm of the room. Sora breathed in and smelled the faint honey that drifted from the tea tray on the nearby table, sweet and gentle. “We can read when everyone feels ready,” Grandma Nia said, her voice unhurried, and she returned to aligning the next book so its corner lined up just so.

Sora looked across the rug and noticed Kavi standing a little apart, one foot on the carpet fringe and one foot still on the cool wood floor, watching the spiral of books without stepping into it. The round window poured a pool of pale gold across the center of the rug, leaving the edge in softer shadow. Kavi’s hands hung loosely at their sides, and Sora could see the chalky hand from an earlier drawing game still dusted white along the knuckles, the powder dry and powdery when Sora later brushed it by accident. Sora did not call out or point. Instead Sora shifted two cushions sideways, making a wider half-circle that opened toward the quieter edge of the rug where the light thinned. The air near the window felt cooler than the middle of the room, and Sora liked the difference.

Kavi stayed near that quieter edge, close to a quiet corner that had been offered as a choice rather than a seat someone had to claim. Grandma Nia did not rearrange anything herself. She simply finished the last book in the spiral and rested her palms on her thighs, waiting. Sora patted the newly opened space once, soft against the wool, and then began turning pages slowly so the paper made a small dry whisper. “This page has a blue river,” Sora said, holding the book so the picture faced both the center and the edge. Kavi took one step closer, then another, until the fringe of the rug brushed both their shoes. The temperature under the window stayed mild, neither warm nor cold, and the books smelled faintly of paper and the honey still lingering from the tray.

Sora changed the pace of the reading without announcing any new rule. Instead of flipping quickly, Sora left each picture open longer, giving time for eyes to wander and for shoulders to settle. Kavi eased down onto the cushion Sora had moved, not as a guest of honor but as someone who simply belonged in the half-circle. Grandma Nia watched with quiet eyes and said nothing about who sat where. The round window’s light slid a little farther across the rug as the afternoon deepened, painting the quiet corner in softer gold. Sora felt the wool nap under their fingers, thick and friendly, and kept the book balanced so Kavi could see every drawing without stretching.

“I like the little boat,” Kavi whispered, and Sora nodded and turned the page even more slowly so the boat stayed visible a moment longer. Grandma Nia reached for her teacup, and the faint honey smell rose again when she lifted the lid, though no one mentioned it. Sora slid one more cushion into the open space between them so the three of them—Sora, Kavi, and the spiral of books—formed an easy curve that included the quiet corner without making it special out loud. The library stayed peaceful; only the soft rustle of pages and the distant hush of the building filled the air. Sora’s shoulder brushed Kavi’s, warm through their sleeves, and neither pulled away.

Grandma Nia let the children keep solving the seating themselves. When Sora noticed the book stack leaning, they straightened it together with Kavi, hands moving side by side until the spiral looked steady again. The chalky hand left a faint white smudge on one book cover, and Sora wiped it gently with a sleeve so the picture stayed clear. “We can stay right here,” Sora said, and Kavi answered, “Yes, right here is good.” The round window’s glass felt smooth when Sora’s elbow rested near the sill, cooler than the rug, and the quiet corner remained simply part of the circle rather than a prize anyone had to win. Grandma Nia smiled into her cup and did not rearrange a single cushion.

They turned three more pages at the new slow pace, pausing whenever Kavi pointed at a detail. Sora felt the wool grow warmer under their legs as they stayed still longer. Outside light softened further, and inside the room the tea tray still carried that gentle honey note each time someone breathed near it. No one hurried. Grandma Nia set her empty cup down with a tiny ceramic click that sounded friendly rather than sharp. Sora leaned the open book against a pillow so both children could see without holding it, and the quiet corner held them both without needing any announcement.

Kavi’s breathing matched Sora’s after a while, slow and even, and the library rug felt like a small island of calm under the round window. Sora rested their chalk-dusted fingertips against the cushion edge and felt the fabric’s nubby weave. Grandma Nia folded her hands and let the afternoon keep its own time. “Shall we look at one more picture?” Sora asked, and Kavi said, “One more,” with a soft smile. The gate had already latched earlier; nothing else interrupted. The light on the rug turned the color of pale butter, and the children stayed exactly where they had chosen to be.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Sora closed the book with care so the covers met without a snap, and Kavi’s shoulder stayed companionably close on the shared cushion. Grandma Nia rose only to dim the overhead lamp a little more, leaving the round window and the lantern to share the room. The wool under them held the day’s leftover warmth, and the quiet corner simply remained part of the circle they had made together. Sora yawned once, small and peaceful, and the three of them sat in the gentle light while the library settled around their easy silence.
