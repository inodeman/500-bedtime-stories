Idris heard a tiny clink from the corner of the quiet sitting room, soft as a spoon touching china. Late afternoon light poured through the curtains in warm honey stripes and warmed the woven rug under Idris's bare toes. Idris turned their head and noticed the little sound again, smiling when they saw Grandma Nia in the doorway with her woven bag. "Idris, the market lane still holds baskets and jars and flower pots if we go now," Grandma Nia said. Idris stood and felt the mild draft from the open window cool their arms. They slipped on soft shoes, took Grandma Nia's hand, and stepped outside where the path stones held leftover warmth from the sun and the sky stretched wide and pale blue.

Along the market lane Idris walked beside Grandma Nia past tall stacks of baskets filled with oranges and green beans, clay jars stoppered with cork, and flower pots whose leaves tumbled over the rims in cheerful green. The stones under Idris's shoes felt smooth and faintly dusty. Vendors arranged their goods with quiet care, and the air held a gentle hush. "Look how many colors the flowers wear," Idris said, pointing toward a pot of yellow blooms. Grandma Nia squeezed Idris's fingers. From a nearby herb stall the fresh smell of mint leaves drifted past, and Idris breathed it in slowly, glad for the cool green scent mixed with warmer scents of bread and earth.

Near a low stone step by a pottery stand Grandma Nia reached for a clear glass jar packed with buttons of every size and color. The jar tipped just enough for the lid to loosen, and buttons spilled down the step in a bright scatter, some rolling toward the flower pots and others nestling in the cracks between the pale stones. The slanting light made each button gleam like a tiny coin. Grandma Nia bent carefully, and Idris could see at once that gathering every button alone would be awkward and slow while people and baskets moved past. The air felt pleasantly warm against the back of Idris's neck as they watched the last few buttons come to rest.

Idris did not wait to be asked and did not look around for praise. They knelt on the cool grainy stone of the step and began picking up buttons with steady fingers, working from the bottom while Grandma Nia gathered from the top. "I can reach the ones that rolled near the flower pots," Idris said in a calm voice. Grandma Nia glanced over, and her eyes crinkled with quiet gladness. "Thank you, Idris," she answered, though Idris simply kept collecting without seeking more words. A shop cat padded past on silent paws, and once the soft chime of a cat's collar bell floated through the lane before the cat curled beside a basket of greens.

Felix, a neighbor with freckles and a kind face, paused nearby carrying his own small basket. "Do you need another pair of hands for the buttons?" Felix asked. Idris shook their head gently and offered a friendly smile. "We have it between the two of us," Idris said. Felix nodded and waited a moment under the cool shade of a striped awning. Among the buttons Idris found a smooth glass bead, cool and slick against their fingertips, and placed it carefully into Grandma Nia's open palm so it would not roll away again. Together Idris and Grandma Nia built two neat piles that slowly filled the jar once more until almost every button found its place inside the glass.

The jar grew heavy and familiar again as the final buttons slid in with soft clicks against one another. Grandma Nia twisted the lid closed and settled the jar deep in her woven bag. Idris brushed fine dust from their knees and stood, feeling the pleasant stretch after kneeling in the mild air. Nearby flower pots held blossoms that glowed soft pink in the long evening light. Idris noticed how Grandma Nia's shoulders eased, and that quiet change was enough. They walked onward past more baskets of round breads and jars of golden honey without needing any extra notice from anyone. The lane now carried a faint sweet warmth from baked goods cooling on a wooden cart.

Farther down the lane Grandma Nia stopped at a bread stall and bought two small soft rolls, handing one to Idris. The roll felt warm and yielding in Idris's hands, and the crust made a gentle crackle when they took a careful bite. "This tastes like the sunny part of the day," Idris said, and Grandma Nia laughed a quiet laugh that made Idris grin. Shoppers drifted homeward as the sky turned the gentle color of ripe peaches above the rooftops. Idris stayed close, matching Grandma Nia's easy steps, and the air grew cooler against their bare forearms. They passed the same stone step where the buttons had spilled, now empty and clean under the pink-gold evening glow.

On the walk home the path beneath the trees felt cooler still, and high leaves whispered above Idris's head with a soft shushing sound. Grandma Nia hummed a short wordless tune, and Idris hummed along in the same low tone. Inside their house the rooms held the leftover warmth of the long day, and the wooden floors felt smooth under Idris's socks. Idris washed their hands in water that felt fresh and tingly, then slipped into soft pajamas that brushed lightly against their arms and legs. Grandma Nia sat nearby in the quiet chair while Idris settled onto the bed, and the stillness between them felt full and peaceful. Outside a last bird called once from a branch and then fell silent.

Idris rested their head on the pillow and listened to the slow breath of the quiet house. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Its gentle light painted a pale circle on the wall beside Idris, warm as a held hand. Grandma Nia smoothed the blanket so it lay snug over Idris's shoulders. The fabric felt fleecy and kind against Idris's chin. Outside the window the market lane had gone quiet for the night, and Idris closed their eyes remembering the bright buttons on the step and the easy way two pairs of hands had made the spilled jar simple again. Sleep came like a soft tide, quiet and sure, carrying Idris into gentle dreams of baskets and flower pots and the cool green air of the lane.
