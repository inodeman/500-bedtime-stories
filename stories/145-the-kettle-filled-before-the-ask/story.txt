"Papa, may I hold the string while you tie the next parcel?" Tala asked, their voice soft above the gentle slap of water against the stones.

The harbor step felt cool under Tala’s shoes as the evening light turned the sky the color of pale honey. Papa knelt beside a neat stack of brown packages, his fingers moving carefully over the twine. Tala stood close and smelled the clean salt air mixed with something quieter, the scent of fresh paper rising from the open wrappings. A wooden cart rolled past on the upper walk, its wheels quiet on the boards, and somewhere behind them a gate latch clicked once and settled. Tala watched Papa’s shoulders rise and fall with the work and decided the step was a good place to stay still and useful.

Papa reached for the little copper kettle he had set on a flat stone, meaning to pour tea for them both once the parcels were ready to change hands. He tipped it and nothing came out but a thin metallic sigh. The kettle that had been full an hour earlier now sat empty just before tea, and Papa’s face showed a small crease of surprise. “I must have used the last of it when I rinsed the cups,” he said quietly. Tala felt the evening breeze brush their cheeks and noticed how the empty kettle made Papa’s hands hover without a clear next job. The water below moved in slow silver lines, and Tala kept their eyes on Papa rather than the empty spout.

Pilar arrived then with two more parcels tucked under one arm, her steps light on the worn stone. “These are the last ones for tonight,” Pilar said, setting them down with care. The stone under Tala’s palm felt smooth and slightly damp from earlier spray, and the air held a mild warmth even as the light faded. Papa tried to balance the empty kettle on a crate while also reaching for fresh twine, and the awkward angle made him pause. Tala saw the way the kettle wanted to tip and how Papa’s free hand searched for a steady place. Without a word about helping, Tala simply stepped closer and rested one hand on the crate so the kettle would not wobble.

Tala took the empty kettle gently from Papa’s fingers and carried it two steps to the low tap that stood near the damp clay wall of the old storage shed. The wall felt cool and grainy under Tala’s fingertips as they steadied themselves, and a thin ribbon of water sang into the kettle with a clear bright sound. Papa kept tying, and Pilar stacked the finished parcels in a careful row. Tala filled the kettle only halfway so it would not be too heavy, then set it back on the flat stone beside the small spirit lamp Papa used for heating. The lamp’s tiny flame made a soft orange glow on the copper side, and Tala could feel the first gentle heat beginning to rise.

“Thank you for bringing those, Pilar,” Papa said as he finished the last knot. Pilar smiled and brushed a fleck of dust from her sleeve. Tala stayed quiet and simply watched the water inside the kettle begin to tremble toward warmth. The harbor water lapped with a steady hush, and the stones held the leftover heat of the day against Tala’s ankles. When the kettle sang its small high note, Tala poured the tea into the two cups Papa had brought, the steam rising in pale curls that smelled faintly of leaves. Papa and Pilar each took a cup, and Tala sat on the lowest step with their own small share, the warm china fitting neatly in their hands.

The parcels changed hands without hurry. A tall boatman accepted the stack from Papa and gave a quiet nod of thanks before carrying them up the gangplank. Tala listened to the soft thud of boots on wood and felt the evening air cool another degree against their neck. Papa rinsed the kettle once more at the tap and dried it with a clean cloth, the copper catching the last scraps of light. Tala helped fold the extra twine into a neat loop and slipped it into Papa’s pocket. No one spoke about who had filled the kettle or who had steadied the crate; the work simply felt finished and easy.

Pilar said goodnight and walked toward the upper path, her footsteps fading into the quiet. Papa and Tala remained on the harbor step a little longer, watching the water turn deeper blue. Tala rested both palms on their knees and felt the solid calm of the stone beneath them. The air smelled cleaner now that the parcels were gone, and a single gull called once far out over the bay. Papa packed the cups and the kettle into his canvas bag, the metal making a soft muted clink against the cloth. Tala stood when Papa stood, and together they turned away from the step toward the short lane that led home.

They walked slowly past the closed shop fronts where warm squares of light spilled onto the pavement. Tala kept close to Papa’s side and listened to the even rhythm of their steps. At the corner the air grew stiller, and the familiar door of their house waited with its blue paint softened by dusk. Inside, the kitchen held the gentle heat of the stove that had been banked earlier, and the wooden floor felt smooth under Tala’s socks once the shoes were left by the mat. Papa set the kettle on its usual hook, empty again but clean, and Tala helped hang the canvas bag on the peg. They both moved quietly so the house would stay peaceful.

Tala washed their hands at the sink and dried them on the soft towel that smelled of soap and sun. Papa turned down the main lamp until only a small circle of light remained near the table. Tala climbed into the wide chair by the window and pulled the knitted blanket over their legs, the wool warm and slightly fuzzy against their skin. Outside, the harbor sounds had grown distant, replaced by the hush of the street. Papa sat nearby and opened a thin book, the pages making the faintest whisper as they turned. Tala’s eyes grew heavy while the room held its quiet temperature, steady and kind. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
