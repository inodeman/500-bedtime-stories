Ms. Okonkwo stood behind the bakery counter already, her hands dusted with flour as she slid a tray of fresh rolls into place and smoothed the paper liners with careful presses of her palms. The pale morning light poured through the front glass and rested warmly on the wooden surface, and Uma stood close enough to feel that gentle heat on their cheeks while the soft, busy hush of early customers filled the room. Uma watched Ms. Okonkwo work and smelled the warm bread rising in gentle waves from the open shelves. A little breeze came through the door each time it opened, cool against Uma’s wrists, and Uma kept their eyes on the neat rows of treats and the wide rain cape that someone had left folded over the end of the counter, its edges spilling farther than one pair of shoulders would need.

Uma shifted nearer to the counter’s edge and felt the solid wood under their fingertips as Ms. Okonkwo finished lining up the buns. Yael stood just behind Uma, quiet and patient, and Uma glanced back to make sure their friend could still see everything on the trays. The rain cape lay there like a soft blue pond of fabric, cut a little too wide for one, and Uma thought about how odd it looked all alone on the busy counter. The light from the high windows made the cape’s buttons shine faintly, and Uma could hear the low clink of cups being set down farther along the counter. Uma’s sweater felt cozy against their neck in the bakery’s mild warmth, and they rocked once on their heels while they waited for Ms. Okonkwo to look up.

Ms. Okonkwo lifted a small plate and said, “Uma, shall I serve you the first sweet roll while it is still soft?” Uma looked at the single roll on the plate and then at the rain cape that was too wide for one person alone. The plate’s rim felt smooth when Uma reached to steady it, and the idea of going first while Yael waited behind them did not sit right. Uma pressed their lips together for a moment and listened to the quiet shuffle of shoes on the bakery floor. Outside the open side window the leaves moved, and Uma heard the wind in the fig leaves making a dry, gentle rustle that seemed to match the soft morning. Uma kept their hand near the plate and thought carefully about fairness and space and friends who stood close by.

“I think the rain cape is too wide for only one,” Uma said, voice calm and clear, “and it would not feel fair if only one person is served first.” Yael stepped a little closer so their shoulder nearly touched Uma’s, and the bakery air smelled faintly of sugar mixed with the earlier warmth. Uma turned the idea over and decided a smaller, even share would bring Yael in properly. Ms. Okonkwo waited without speaking, her floury hands resting lightly on the counter edge, and the light from the window made a soft stripe across the floor near Uma’s shoes. Uma felt the mild heat of the ovens drifting forward and decided the cape and the roll could both be shared in the same spirit.

“We can fold the cape so it covers both of us on the walk later,” Uma said to Yael, “and we can split the roll into two even pieces right now.” Yael smiled and answered, “I would like that very much, Uma.” Uma took the wide rain cape and gathered the extra fabric into neat smaller folds so the shoulders would fit two children side by side without dragging. The cloth felt smooth and slightly cool under Uma’s palms as they shaped it. Ms. Okonkwo watched the careful work and simply nodded once, letting the choice stand without any extra words. The morning light brightened a little more across the glass jars of jam, and Uma felt proud of the tidy smaller share they had made.

Uma used a clean knife Ms. Okonkwo offered and cut the sweet roll down the middle so each half looked the same size. “Here is yours, Yael,” Uma said, holding out one half on a napkin. Yael took it gently and said, “Thank you, Uma. It smells wonderful.” Uma bit into their own half and tasted the soft center while the bakery stayed gently busy around them. The rain cape now rested in its new even folds over Uma’s arm, ready for both of them, and Uma felt the light weight of the fabric against their sleeve. A soft clatter of spoons came from the far end of the counter, and Uma stayed close to Yael so the shared cape would be easy to lift later.

Ms. Okonkwo turned to wipe a spot of flour and then set a small empty bowl near the edge for clearing. Uma noticed the bowl and, because it fit the calm rhythm of the morning, carried the empty bowl to the sink and came back with quiet steps. The floor felt cool under Uma’s shoes as they returned, and the shared cape shifted softly against their side. Yael waited by the counter holding both halves of the napkin, and Uma smiled at the neat way everything had settled. The air near the ovens stayed pleasantly warm, and Uma rested one hand on the counter again while Ms. Okonkwo continued her quiet work without comment on the choice.

Uma and Yael stood together finishing their even pieces of roll, the rain cape folded between them like a friendly blanket ready for later. Uma ran one finger along the cool ceramic rim of a water cup that sat nearby and felt how smooth and rounded it was. The busy morning sounds stayed gentle—soft voices, a distant clock tick, the quiet scrape of a tray—and Uma felt settled and glad. Ms. Okonkwo glanced over once more and left the arrangement exactly as Uma had made it, the smaller share for two and the cape ready for both shoulders. Uma breathed in the last mild warmth of the bakery air and leaned a little toward Yael so the folded cape touched both of them at once.

When the last crumb was gone Uma lifted the rain cape and settled it carefully so Yael could slip under one side while Uma took the other. The fabric covered them evenly now, no longer too wide for one alone, and they stood side by side in the soft light near the door. Outside the fig leaves still moved, but inside the bakery felt calm and finished for them. Uma looked once more at Ms. Okonkwo, who simply raised a hand in a small goodbye. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Uma and Yael stepped toward the door together under the shared cape, the morning still gentle around their shoulders and the quiet bakery behind them full of ordinary kindness.
