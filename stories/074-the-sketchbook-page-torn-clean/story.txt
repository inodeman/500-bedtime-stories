The sketchbook page torn clean along the grain rested across Delia’s open hands, its edge so straight that the paper fibers looked like tiny silver threads in the dim cloakroom light. Delia tilted the page and watched a pale stripe of sun slide over the crayon suns and leafy trees they had drawn earlier. The paper felt cool and dry, almost powdery where the grain had given way without a tear or a snag. Delia breathed slowly and studied every soft line, holding the page close enough that the white surface filled their whole view. A faint warmth from the nearby radiator brushed Delia’s wrists while they kept the page steady. Somewhere beyond the hanging coats the floorboards creaked once, but Delia stayed with the quiet rectangle and the gentle light that made the drawing seem ready to speak.

Delia stepped farther into the school cloakroom, where wool scarves and rain jackets brushed their shoulders with a scratchy whisper. Just beyond the last row of pegs the lunch mats waited in two neat rows, their blue cloth still showing faint fold marks from the morning. Delia carried the sketchbook page torn clean along the grain as carefully as a tray, walking past the boot rack toward the open space. The air near the mats held a mild chill from the tile, and Delia felt it rise through their socks. They knelt and set the page down on the nearest mat so the drawing faced the doorway. Delia smoothed one wrinkle out of the cloth with the flat of their hand until the blue lay flat and calm under the paper.

Uncle Idris arrived with a wide basket whose wicker handle felt rough and woven under Delia’s curious fingers when they helped steady it. He set the basket on the mat edge and lifted the lid, and at once the warm smell of tomato soup drifted up in a rich red steam that fogged the cooler air for a moment. Delia leaned closer and watched the steam curl like thin ribbons. Uncle Idris poured careful portions into three small bowls while Delia kept one palm near the sketchbook page so it would not curl in the rising heat. The soup glowed a soft orange in the low light, and Delia could hear the tiny bubble sounds as the liquid settled. “The mats are ready whenever you are, Delia,” Uncle Idris said in a low voice that matched the quiet room.

A soft metallic chime floated in from the hallway, the bright ring of a cat’s collar bell, and then Willa appeared in the cloakroom doorway with rosy cheeks from the walk. Delia looked up and felt the cooler hallway air mix with the warmer soup steam around their ankles. Willa’s shoes scuffed gently on the tile as they came to sit on the second lunch mat. Delia held the sketchbook page so Willa could see the three smiling suns. “I hoped you would come before the bowls were filled,” Delia said, and the words came out soft as the steam. Willa settled cross-legged and smiled at the page while the last echo of the collar bell faded somewhere near the office door.

Uncle Idris began to set the first bowl nearer to Delia’s mat, the spoon handle catching a glint of window light. Delia watched the motion and felt the paper under their fingers grow slightly warmer from the nearby soup. The cloakroom smelled even stronger of tomato now, thick and friendly, and Delia could feel the heat of the bowl on their cheek when they leaned. For a second Delia thought of taking the first share and showing the drawing right away, yet the single bowl looked lonely on the wide mat. Delia glanced at Willa’s empty place and at the third mat still waiting. The sketchbook page torn clean along the grain suddenly seemed too large for only one person to claim first. Delia’s shoulders stayed loose, and the room stayed peaceful while they thought.

Delia drew the page a little closer and spoke before the second bowl moved. “If only one person is served first, it will not feel fair,” Delia said, looking from the soup to Willa and back again. Uncle Idris paused with the ladle still in the air, and the steam rose in a quiet column between them. Delia’s voice stayed even and kind. “We can make smaller, even shares so everyone begins together.” The lunch mats felt smooth under Delia’s knees as they shifted to make more room. Light from the high window softened across the blue cloth and the white page alike. Willa nodded without hurry, and the cat’s collar bell chimed once more far away like a tiny agreement.

Delia took the sketchbook page and carefully folded it once along a blank margin so that three equal spaces appeared beside the drawing, then unfolded it flat again like a gentle map. They asked Uncle Idris for the smaller cups from the basket bottom and watched him pour the tomato soup into three matching portions that steamed side by side. Delia set one cup on each lunch mat and laid a corner of the page near every cup so the crayon suns touched all three places at once. “Now the page belongs with all of us,” Delia said, and the words floated on the warm air. Willa reached out and touched the paper edge with one careful fingertip. The wicker handle of the basket rested against Delia’s other hand, its woven ridges steady and familiar while the even shares settled into place.

Uncle Idris looked at the three cups and the shared page and simply nodded, letting the choice stand without a speech. He sat on the edge of the third mat and lifted his own cup so the others could begin. “That looks just right,” he said only, and then sipped. Delia felt the first warm spoonful of tomato soup on their tongue and heard the soft clink of spoons against cups. Willa smiled across the mats and Delia smiled back while the sketchbook page lay between them like a quiet bridge. The cloakroom light grew softer as clouds moved outside, and the hanging coats hung still. Delia rested one hand on the cool paper and the other on the warm cup, content that every share had arrived at the same gentle moment.

Later the bowls sat empty and the sketchbook page rested again in Delia’s lap while the three of them leaned against the lower coat pegs. Delia felt the last mild heat of the soup still in their chest and listened to the distant hush of the empty hallway. Uncle Idris folded the lunch mats into a neat stack, and Willa helped tuck the basket lid closed. Delia traced one crayon sun with a slow fingertip and felt the paper’s grain once more under their skin. Outside the window the sky turned a deeper blue, and inside the air cooled to a sleepy stillness. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Delia closed their eyes for a moment and kept the shared page safe against their knees until it was time to walk home.
