A soft clink of a butter knife against a plate rose in the warm kitchen, and Greta heard it right away while sitting at the long table. The light from the overhead lamp poured gently across the wood, showing the little pots of jam and butter and the shared pencil tin that held stubby crayons and a stub of graphite. Greta felt the smooth edge of the table under their palms and smelled the faint green scent of basil from a pot on the windowsill. Ms. Okonkwo stood nearby humming a quiet tune, and Suri sat across from Greta with a napkin half folded. Greta remembered the claim they had made earlier about teeth already brushed, and the words sat heavy like a small stone in their pocket.

Greta almost tucked that claim deeper away when Ms. Okonkwo turned with a kind look. The room felt pleasantly warm from the oven that had cooled after supper. “I already brushed,” Greta had said before, yet the minty taste was missing and the claim felt tangled. Suri passed the pencil tin so Greta could draw a little pear on scrap paper, and the tin made a tiny rattle. Greta’s fingers brushed a polished pebble that someone had left among the pencils, cool and round and pleasing to roll once between thumb and finger. Ms. Okonkwo set two more napkins down and said, “We can fold these together if you like.” Greta nodded, still holding the almost-hidden truth close.

They began to fold the cloth napkins so the stitched pear faced up on each one, the fabric soft against Greta’s hands. The overhead light made soft shadows that moved when anyone shifted. Greta wanted to keep the fib quiet, yet Ms. Okonkwo’s calm face made the hiding feel lonelier than speaking. “Greta, is something resting on your mind?” Ms. Okonkwo asked in a low voice that matched the quiet kitchen. Greta looked at the jam jar’s shiny lid and at Suri’s friendly eyes. The tick of a tin clock on the shelf marked steady seconds while Greta gathered breath. Suri whispered, “You can tell her. She always helps.”

Greta set the polished pebble back into the pencil tin and spoke the plain truth. “I said my teeth were brushed, but I only rinsed with water. I almost hid it.” The words came out soft and clear. Ms. Okonkwo smiled without any teasing and rested a gentle hand on the back of Greta’s chair. The kitchen air carried that fresh basil smell again as a breeze moved the curtain. “Thank you for telling me straight, Greta,” Ms. Okonkwo said. “We can mend what needs mending right here.” Suri grinned and kept folding so every pear pointed the same way. Greta felt the warm air on their cheeks and the solid wood under their elbows.

Together they walked the few steps to the sink that stood near the table’s end. Cool water ran over Greta’s hands first, then they held the familiar brush while Ms. Okonkwo squeezed a pea-sized bit of paste. The paste smelled clean and mild. Greta brushed carefully in small circles, listening to the quiet swish. Suri waited at the table and tapped the shared pencil tin in a soft rhythm that matched the tin clock’s distant ticks. When Greta finished and rinsed, the mint lingered pleasantly. “There,” Ms. Okonkwo said. “All set, and no hurry at all.” Greta dried their mouth on a soft towel that felt fluffy against their skin.

Back at the kitchen table Greta sat again among the jam, butter, and open pencil tin. The lamp light glowed steady and kind across the folded napkins with their pears facing up. Greta drew a tiny smiling sun beside the earlier pear sketch while Suri added a blue cloud. Ms. Okonkwo spread a thin layer of butter on a small piece of toast and offered it without fuss. Greta took a careful bite; the toast tasted simple and good. “I feel lighter now,” Greta said. Suri nodded and answered, “Truth sits nicer than hiding.” The room stayed peaceful, filled only with the soft sounds of crayons and quiet chewing.

Greta rolled the crayon between their fingers and noticed how the wax felt slightly warm from use. Ms. Okonkwo wiped a crumb from the table with a cloth that left the wood smooth again. Outside the window the evening sky held a gentle blue that filtered in as cooler air. Greta remembered the earlier tangle and how speaking had loosened it. They colored the sun brighter and shared the pencil tin so Suri could borrow the yellow. “May I keep drawing a little longer?” Greta asked. “Of course,” Ms. Okonkwo replied, settling into the chair beside them with her own quiet smile. The three of them stayed close, the jam jar glinting like a small red lantern of its own.

After the toast was gone Greta helped gather the crayons back into the tin with careful clicks. The polished pebble nestled among them once more, its smooth surface catching a glint of light. Suri finished the last napkin fold and stacked them neatly. Greta felt calm settle like a soft blanket across their shoulders. Ms. Okonkwo poured a little warm milk into two cups, and the steam rose in thin curls that carried a mild sweet scent. Greta wrapped both hands around the cup and felt its pleasant heat. “We mended it together,” Greta said softly. Suri clinked their cup gently against Greta’s in a tiny cheerful toast that made no loud noise.

Later the kitchen grew quieter still as the milk cooled and the drawings were set aside. Greta yawned a small yawn and leaned against the table’s edge, eyelids growing heavy in the kind light. Ms. Okonkwo smoothed Greta’s hair with a light touch that felt like a whisper. Suri waved a sleepy goodbye and headed down the short hall. Greta stayed a moment longer, breathing the last of the basil from the sill and hearing only the softest house sounds. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Greta rose, ready for bed, carrying the settled feeling of truth spoken and evening peace all the way down the quiet hall.
