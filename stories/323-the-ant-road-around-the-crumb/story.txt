Auntie June stood already at the low wooden table near Gita’s bed, smoothing a pale cloth with both hands while pale frosty light slipped through the glass and made the room feel cool on the skin. Gita padded closer in soft socks and watched the careful motion, breathing the quiet air that carried a faint chill from the window. Outside the frost had painted tiny white feathers on the pane, and inside the floorboards felt smooth and cool under Gita’s feet. Auntie June hummed a low tune as she worked, and Gita stayed right beside her, noticing how the morning brightness turned the edges of everything silver. The bedroom smelled clean and still, and Gita rested one hand on the table edge, feeling the solid wood.

Gita turned toward the tall bedroom window where the frost glittered like scattered sugar in the early light, and the glass itself felt cold when Gita pressed a fingertip gently against it. Tamsin arrived a moment later carrying a small cup of warm milk that sent a thin curl of steam upward. Gita smiled at Tamsin and pointed toward the wide sill. “Come see the window with me,” Gita said softly. Together they leaned near the glass, and the cool air from the tiny gap at the bottom brushed their cheeks. Outside a thin layer of frost covered the garden path, yet inside the room stayed gentle and safe. Gita kept close to the sill, eyes bright with curiosity about whatever tiny thing might live there this morning.

Right on the broad wooden sill Gita spotted a thin dark line of ants walking steadily, and the line curved the long way around a single pale crumb that must have fallen from breakfast toast. The ants moved like a living ribbon, each one following the one ahead without hurry, their tiny legs making no sound at all on the wood. Gita felt a real wish rise up to pocket the crumb or poke the neat road just to see what would happen, yet Gita stayed still and only watched. The crumb looked soft and dry in the cool light, and the ants kept their careful path around it as if the crumb were a little hill they preferred to circle. Gita’s breath fogged a small circle on the glass for a second before it faded.

Gita beckoned Tamsin closer with a quiet hand and whispered, “Look how they go the long way around. We can watch without touching.” Tamsin knelt so their shoulders touched Gita’s and both children peered down at the moving line. The wood of the sill felt smooth and slightly cool under Gita’s palm when they rested it nearby without pressing close. “They know exactly where to walk,” Tamsin answered in the same soft voice. Gita nodded and kept naming the slow parade for Tamsin, pointing with one careful finger held high above the ants so nothing would startle them. The frosty morning light made every ant shine like a tiny polished seed as they traveled.

Auntie June finished at the table and came over, wiping her hands on a floured towel that left a fine soft dust across her fingers before she set the cloth aside. She smelled faintly of lemon soap from washing earlier, a bright clean scent that drifted near Gita as she leaned in. “What have you found, Gita?” Auntie June asked. Gita answered, “An ant road going all the way around this crumb. We are looking without disturbing it.” Auntie June smiled and sank to their level so the three of them formed a quiet half-circle of watchers. The ants continued their steady march, never breaking the long curve they had chosen.

Outside the window the wind moved through the fig leaves with a low rustling hush that rose and fell like quiet breathing, and Gita listened while still watching the tiny travelers. The sound mixed with the soft creak of the floor when Tamsin shifted weight, yet nothing inside the room felt hurried. Gita described each turn of the ant road again for Auntie June, speaking slowly so every word stayed gentle. “See how they never climb the crumb? They just keep going around,” Gita said. Auntie June nodded and rested a warm hand on Gita’s shoulder, and the shared warmth felt comforting against the cool air near the glass.

Gita invited Tamsin to count the ants in whispers, and together they reached twelve before the line curved out of easy view toward a tiny crack in the sill. The children stayed perfectly still except for the small tilt of their heads, and the frost on the outer glass caught the rising sun so that pale gold sparks danced across the pane. Auntie June murmured, “They have their own map this morning.” Gita felt glad that the wish to poke or pocket had passed, replaced by the calm pleasure of simply seeing. The wooden sill held the whole small world steady, and Gita’s knees pressed comfortably into the rug that felt thick and kind under them.

They stayed a little longer while the ants kept their patient road, and Gita showed Tamsin how to breathe slowly so no sudden puff of air would reach the crumb. Auntie June rose at last and returned to the table, wiping its surface in slow circles with a clean cloth that made a soft whispering sound against the wood. Gita and Tamsin stood too, still glancing back at the sill where the living line continued undisturbed. The bedroom felt peaceful around them, filled with the quiet glow of morning and the knowledge that the ants could finish their journey exactly as they pleased. Gita reached for Tamsin’s hand and gave it a light squeeze of shared delight.

Later when the frost began to thin on the glass Gita climbed into bed while Auntie June tucked the blanket high and Tamsin settled in the chair nearby with a picture book. The room held its gentle coolness near the window yet warmth gathered under the covers, and Gita’s eyes grew heavy watching the last silver light. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the garden rested under its thin white coat, and inside everything stayed safe and still as Gita drifted toward sleep knowing the ant road remained exactly where they had left it.
