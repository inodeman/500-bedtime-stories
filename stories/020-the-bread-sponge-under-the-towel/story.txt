A tin clock gave one small, steady tick from the shelf, and Tala noticed it at once. The quiet craft corner held soft evening light that rested on stacks of colored paper, a jar of paste, and a wooden rack with clean cloths hanging like quiet flags. Tala stood still in the warm air and listened again. Auntie Luz sat close by on a low stool, smoothing a scrap of paper with her palm. Near the rack, a round bowl waited under a floury towel, and Tala knew the bread sponge napped inside it. The floorboards felt smooth under Tala’s bare feet. Tala’s shoulders loosened as the calm room held them.

Tala stepped nearer the covered bowl and whispered, “Auntie Luz, may I lift the towel just a little?” Auntie Luz smiled and kept her voice gentle. “Not yet, Tala. The bread sponge is still resting, and it likes its cover until it is ready.” Tala looked at the floury towel and felt the wish to peek rise and then settle. The paste jar gave off a mild, clean scent that mixed with the warm air. Tala rested one hand on the edge of the table and watched the soft folds of the towel stay still. Light from the window touched the wooden rack and made the hanging cloths glow a little. Tala nodded slowly.

Auntie Luz set a linen napkin beside the paper stacks so Tala could wipe paste from their fingers later. Tala touched the neat square and felt its dry, even weave under their fingertips before letting go. “What can I do while we wait?” Tala asked. “You may cut a quiet star from the blue paper,” Auntie Luz said, “or simply sit and listen with me.” Tala chose the paper and the small scissors with rounded tips. The craft corner stayed peaceful. A faint draft brushed Tala’s wrists like a cool ribbon. Tala cut carefully along a pencil line Auntie Luz had drawn earlier, and the soft snip of the blades made a tiny rhythm that matched Tala’s breathing.

The orange peel smell drifted from a small dish Auntie Luz had set near the paste for a sweet craft scent, and Tala breathed it in while they worked. The bread sponge stayed covered under its floury towel on the corner of the table closest to the wooden rack. Tala glanced at it once, then returned to the blue star. Auntie Luz hummed a low tune without words. The room felt comfortably warm around Tala’s shoulders. Paper scraps gathered in a neat pile. Tala pressed the star flat with a clean palm and smiled at the smooth shape. “It looks ready,” Tala said softly. Auntie Luz answered, “Your star is ready when you are, and the sponge will be ready when it is.”

Tala wanted to check again, yet they remembered Auntie Luz’s words and left the towel in place. Instead, Tala carried their finished star to the wooden rack and clipped it gently on a low rung so the paper could rest without curling. The clip made a tiny click. Cool air from the open window brushed Tala’s ankles. Tala returned to the table and wiped a speck of paste with the edge of their sleeve, then reached for the linen napkin only long enough to finish the job. The floury towel over the bread sponge did not move. Tala sat on the stool beside Auntie Luz and folded their hands.

From the little sink just beyond the craft corner, the tap let out slow, clear drips. Tala decided to count twelve of them without peeking at the bowl. One. Two. Three. The drips sounded soft and even. Four. Five. Six. Tala kept their eyes on the wooden rack and the hanging cloths. Seven. Eight. Nine. The warm air smelled clean. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Tala exhaled and felt proud of the quiet waiting. Auntie Luz rested a kind hand on Tala’s shoulder for a moment. “That was a gentle way to pass the time,” she said. Tala nodded and stayed close.

Tala cut a second shape, a simple moon from pale yellow paper, and paste dotted the back like tiny pale coins. They pressed the moon onto a scrap of cardboard and smoothed it with careful strokes. The table surface felt cool under Tala’s wrists. Light shifted a little as clouds passed outside, and the craft corner grew softer and grayer for a breath before brightening again. The bread sponge remained under its floury towel, napping in its own slow way. Tala did not lift the edge. They only listened to Auntie Luz’s quiet breathing and to the settled hush of the room. Paper dust sparkled once in a beam of light and then drifted down.

When enough quiet minutes had passed, Auntie Luz rose and stood beside the bowl. “Now we may look together, Tala,” she said. Tala stood too, heart steady. Auntie Luz lifted the floury towel with both hands, and the warm, yeasty air rose gently. The bread sponge had grown soft and high, full of tiny bubbles that caught the light like small moons. Tala clapped once, softly. “It finished while we waited,” Tala whispered. Auntie Luz nodded and laid the towel aside on the wooden rack. The paste jar lid clicked shut. Tala held the blue star and the yellow moon and felt the cool cardboard edges against their palms. The craft corner seemed to breathe with them.

Tala and Auntie Luz washed their hands in lukewarm water that smelled faintly clean, then dried them on a soft cloth from the rack. Tala placed the paper star and moon side by side on the table so the evening light could rest on both. The bread sponge sat uncovered now, ready for the next kind step whenever Auntie Luz chose it. Tala leaned against Auntie Luz’s arm and felt the steady warmth there. Outside, the sky turned a deeper blue. Inside, the room held its peaceful hush. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Tala closed their eyes for a moment and listened to the settled quiet, knowing the sponge had taken the time it needed and that waiting beside Auntie Luz had felt safe and full.
