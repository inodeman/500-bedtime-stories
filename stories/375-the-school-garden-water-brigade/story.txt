The warm smell of beeswax filled the cottage room as Jorie's busy hands rubbed a smooth yellow block carefully along the edge of the long tablecloth, polishing until the fabric caught the light. Soft golden afternoon sun poured through the window and rested on Jorie's bare toes, warming them while paper stars hung motionless overhead like quiet guardians. Jorie worked with steady circles, feeling the cloth grow silkier under their palms, and noticed the two watering cans waiting by the single tap near the deep sink. Ines sat close by, her elbows resting lightly on a chair back, and the baker moved slowly at the far counter, brushing stray flour from his sleeves. Jorie breathed in once more and kept their hands moving in the peaceful hush.

Jorie glanced toward the windowsill where small pots of mint and thyme stood in a neat row beneath the paper stars, their leaves needing water before night fully arrived. The air near the open window felt pleasantly cool against Jorie's arms as they rose from the table and stretched. "Ines, will you help me fill these two watering cans from the tap?" Jorie asked in a low voice that fitted the calm room. Ines stood at once, her skirt making a faint whisper against the long tablecloth as she crossed the floor. The baker looked over with a gentle nod, his hands still dusted pale, while Jorie lifted one can and felt its empty lightness.

Jorie and Ines carried both watering cans to the sink together, the metal cool and smooth beneath Jorie's careful fingers. Jorie understood that rushing at the single tap could send water spilling over the rims and onto the floor near the long tablecloth, so they spoke first. "We can split it into two slow jobs," Jorie said. "I will hold the first can steady while you turn the handle only a little at a time." Ines answered softly, "That sounds perfect, Jorie." Warm water began a gentle trickle when Ines eased the tap, and Jorie watched the level climb inside the can without hurry. Tiny shadows from the paper stars drifted across the tabletop as the sunlight shifted lower.

When the first can reached halfway, Jorie changed places with Ines so both jobs stayed even and unhurried. Jorie now turned the tap in the smallest motions while Ines cradled the second can, and every drop stayed inside. The baker stepped nearer and saw a length of soft twine coiled beside the sink. "You will want a knot to join the handles so you can carry both cans together afterward," the baker said. "I call it the garden loop, and you count slowly to four while you tighten it." Jorie felt the mild steam from the water rise against their wrists like a friendly breath and nodded in thanks.

Jorie practiced the looping motion once in the air, remembering the name. "Garden loop, and count to four," Jorie murmured to keep the words clear. Ines held the twine ends ready while the second can filled drop by quiet drop, the water making a tiny steady music against the metal. The temperature in the cottage room stayed mild and even, wrapping around them like a soft shawl as they worked. Jorie kept their eyes on the rising water and felt completely present in each small turn of the tap.

Soon both watering cans stood full on the counter, cool droplets beading on their sides that Jorie wiped away with a clean cloth. Now they formed the knot. Jorie and Ines stood side by side, guiding the twine through the handles just as the baker had described. "One, two, three, four," Jorie counted aloud while they drew the ends snug and firm. The garden loop held without any slip, and Jorie smiled at the neat join glowing under the paper stars. Through the open window drifted the sound of wind in the fig leaves, a low rustling that brushed past like a lullaby and made Jorie pause with quiet delight.

Jorie took one side of the joined cans and Ines took the other, walking the short path to the windowsill pots. Jorie set their can down gently and reached for a broad mint leaf, turning it to touch a leaf's soft underside with one careful fingertip. The texture felt like the finest cool velvet, and Jorie stroked it once before tilting the can to pour a slow silver stream into the dark soil. Ines matched the pace at the next pot, and the baker watched from beside the long tablecloth with quiet warmth in his eyes. Evening light painted long orange stripes across the fabric and the floor.

All the pots drank their fill without any rush, and Jorie carried the empty cans back to rest beside the closed tap. Then Jorie spotted a small snail beginning its journey across one whole brick on the outer windowsill ledge, its shell catching the last light. Jorie and Ines stood together in stillness and watched the snail travel its silver path for a long peaceful stretch of time, the air growing cooler around their shoulders. "Look how steadily it goes," Ines whispered. Jorie answered, "Just like us with the cans." The baker smiled and held out a warm biscuit for each of them, the crumb soft in Jorie's palm.

After the snail reached the far edge of the brick, Jorie and Ines returned to sit at the long tablecloth under the paper stars once more. Jorie's hands rested quiet and still now, and they listened to the evening settle deeper into the cottage room. The baker lowered the main lamp until only the gentlest lights remained. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Jorie felt the smooth weave of the tablecloth under their resting palms and tasted the mild honey sweetness of the biscuit while a comfortable warmth lingered in the air, everything complete and ready for sleep.
