The blue rain cover lay folded in careful layers over the stack of storybooks on the cottage kitchen table, its smooth surface catching the soft morning light that poured through the open window. Brisa leaned close enough to see the tiny stitched seams and the way the fabric tucked around the corners so no page could slip free. They rested one hand on the top fold and felt the quiet coolness of the cloth against their palm while the rest of the room stayed gently warm from the overnight stove. The books waited underneath like treasure kept dry on purpose, and Brisa stayed there a long moment simply looking, breathing the mild air that smelled faintly of yesterday’s bread.

Cousin Pio stood at the sink with his sleeves rolled up, rinsing the last breakfast cup under a thin stream of water that sang against the porcelain. Brisa noticed the scarf hanging by the door still held the smell of wet wool from Pio’s early walk to the garden gate, earthy and soft in the kind ordinary morning. They stepped across the cool floorboards toward the table again and spoke first. “You put the rain cover over my books before I came down,” Brisa said. Pio turned with a small nod and a quiet smile that needed no big words yet. Golden light rested on the counter beside him and made the edges of the cups gleam.

A tin clock on the high shelf ticked with a steady metal heart, counting the slow seconds while Brisa thought a quick thank-you would feel too thin for such a ready kindness. They wanted to offer one useful thing Cousin Pio had not asked for at all. The empty kettle sat cold on the stove, so Brisa lifted it, filled it at the tap with clear cool water that whispered as it rose, and set it back over the burner. They stood still and breathed while the kettle began its quiet tick of warming metal. Then Brisa took a soft cloth and wiped every crumb from the wooden bread board until the grain felt smooth again under their fingers.

Pio dried his hands on a towel and watched without interrupting. “The cover will keep the pages safe if any mist drifts in later when we open the door,” he said in his calm voice. Brisa answered by sliding one book a little farther under the fold and running a careful finger along a paper edge that had peeked out, feeling its thin crisp line before tucking it away. They picked up the small broom next and swept the mat by the door where a few dry leaves had gathered from Pio’s boots. Warmth from the stove wrapped around Brisa’s shoulders like a second scarf while they worked, and the kitchen stayed peaceful and bright.

Brisa kept sweeping until the floor looked tidy and the leaves rested in a neat little pile ready for the compost pail. “I filled the kettle and cleaned the board and swept the mat,” they said plainly, “because I noticed what you did for the books and a quick thank-you felt too small.” Pio’s eyes crinkled at the corners in the same gentle way the light crinkled across the table. Outside the window the sky held a pale friendly blue, and the rain cover still made perfect picture-book sense for a morning when books might later travel to the porch for reading under the wide eaves if clouds gathered. Brisa set the broom aside and felt the quiet satisfaction of matching care with care.

They poured the now-hot water into two mugs and let the tea bags steep while the steam rose in soft curls that warmed their cheeks. Cousin Pio took his mug and sat at the table near the protected books. “Your noticing makes the whole kitchen feel even kinder,” he told Brisa. They sat too, elbows on the worn wood, and listened to the last faint tick of the kettle cooling down. The air between them stayed easy and ordinary, filled with the small sounds of settling chairs and the distant birds outside the cottage wall. Brisa wrapped both hands around their warm mug and watched the steam dance.

Anouk appeared in the doorway with a basket of fresh eggs from the coop, the shells still holding a bit of morning coolness. “I brought these for later,” Anouk said, setting the basket down carefully so nothing would roll. Brisa smiled and made space on the counter, then told Anouk how the rain cover had already been waiting over the books. Together they washed the eggs under cool running water that sparkled in the light, and Brisa dried each one with a soft towel until the shells felt smooth and clean. The three of them moved around one another like leaves drifting in a slow stream, never hurrying, always leaving room.

Brisa returned to the table and rested both palms flat on either side of the folded rain cover, feeling again how thoughtfully it had been placed. They spoke once more so the words would sit clear in the room. “I saw that you took care of my books first thing, Cousin Pio, and I wanted you to know I noticed.” Pio reached over and gave Brisa’s shoulder a light friendly pat that felt steady and warm. Anouk hummed a quiet tune while arranging the eggs in a bowl, and the whole cottage kitchen glowed with the simple brightness of an ordinary kind morning that asked for nothing more than gentle noticing and matching helpful hands.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Brisa watched its calm light join the daylight on the rain cover and the books beneath, then finished the last warm sip of tea while the tin clock kept its distant company and the smell of wet wool faded into the larger comfort of home. They stayed seated a little longer with Cousin Pio and Anouk, listening to the quiet house settle around them, feeling the smooth table under their arms, and letting the peaceful morning stretch like a soft blanket ready for the rest of a slow good day.
