Soft morning mist pressed gently against the kitchen window panes and left tiny cool beads that caught the first pale gold light. Quinn sat at the kitchen table before breakfast with Mama, their chairs drawn so close that the wooden seats nearly touched and Quinn could feel the steady warmth of Mama’s arm nearby. The tabletop felt smooth and slightly cool under Quinn’s palms. A soft gray cat collar bell tinkled once from the hallway as the house cat wandered past, then fell quiet again. Quinn’s fingers traced a faint groove in the wood while the smell of warm toast began to drift from the counter. They had bigger news bubbling up inside, yet the quiet kitchen held them still for a moment longer.

Mama poured two cups of warm milk and set them down with a soft clink that sounded friendly in the early light. “I want to tell you about the rule of the puzzle table,” Mama said in her calm morning voice. Quinn’s toes curled inside their slippers at the sound of those words, because the rule had always seemed fussy whenever anyone mentioned it before. The kitchen air felt pleasantly cool against Quinn’s cheeks. They watched Mama’s hands move slowly and wanted to burst out with their own story, yet they pressed their lips together and listened. Steam rose in thin curly shapes from the cups and brushed Quinn’s nose with a gentle heat.

“The rule said that every single piece had to stay on the table until the whole picture was finished, and no one could scoop leftover pieces into a box early even if they felt impatient,” Mama continued while the light through the window brightened by slow degrees. Quinn nodded and felt the soft fabric of their sleeve against their wrist. The rule still sounded picky, like something that might slow everything down. Mama’s voice stayed even and low. Quinn breathed in and caught a brief smell of soapy wool from the clean sweater draped over the chair back, fresh from yesterday’s wash. They kept their bigger news tucked away and watched Mama’s face instead.

Mama reached for the little pile of breakfast spoons and began to slide them gently across the wood. “It also said you had to wait until the person beside you had finished placing their piece and had taken one quiet breath before you touched the next one,” she said. Quinn heard the faint scrape of metal on wood and felt curious. The kitchen stayed hushed except for that small sound. Quinn’s own hands stayed folded in their lap even though their feet wanted to swing. The chairs stayed close, and Mama’s knee bumped Quinn’s once in a friendly way that felt warm through their pajamas.

“People used to think the rule was too careful and too slow,” Mama went on, lining the spoons from longest to shortest so they made a neat shiny row. Quinn watched the silver shapes catch little sparks of morning light. The table surface felt solid and steady under their elbows. A leather lace from Mama’s old notebook dangled over the edge of her chair and brushed Quinn’s fingers with its smooth cool texture when they reached to straighten it. Quinn still held the bigger news inside like a bright marble they were not yet ready to show. They listened all the way, eyes on Mama’s calm mouth.

“But at the very end, when the last piece clicked into place and the whole picture shone under the lamp, everyone saw how the waiting had kept every color safe and every edge ready,” Mama said, and her voice grew softer still. “No piece had rolled away, and no one had felt rushed, so the finished puzzle looked brighter than anyone expected.” Quinn felt the gentle heat of the milk cup when they wrapped both hands around it. The kitchen smelled faintly of butter now. Mama paused and looked at Quinn with quiet eyes that invited one question and nothing more.

Quinn took a slow breath that tasted like warm milk and asked the one real question that had been waiting. “Did the rule make the puzzle take a very long time, or did it just feel that way at first?” Mama smiled and answered, “It only felt long at the beginning. Once everyone settled into the quiet turns, the time floated by like a soft cloud, and the end arrived sooner than anyone guessed.” Quinn heard the soft drip of the kitchen faucet and felt the cool air from the window touch the back of their neck. They nodded because the answer settled gently inside them like a feather.

Only then did Quinn share something small of their own. “Yesterday Jaleh and I found three smooth pebbles by the garden step and lined them up by size the same way you lined the spoons,” they said, voice quiet as the morning light. Mama’s hand rested lightly on Quinn’s shoulder, warm through the sweater. Quinn felt proud of the small share and glad they had waited. The spoons gleamed in their neat row. Outside, the mist had thinned so that pale sun laid soft squares of light across the floorboards near their chairs.

Quinn leaned a little closer to Mama while the last coolness of night slipped away from the room. The chairs stayed near each other, and the lined spoons rested like a tiny silver path. Quinn’s bigger news could wait for another gentle hour because this small sharing already felt full enough. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
