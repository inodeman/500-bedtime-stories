"We should set the stones slowly so none tip," Tamsin said, their voice soft in the dim bakery kitchen where the early table still waited bare and the air held a gentle warmth from the cooled ovens.

Tamsin crouched low beside the first flat gray stone and ran one palm along its cool edge while Lior stood ready with the next piece. The kitchen tiles felt smooth under Tamsin’s knees, and a thin ribbon of morning light slipped through the high window to rest on the flour-dusted counter. Ms. Okonkwo stood near the big mixing bowls, her apron already dusted white, and watched the two children with quiet eyes. Tamsin knew the stepping stones had to sit level across the wide kitchen floor so the bakers could walk the short path to the early table without carrying flour on their shoes. Lior waited for Tamsin’s nod before lowering the second stone. The air smelled faintly of yesterday’s bread, and Tamsin breathed it in while they checked the first gap with careful fingers.

Tamsin remembered that rushing would make the stones rock and slide. If one person hurried the whole path, the edges would sit uneven and the work would need starting again. Tamsin looked at Lior and spoke clearly. “You place each stone while I watch from low and call when it sits true.” Lior answered, “I will move only when you say the word.” Ms. Okonkwo smiled but stayed back, offering no hands, only the soft light from the wall lamp that painted gold circles on the floor. Tamsin pressed their cheek close to the stone surface and felt its solid chill. The bakery kitchen stayed peaceful, full of the quiet tick of the wall clock and the far-off hush of the street outside.

They split the work into two slow jobs right away. Tamsin stayed low on the floor, eyes level with each stone, while Lior lifted and settled every piece with both hands. Tamsin would whisper “stop” or “a little left,” and Lior would shift the weight by the smallest measure. The third stone came down with a soft scrape that Tamsin heard clearly. Warmth from the nearby proofing shelves brushed Tamsin’s arms as they worked. Ms. Okonkwo stepped closer once and said, “The knot you need for the measuring string is called a reef knot.” Tamsin repeated the name under their breath and tied the short cord that would help them check the heights. The string felt smooth between Tamsin’s fingers as they looped it.

Tamsin and Lior moved to the middle of the path. The smell of honey rose from a small open jar on the counter where Ms. Okonkwo had set out the morning glazes, sweet and golden in the growing light. Tamsin paused to notice it, then returned to the stones. Lior lowered the fifth stone while Tamsin kept their head near the floor and studied the gap. “It needs a thin wooden shim under the far corner,” Tamsin said. Lior fetched the small scrap and slid it into place. The stone settled with a quiet finality. Ms. Okonkwo counted softly from her place by the bowls, “One, two, three,” giving them a steady rhythm without touching the path. Tamsin’s chalky hand brushed the edge of a stone after they had tested a white mark left from earlier measuring, the dry powdery feel grounding them in the careful work.

A gate latch clicked once in the back courtyard, a clear metal sound that drifted through the open kitchen door and reminded Tamsin the day was still beginning. They waited through a single chime from the bakery clock before placing the next stone. Lior held the sixth piece steady while Tamsin checked from low. The light had grown a little brighter now, painting pale squares across the finished stones. Tamsin felt the cool kitchen air on their neck and smiled when the stone sat true. “We are more than halfway,” Lior said, and Tamsin answered, “We keep the same slow pace.” Ms. Okonkwo only nodded and named the reef knot again so Tamsin could retie the measuring string that had loosened. The two children worked side by side in the calm space before the early table.

Tamsin guided the seventh and eighth stones with the same patient care. Their knees rested on a folded cloth that felt soft against the hard floor. Lior’s movements stayed measured, never sudden. The path began to look like a gentle river of gray leading toward the empty early table. Tamsin smelled the honey once more as a breeze from the door stirred the air, then returned their full attention to the level line. Ms. Okonkwo offered the count again, her voice like a soft drumbeat that helped them keep time. Tamsin pressed the string across two neighboring stones and saw they matched. The kitchen remained quiet except for the small sounds of stone against tile and the children’s careful breathing.

The last two stones needed the same slow jobs. Tamsin stayed low, chin nearly touching the floor, while Lior set each one. “Left a finger’s width,” Tamsin called, and Lior adjusted. The final stone settled into place with a gentle hush. Tamsin ran their chalky hand along the completed path one last time, feeling the even surface and the dry powder that still clung lightly to their skin. Ms. Okonkwo stepped forward at last and said, “You finished together.” Tamsin and Lior stood, brushed their clothes, and looked at the level stepping stones that now waited for the bakers. The early table stood ready beyond the path, still empty but no longer far. Warmth from the ovens that would soon wake filled the corners of the room.

Tamsin and Lior walked the new path once, side by side, their steps light and sure on the level stones. The bakery kitchen glowed with soft morning light, and the air held the lingering sweetness of honey along with the clean scent of flour. Ms. Okonkwo began to lay out the first trays near the early table while the two children watched in happy quiet. Tamsin felt the smooth cool of the final stone under their shoe and knew the work had been done without hurry. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the gate latch rested silent again, and the whole kitchen seemed to breathe with them as the day began.
