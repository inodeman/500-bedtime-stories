The tin clock on the shelf ticked once, soft and clear in the still room. Kito opened their eyes and heard that small bright sound again. Cool air brushed their cheeks like a gentle breath from outside. Frost had drawn white lace across the bedroom window, and pale morning light sifted through it. Kito sat up slowly and rubbed their eyes with warm palms. They listened a moment longer, then swung their feet over the edge of the bed. The floorboards felt smooth and chilly under their toes. Kito stood, stretched, and walked toward the bright glass with quiet steps, still holding the quiet of sleep.

At the window Kito pressed their nose near the cold pane and breathed a soft fog circle. Outside, Auntie June had left the garden hose dripping near the flower boxes so the winter greens would drink before the day warmed. The fine mist rose in the frosty air and caught the low sun. Inside that mist Kito saw a small rainbow, thin as a ribbon and bright with rose and lemon and soft blue. The colors hung just beyond the glass, trembling a little with each drip. Kito’s fingers itched with the wish to open the window and pocket the rainbow or poke it gently to see if it would bounce. The glass stayed cold under their palms. Kito whispered, “Look at you, little rainbow,” and kept both hands still on the sill.

A soft knock came at the door, and Hadi stepped in wearing thick socks that made no sound. Hadi smelled faintly of toast from the kitchen and carried a wool scarf. Kito waved them over without turning from the glass. “Come see, but walk slow,” Kito said. Hadi crossed the room and stood shoulder to shoulder with Kito. Together they watched the mist rise and the tiny rainbow hold its place in the cold sparkle. The light through the frost made tiny diamonds on the quilt folded at the foot of the bed. Kito pointed with one careful finger that never touched the glass. “It lives in the hose mist,” Kito said. “If we poke it or try to keep it, the colors might go away.”

Hadi nodded and kept their hands in their pockets. They both leaned closer until their breath made twin clouds on the pane. The rainbow shifted a thumb’s width when a breeze stirred the mist, then settled again. Kito showed Hadi how to tip their head left and right so the colors stayed clear without any touch. “We just look,” Kito said softly. “That is enough.” Hadi smiled and answered, “It is prettier when it stays where it is.” The room felt warmer behind them where the heater whispered, yet the window kept its fresh chill. Outside a bird hopped along the fence and left no sound that reached them.

Auntie June appeared in the doorway with a tray and the clean smell of lavender rising from the small cloth she had tucked beside the cups. She set the tray on the low table and joined them at the glass. Her cardigan sleeves brushed Kito’s arm with soft wool. “What have you found this morning?” she asked. Kito answered, “A rainbow in the hose mist. We are leaving it right there.” Auntie June bent so her eyes met the same height as theirs. She watched the colors glow and fade and glow again in the silver air. “How kind of you to keep it safe by looking only,” she said. The three of them stood in a quiet half-circle, shoulders almost touching, while the mist kept spinning its thin bright band.

Kito felt the wish to reach out rise once more, then let it drift away like steam. They rested one hand on the quilt folded nearby and traced a single raised quilt stitch with one fingertip, feeling its neat bump and the smooth cotton around it. The stitch held firm and friendly under their touch. Hadi watched the shadow of the window latch move the width of a thumb across the sill as the sun climbed a little higher. No one spoke for a long breath. The rainbow stayed whole. Auntie June poured warm milk into three cups, and the steam curled up smelling faintly of the lavender cloth still resting on the tray. Kito took a cup and held it so the heat warmed their fingers without spilling.

They sipped and watched together. Kito told Hadi again how the mist had to stay free so the colors could live. Hadi repeated the words in a hush, practicing the same gentle looking. Auntie June rested a hand on each child’s shoulder and said nothing more, only smiled at the bright strip hanging in the cold morning. Frost began to thin at the edges of the glass where the room’s warmth touched it, yet the rainbow held steady in the hose spray beyond. Kito felt proud of the quiet choice in a soft way that needed no words. The tin clock had already spoken its earlier tick; now only the soft sip of milk and the far drip of water filled the air.

Later Kito sat on the rug with Hadi while Auntie June opened the curtain a little wider so the light could paint the floor gold. They drew the rainbow with crayons on scrap paper, careful never to claim the real one. Kito chose soft pinks and greens and left white space around the arc the way the mist left space around the true colors. Hadi added tiny dots of blue. When the drawings were done, Kito carried them to the sill and set them beside the glass so the paper rainbows could keep the living one company from inside. The air near the window stayed cool, and Kito’s cheeks felt the same coolness whenever they leaned close again to check. The small rainbow still floated, safe and free.

Kito climbed back onto the bed when the milk cups stood empty and the morning grew brighter. They pulled the quilt up to their chin and felt sleep returning in slow waves. Hadi curled in the big chair with the scarf over their knees. Auntie June hummed a tune without words while she folded the lavender cloth. Outside the hose mist thinned as the sun climbed, yet for one last look the rainbow flashed clear and kind. Kito closed their eyes and kept the picture of it behind their lids. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
