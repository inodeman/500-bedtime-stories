Grandma Nia was already bent over the long wooden bench in the market lane, her sleeves rolled and a limp cloth bunched in her hands. Cool patches of water from the afternoon flower watering still shone on the seat, and the cloth only pushed the damp around instead of clearing it. Rows of woven baskets, tall honey jars, and clay flower pots lined both sides of the lane under a soft amber light that made every surface look gentle. Cosima walked up the packed earth path and felt the mild warmth of the stones under their shoes. Grandma Nia pressed harder, and the bench still looked shiny and useless for sitting.

Cosima stopped beside a stack of empty baskets and watched Grandma Nia shift her feet for better balance. The scent of warm bread drifted from a nearby stall where a baker stacked loaves on a wooden board. Cosima breathed it in and saw how the cloth kept catching on the grain of the wood. “Grandma Nia, that bench looks too big for one pair of hands,” Cosima said. Grandma Nia glanced up with a tired smile but kept working the wet spot near the middle. Cosima reached for a second dry cloth hanging from a basket handle and felt its soft folds against their fingers.

Without waiting for an answer, Cosima knelt at the far end of the bench and began wiping in slow steady circles. The wood felt slick at first, then slowly turned matte under the cloth. Grandma Nia noticed and moved her own cloth toward the center so the two halves met. “You found the other cloth so quickly,” Grandma Nia said. Cosima just nodded and kept drying the legs and the seat edge where droplets had gathered. The market lane stayed quiet except for the soft scrape of cloth on wood, and the golden light made the flower pots glow like little lanterns of their own.

They worked side by side until half the bench no longer held any water. Cosima paused to shake their cloth out and listened to the wind in the fig leaves that hung over the far wall of the lane. The leaves made a dry rustling sound like paper being turned gently. Grandma Nia finished her half and sat back on her heels. “The middle is almost done now,” Grandma Nia said. Cosima smiled and reached across to catch the last few beads before they could roll back. The air cooled a little as the sun slid lower, and Cosima felt the change on their cheeks while they smoothed the final stretch of wood.

A tall ceramic jar stood near Cosima’s knee, its surface painted with blue leaves. Cosima rested one hand on the cool ceramic rim while they waited for Grandma Nia to check the underside of the bench. The rim felt smooth and solid under their palm. “Shall we wipe the legs one more time just to be sure?” Cosima asked. Grandma Nia nodded and handed over her cloth so Cosima could finish the nearest leg. Together they turned the bench dry and ready, and Cosima never once asked if the help was noticed. The baskets nearby held the faint sweetness of dried herbs, and Cosima liked how the whole lane smelled settled and calm.

Grandma Nia stood and brushed her skirt. Cosima folded both cloths into neat squares and set them on the edge of a flower pot. An empty bowl sat on a low stool where someone had mixed water for the plants earlier. Cosima picked up the bowl, carried it a few steps to the small sink built into the stall wall, rinsed it, and brought it back empty and clean. “There, now nothing is left wet,” Cosima said. Grandma Nia placed a hand on Cosima’s shoulder for a moment and then patted the dry bench. The two of them sat down together and felt the solid wood hold them without a single damp spot.

They stayed seated while the market lane grew quieter. A few last customers walked past with bags of jars and pots, their footsteps soft on the earth. Cosima leaned against Grandma Nia’s arm and watched the light turn from gold to soft rose. Grandma Nia pointed to a row of baskets that still needed stacking. Cosima hopped down, lifted two light baskets, and set them neatly against the wall. Then they returned to the bench and sat again, feeling the dry wood under their legs. The evening air held a gentle coolness that made Cosima pull their sweater sleeves down a little.

Grandma Nia rose and gathered the cleaned cloths. Cosima walked with her toward the end of the lane where the flower pots stood in tidy groups. They paused once more by the fig tree and listened to the quiet settling of the place. Cosima touched one last basket handle just to feel its woven ridges. Grandma Nia locked the small stall gate, and together they started the short walk home under the first pale stars. Cosima kept pace close beside Grandma Nia and felt the comfortable tiredness that comes after steady work done side by side.

At home Cosima washed their hands at the kitchen basin and dried them on a soft towel. Grandma Nia poured warm milk into two cups and set them on the table near the window. Cosima climbed into the wide chair by the sill and held the cup carefully. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Cosima watched the small flame behind the glass and felt the room grow still and safe while Grandma Nia sat nearby with her own cup, both of them quiet in the gentle light.
