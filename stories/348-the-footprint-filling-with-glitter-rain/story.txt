The little hollow pressed into the dark garden soil held its shape like a cup, and fine rain slid into it drop by drop until the heel shone first and then the long middle and the toe, each new drop sending a quiet ripple across the surface that caught the frosty morning light and turned it silver. From the bedroom window Gita leaned so close that the cold glass touched their cheek, and they could see every grain of soil around the rim and the way the water trembled without spilling. The garden just below the sill looked soft and gray-blue in that early light, and the air that slipped through the tiny crack at the window edge felt clean and chilled against Gita’s nose.

Gita kept watching while the water rose a little higher, then turned and whispered so the sound stayed soft inside the room. “Maren, come see the footprint filling up,” Gita said. Maren padded over on quiet feet and rested their elbows on the wide sill beside Gita, and together they stared at the shining pool that now mirrored a pale scrap of sky. The window glass felt smooth and cool under Gita’s palms, and the frosty light made tiny bright lines on the water as if someone had drawn them with a thin silver pencil.

For a moment Gita thought how easy it would be to open the window, lean out, and poke one careful finger into the little pool, or even scoop a bit of the shining water into a jar to keep. The wish sat warm and real in Gita’s chest. Yet Gita only breathed on the glass again and said, “If we poke it the edges will blur and the rain will spill away.” Maren nodded slowly, eyes still fixed on the footprint, and the room around them held the gentle hush of early morning while the fine rain kept its slow work outside.

Gita showed Maren how to look without disturbing anything at all by simply staying still and letting their eyes travel the whole outline, from the deep curve of the heel to the five faint marks where toes had pressed. “See how the water sits higher on this side,” Gita said, pointing with a careful finger that never left the glass. Maren answered, “It looks like a tiny lake just for us,” and both children smiled at the thought while the frosty light shifted a little brighter across the garden soil and warmed the air near the window by the smallest degree.

Auntie June stepped into the bedroom with quiet steps and came to stand behind them, resting one hand lightly on the back of Gita’s chair. “What have you found this morning?” Auntie June asked in a low voice that matched the calm of the room. Gita explained about the footprint and the fine rain filling it, and how they had chosen only to watch, and Auntie June leaned forward so she could see the silver pool too. The three of them stayed there in a comfortable cluster, and the soft light from outside made a pale rectangle on the wooden floorboards that felt cool when Gita shifted one socked foot into it.

They spoke a little more while the rain continued its gentle filling. “We can leave it exactly where it is,” Gita said, and Auntie June answered, “Yes, that way it keeps its own quiet story.” Maren added that the water looked almost like it was breathing when the drops landed, and Gita felt glad that no one had rushed outside to touch or take. The temperature near the window stayed pleasantly cool, and the children watched a thin shadow from the curtain edge move the width of a thumb across the sill as the morning grew slowly brighter.

Later Gita noticed the faint smell of lavender drifting from the little cloth sachet Auntie June had set on the dresser the night before, and the scent mixed with the clean cold air in a way that made the whole room feel settled and kind. Maren rested their chin on folded hands and kept gazing out, and Gita did the same, both of them content to let the footprint hold its shining water without any help from fingers or jars. The garden soil around the print looked darker where the rain had darkened it, and the frosty light kept polishing the surface of the tiny pool so that it never quite looked the same from one breath to the next.

A tin clock on the bedside table gave one clear soft tick that marked the quiet passing of the moment, and Gita heard it like a friendly reminder that there was no hurry at all. Auntie June smoothed the blanket at the foot of the bed, and Gita’s fingers found a quilt stitch that felt slightly raised and familiar under the tip of one thumb, a small neat ridge of thread that had been sewn long ago and still held firm. They stayed by the window a while longer, three quiet watchers, while the fine rain finished its slow filling and the footprint shone complete and undisturbed in the garden soil below.

Gita finally leaned back and felt the calm of the morning settle all the way through them, knowing the little pool would stay right where it belonged for as long as the rain and the light allowed. Maren stretched once and smiled, and Auntie June gave both children a gentle pat on the shoulder before she moved toward the door. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the footprint rested full and bright, and inside the bedroom the air stayed cool and peaceful while Gita and Maren watched the last pale sparkles dance across the water they had chosen only to see.
