The evening breeze touched the bakery windows with a soft coolness, and pale gold light from the street lamps lay quietly along the glass. Sora stood outside with their coat still warm from the walk, watching the bright counter that faced the street. Inside, people already sat on little stools, and a story voice had begun to rise and fall. The tin clock on the wall ticked a steady, friendly beat that Sora could hear even through the door. Sora’s mitten brushed the cool brass handle, and the air smelled faintly of sugar and clean wood.

Sora felt their cheeks grow warm in the soft evening chill. Through the glass they saw the ferryman seated near the counter, his river-coat folded beside him, speaking in a calm voice to the small circle. The story had already started, and Sora’s feet wanted to stay outside where no one would notice. The polished pebble Sora kept in their pocket felt smooth and round when their fingers closed around it for comfort. A lamp inside threw gentle yellow light across the flour-dusted counter, and Sora took one slow breath of the cool outdoor air before deciding what to do.

Instead of turning away or pretending not to care, Sora tried the smallest brave version they could manage. They opened the door just wide enough for their shoulders and stepped onto the warm wooden floor. “I am here,” Sora said in a voice that could be heard, though it was soft as a feather. The ferryman looked up with kind eyes and did not ask Sora to perform or speak loudly. Warm bakery air wrapped around Sora’s face, carrying the bright green smell of basil from a little pot of leaves on the counter, and Sora felt the heat of the ovens reach their hands.

The ferryman smiled the way river water smiles when it is calm. “Welcome, Sora. There is room right here,” he said, patting the empty stool beside the counter that faced the street. He did not push for a show or ask Sora to catch up all at once. Sora sat down, and the stool felt steady under them. Soft light from the street lamps pooled on the countertop, and a tray of cooling rolls sent up a gentle heat that touched Sora’s wrists. Somewhere behind the counter a kettle gave a tiny peaceful hiss.

Sora’s friend Lumen, who had arrived earlier, slid a small stack of cloth napkins across the wood. The napkins had a stitched pear on each corner, and Sora began to fold them so the stitched pear faced up, the cloth soft and slightly warm from resting near the ovens. “I missed the beginning,” Sora whispered, still holding the polished pebble in one pocket like a quiet friend. Lumen answered, “We can tell you the parts you need,” in a voice as gentle as the ticking of the tin clock that kept time above the shelves. The basil smell rose again when someone brushed the little plant while reaching for cups.

The ferryman continued the story without making Sora the center of anything. He spoke of a quiet boat that carried moonlight across still water, and his words moved slowly so that latecomers could step into them. “The boat waited for whoever needed it,” the ferryman said, glancing once at Sora with the same kindness as before. Sora listened, fingers folding another napkin, and the warm bakery light made the counter glow like a small stage that asked for nothing. Outside, the street sounded distant and peaceful, and the cool glass of the front window held the evening gently against the room’s heat.

Sora’s shoulders loosened. They placed one finished napkin on the pile and felt the smooth texture of the polished pebble again when they reached into their pocket for a moment of quiet. “Thank you for saving the stool,” Sora said, looking at the ferryman. He nodded and answered, “Stories like company at every part.” No one stared, and no one asked Sora to retell what they had missed. The tin clock kept its soft regular sound, and the air stayed filled with bread warmth and the green note of basil leaves. Light from the street lamps slipped across Sora’s shoes in slow silver shapes.

When the story paused for cups of warm milk, Sora helped set the folded napkins in a neat row so every stitched pear faced the ceiling. Lumen passed Sora a small cup, and the ceramic felt pleasantly hot against Sora’s palms. The ferryman remained easy and kind, never urging Sora to speak more than they wished. “I like the boat part,” Sora offered in that same clear, small voice, and the ferryman’s smile deepened. Outside the counter that faced the street, a quiet bicycle bell rang once far away, and inside the bakery the temperature stayed gentle and even, wrapping everyone like a soft blanket.

Later, when the circle grew quiet and eyelids grew heavy, Sora rested both hands on the warm counter wood and felt glad they had walked in. The ferryman packed his river-coat without hurry, and Lumen stacked the last clean cups. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Sora slipped the polished pebble back into their pocket, breathed the last faint basil in the air, and listened one more time to the tin clock’s calm tick while the bakery lights dimmed to a sleepy gold.
