"The water looks so still from here," Pema said, leaning on the smooth wooden rail inside the harbor shelter on the lee side of the wind, where the air felt cool and gentle against their cheeks.

Outside, the harbor lay calm and silver under the late afternoon light, and the shelter smelled faintly of dry rope and old boards as Pema watched small boats rock without hurry. Neighbor Sami sat at the wide table near the open side, a sheet of pale paper spread flat, and the soft scrape of a pencil had already stopped. Pema stepped closer and saw that a pencil line had been erased gently so the paper did not roughen, the mark gone as if it had never pressed hard. Neighbor Sami’s hands rested quiet on either side of the sheet, and the light from the doorway made the clean paper glow like milk. Pema felt glad to be there with the steady boards under their feet and the hush of the protected side of the harbor holding everything soft.

Pema traced the edge of the table with one fingertip and noticed how the erased place looked smooth and kind, not scratched or fuzzy the way a hurried rub might leave it. Neighbor Sami had taken care already, before Pema arrived, and the paper still felt even when Pema brushed it lightly. A quick thank-you rose in Pema’s mind, yet it felt too thin for the careful work that left no roughness behind. The air inside the shelter stayed mild, and far off the sound of wind in the fig leaves drifted in like a slow whisper through the open wall. Pema breathed in and decided to do one useful thing Neighbor Sami had not asked for, something simple that would sit beside the gentle erasing like a matching kindness.

Near the corner of the shelter a small basket held fruit for later, and Pema lifted two pears, turning them so the cut sides faced down on a clean cloth. The smell of pear skins rose sweet and green, fresh as morning grass after rain, and Pema set a third pear upright so it would not roll. Neighbor Sami glanced over once but said nothing, only smiled with quiet eyes. Pema also noticed the rug by the doorway had a corner kicked crooked from earlier footsteps, so they straightened the rug corner with a toe until the edge lay straight and neat against the boards. The wood floor felt cool through their sock, and the small act settled something warm in Pema’s chest.

Pema returned to the table and stood near Neighbor Sami, watching the clean paper catch the shifting light that came through the lee-side opening. “I saw what you did with the pencil line,” Pema said. “You erased it so gently the paper stayed smooth.” Neighbor Sami nodded once, the motion slow, and the shelter filled with the low creak of a boat fender brushing a post outside. Pema kept their voice plain and steady. “It mattered to me that you took care like that.” The words felt solid in the air, better than a thin thank-you tossed away, and Pema rested both hands on the table edge where the grain ran like tiny rivers under their palms.

Niko, who had been stacking spare coils of soft line near the far wall, came closer and set a warm coin into Pema’s open hand for no reason except friendship, the metal smooth and heated from Niko’s pocket. Pema closed their fingers around it and felt the gentle weight, then placed the coin on the table beside the paper so it would not roll away. “The shelter feels nicer when things are tended,” Niko said softly, and Pema agreed with a small nod. Outside the protected lee, the harbor light turned gold along the water, and inside the boards still held the day’s mild warmth. Pema looked again at the erased place and felt the paper’s even surface under one careful fingertip, glad the mark had left no scar.

Neighbor Sami folded the top edge of the sheet once, neat and slow, then unfolded it again so the page lay ready for whatever might come next. “I like when someone notices the quiet work,” Neighbor Sami said, voice low as the lap of water against the shelter piles. Pema smiled and pulled a chair closer, the legs whispering across the floor without scrape. The three of them sat for a stretch of easy minutes while the light grew softer and the air inside stayed kind. Pema kept the warm coin in mind even after it rested on the table, the memory of its texture lingering like a small held secret. No one rushed, and the harbor shelter on the lee side of the wind kept its calm like a cupped hand around a candle flame.

Pema rose once more and checked the pears on the cloth, turning one so its stem pointed the same way as the others, a tiny order that pleased the eye. The smell of the fruit had already done its quiet work earlier, and now only the clean air remained, cool and resting. Neighbor Sami sharpened the pencil with a small knife that made no loud sound, only a faint hush of wood against blade, and set the tool down carefully. “Tomorrow we can draw the boats if you like,” Neighbor Sami offered, and Pema answered, “I would like that very much.” Niko hummed a short tune without words, the sound blending with the distant water, and Pema felt the day folding itself neatly toward evening inside the safe wooden walls.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Pema sat back in the chair and listened to the quiet settle thicker around the harbor shelter, the boards cool now under their bare feet where the socks had slipped. Neighbor Sami covered the paper with a light cloth so dust would not find it, and Niko dimmed nothing because the lantern already gave enough gentle gold. Pema held the thought of the smooth erased line and the straightened rug and the plain words spoken aloud, all of them resting easy together like boats tied side by side. Outside, the lee side kept the stronger gusts away, and inside the three friends shared the mild air and the soft light until eyelids grew heavy and the harbor rocked itself toward sleep.
