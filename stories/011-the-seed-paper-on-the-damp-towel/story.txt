Mama stood at the long wooden counter in the bakery kitchen, her hands already deep in a soft mound of dough that needed more resting time before the jam jars could be opened later. Warm golden light from the low lamps pooled across the flour-dusted surface, and Brisa stepped closer to feel the gentle heat rising from the big oven that still held yesterday’s gentle warmth. The air carried a quiet hush broken only by the soft pat of Mama’s palms, and Brisa watched the dough stretch and fold like a slow cloud. Nearby, a clean damp towel covered a special sheet of seed paper that Mama had set out earlier so the tiny seeds could soften and settle. Brisa breathed in the cozy space and felt glad to be there beside Mama while everything waited to finish in its own calm way.

Brisa leaned on the counter edge and looked at the neat square of damp towel, curious about the seed paper resting underneath it like a secret letter waiting for morning. The kitchen windows stood open just a little, letting in the cool evening air that brushed Brisa’s bare arms with a pleasant chill. “Mama, may I lift the towel and see if the seeds look ready yet?” Brisa asked in a soft voice that barely stirred the flour dust. Mama smiled without stopping her slow kneading and answered, “Not quite yet, Brisa. The paper still needs more quiet time on the damp towel.” Brisa nodded and kept their hands at their sides, noticing how the dough under Mama’s fingers made a faint sticky sound that felt friendly and safe.

Brisa walked a few steps to the side table where a straw mat lay rolled out for resting trays, and they pressed one palm flat against its dry woven surface to feel the tiny ridges that tickled their skin. The mat smelled faintly clean from the morning sweep, and Brisa sat cross-legged on it for a moment while Mama kept working the dough. Outside the open window the fig tree stirred, and Brisa heard the wind in the fig leaves making a soft rustling whisper that sounded like quiet pages turning. “I really want to peek just once,” Brisa said, rocking a little on the mat. Mama wiped her hands on her apron and came over to sit nearby, saying, “I know the waiting feels long, Brisa, but the seeds grow best when we leave the towel in place until they are truly ready.”

Brisa stood again and drifted back toward the covered seed paper, their bare feet cool on the smooth kitchen floor that held the day’s leftover warmth. They hovered one hand above the damp towel but did not touch it, remembering Mama’s words and the gentle way the dough still needed time too. The jam pots sat sealed along the shelf, their glass sides catching the lamp light in rosy glints, and Brisa decided the seed paper deserved the same patient care. “I will leave it covered,” Brisa said aloud, feeling the choice settle comfortably in their chest like a warm blanket. Mama nodded and returned to her dough, the soft slap of flour and water filling the air once more while Brisa looked around for something quiet to do with their hands.

Brisa spotted a small wooden cup that held measuring spoons near the edge of the counter, and they carefully turned the cup so the handle faced the open room instead of the wall. The wood felt smooth under their fingers, and a mild scent of cedar drifted up from the nearby spice box that Mama kept for special baking days. Brisa smiled at the tidy new position of the cup and wiped a light dusting of flour from the counter with a clean cloth, moving slowly so nothing hurried. The kitchen stayed peaceful, the dough resting under a cloth of its own now, and the seed paper stayed hidden under its damp towel just as it should. Brisa hummed a tiny tune under their breath that matched the distant rustle still coming through the window.

Mama poured a little warm water into a bowl for washing her hands, and the steam rose in a thin white curl that brushed Brisa’s cheek with damp heat when they stood close. “Would you like to help me set the jam jars in a neat row while we wait?” Mama asked. Brisa answered, “Yes, Mama, that sounds perfect,” and they lifted each jar with both hands, feeling the cool glass against their palms and smelling the sweet fruit sealed inside. One by one the jars lined up like colorful stones along the back shelf, catching soft reflections from the lamps. Brisa checked the seed paper only with their eyes from a respectful distance, proud that the damp towel had not been lifted even once.

Time drifted like flour floating in a shaft of light, and Brisa found another quiet task by folding extra clean towels into soft squares and stacking them on the straw mat they had touched earlier. The fabric felt thick and comforting under their fingers, and each fold made a small whisper of cotton against cotton. Outside, the wind in the fig leaves kept its gentle song, and inside the bakery kitchen the dough continued its silent rise under its own cover. Brisa glanced at Mama, who was now wiping the counter in slow circles, and felt the same peaceful rhythm settle into their own shoulders. “The paper must be almost ready by now,” Brisa murmured, yet they still kept both hands busy with the towels instead of reaching for the damp cloth.

At last Mama checked the clock on the wall and gave a quiet nod that made Brisa’s eyes brighten. Together they stepped to the covered seed paper, and Mama lifted one corner of the damp towel so Brisa could see. The sheet had softened just right, the tiny seeds nestled firmly in place like little stars on a night field, and the paper felt cool and perfect when Brisa touched only the edge with one careful fingertip. “It finished in its own time,” Mama said with a warm smile. Brisa answered, “I am glad we waited,” and they both stood in the gentle kitchen light feeling the good quiet that comes after patience well spent.

Brisa helped Mama carry the finished seed paper to a dry tray near the window while the dough and jam stayed behind to complete their own slow work. The evening air cooled the room a little more, and Brisa rubbed their arms to keep the pleasant chill from settling too deep. They looked once more at the neat rows of jars and the resting dough and the tidy cup whose handle still faced the room. Everything felt complete and kind. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
