Idris heard a tiny silver jingle float through the open bedroom door while the blankets still felt warm against their arms. The soft chime of a cat's collar bell rang once more and then faded into the cool night air that slipped across the floorboards. Idris turned their head on the pillow and listened until the house grew quiet again, and the memory of the afternoon at the water rose up clear and gentle. Earlier that day the sky had held a pale blue light, and Idris had walked with Felix along the old stones that made the harbor wall. The tide had gone far out, leaving wet sand and quiet pools, and the air carried a faint green scent that made Idris breathe deeper.

Idris and Felix stepped carefully onto the low harbor wall where the stones still held the day's leftover warmth. Pools of water shone like quiet mirrors between the rocks, and a wooden bucket sat forgotten near a coil of rope. Inside the bucket the water lay so still and round that the whole white moon seemed to rest there, perfect and bright, as if it had slipped down for a rest. Idris crouched close and felt the rough grain of the wooden rim under their fingertips. The light from the sky touched the water and made the moon look solid enough to hold, and for a moment Idris wanted to slip it into a pocket or give it one careful poke just to see if it would ripple.

Felix leaned in beside Idris and the two of them watched the glowing circle without speaking at first. The wish to touch stayed real inside Idris, yet they remembered how still the water needed to stay. Idris reached into a jacket pocket and found a smooth glass bead that had ridden along all day; the cool round texture rolled once across their palm before they tucked it away again. Then Idris spoke in a voice as soft as the evening. “Look, Felix, the moon is sitting right in the bucket,” Idris said. “We can see every part of it if we keep our hands still.”

Felix nodded and settled onto the warm stone so their shoulders almost touched. “It looks like it came down just for us,” Felix answered quietly. The water never trembled. Far along the wall a small patch of mint leaves grew between two cracks, and the fresh green smell rose when a breeze brushed past. Idris breathed it in and felt the quiet settle deeper. They pointed with one careful finger that never quite reached the bucket. “If we only look, the moon stays round and whole,” Idris told Felix. “That way everyone can see it later too.”

The gentle splash of a boat hook against wood sounded nearby, and the ferryman stepped onto the wall with slow boots. He paused when he saw the children and the glowing bucket. His coat smelled faintly of river water and rope, and the lantern light from his boat threw soft yellow shapes across the stones. “What have you found there?” the ferryman asked in a low, kind voice. Idris looked up and smiled. “The moon is resting in the water,” they said. “We are looking without touching so it stays bright.”

The ferryman knelt on one knee and peered into the bucket the same way Idris and Felix had done. The reflected moon shone back at all three of them, steady and calm. He rested his hands on his thighs and let the quiet stretch. “I have rowed under that moon many nights,” the ferryman said, “and I never thought to see it cupped so neatly. You chose well to leave it be.” Idris felt the last of the wanting fade into simple gladness. The stones under their knees held a gentle chill now that the sun had gone, and the air cooled against their cheeks.

Together they stayed a little longer, watching the light shift across the surface while the tide whispered far below. No one reached for the bucket. Idris named the smells that drifted past without hurry—the mint leaves still sharp and green, the clean salt from the open water, and the warm wood of the ferryman’s boat—and the naming made everything feel settled. Felix rested one hand on the stone and Idris did the same. The moon inside the water kept its perfect shape, and the real moon above them watched without hurry.

When the sky deepened they stood up slowly. The ferryman rose too and gave a small nod toward the bucket. Idris and Felix walked back along the harbor wall side by side, shoes quiet on the damp path. Behind them the moon stayed exactly where it belonged, shining in its circle of water. The walk home felt short and peaceful, and by the time they reached the familiar door the first stars had begun to prick the dark.

Later Idris climbed into bed again and pulled the covers high. The room held a mild warmth and the pillows felt soft under their cheek. Outside the window the real moon sailed high and clear, and the day at the harbor wall rested safely inside memory. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Idris closed their eyes and let the quiet carry them toward sleep while the house settled around them like a kind hand.
