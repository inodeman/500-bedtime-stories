The clean smell of fresh paper rose from the heap as Pilar’s hands already moved through the name tags on the wide harbor step. Warm late-day light lay across the stone and touched Pilar’s wrists while they gathered the pale rectangles that had blown into a soft pile beside the waiting parcels. The tags belonged to the choir chairs that would ride out on the evening ferry, and a playful breeze had scattered them just as the packages arrived to change hands. Pilar felt the smooth cut edges under their fingertips and stacked them by size without hurry. Beside them the water made gentle tapping sounds against the pilings, and the air stayed mild and kind. Papa stood a short distance away with his arms wrapped around three brown parcels that kept sliding, and Pilar saw the awkward tilt of his shoulders.

Papa shifted his weight and tried to bend for a tag without dropping anything. “These keep wanting to dance away,” Papa said, half laughing, half careful. Pilar looked up at the calm water and then back at the heap. The stone under Pilar’s shoes held a cool memory of the tide, and the light on the parcels turned their paper wrapping a soft gold. Pilar decided then and there to take half the work. They did not wait for a request. They simply slid closer on their knees and began to sort the tags into neat rows so Papa could keep hold of the parcels. A quiet gull floated past on the breeze, and the harbor smelled faintly of clean rope and salt.

“I can match them while you hold the packages,” Pilar said, their voice steady and low. Papa’s eyes crinkled with relief. “That would make everything smoother,” Papa answered, and he settled the parcels more firmly against his chest. Pilar’s fingers moved from tag to tag, reading the carefully printed names and placing each one face up. The light shifted a little as a cloud drifted, and a pleasant coolness brushed Pilar’s neck. They worked side by side without fuss. Whenever a tag threatened to lift again, Pilar pressed it gently flat with one palm. The parcels smelled of cardboard and dry tea leaves from inside, and Pilar liked the solid way Papa stood once the tags were no longer his worry.

Tala appeared at the top of the steps with a small basket of spare string and paused to watch. “Do you need an extra pair of hands?” Tala asked. Pilar shook their head and smiled. “We have a good plan already,” Pilar said. Tala nodded, left the string nearby in case it was wanted later, and wandered off to look at the boats. The harbor step felt broad and safe under Pilar’s knees. Papa kept the parcels steady while Pilar finished the rows. Every so often the water slapped softly against wood, and the sound mixed with the distant call of a ferry horn. Pilar’s sleeves brushed the cool stone as they reached for the last few tags at the edge of the heap.

Together they began fastening the sorted tags to the parcels that would travel with the choir chairs. Pilar held each tag while Papa looped a short length of the spare string and tied a simple knot. The light turned more honey-colored as the sun lowered, and it painted long gentle stripes across the step. Pilar felt the slight roughness of the string and the clean paper once more as they smoothed a corner. No one hurried. A pair of ducks paddled past the pilings in slow circles, and their soft quacks drifted up. Papa hummed a short choir tune under his breath, and Pilar recognized the melody and hummed along for a few notes. The work felt even and shared.

When the last parcel wore its proper tag, Pilar stood and brushed the stone dust from their trousers. Papa set the marked packages in a tidy line ready for the evening exchange. The air had cooled another degree, and Pilar’s bare arms prickled pleasantly with the change. They walked a few steps along the harbor wall to a place where the old surface still held the day’s dampness. Pilar rested one hand against the damp clay wall and felt its cool, smooth grit under their palm, steadying themselves while they looked out at the quiet boats. Papa joined them and rested a hand on Pilar’s shoulder. Neither of them spoke for a long moment. The water glowed with the last of the light, and the whole scene felt settled and complete.

A small gate farther along the walk gave a clear metallic click as someone closed it for the evening, the sound of a gate latch carrying cleanly across the still air. Pilar turned toward the sound and then back to Papa. “We finished it all,” Pilar said. Papa squeezed their shoulder once. “We did, and it went so well because you saw what was needed,” he replied. They gathered the leftover string and the empty basket Tala had left, then started the short walk home along the quiet path that followed the water. The temperature dropped gently, and Pilar felt the cooler air on their cheeks like a soft cloth. Street lamps began to bloom one by one with warm yellow circles, and their footsteps made soft scuffs on the packed earth.

At the door of their house Pilar waited while Papa turned the key. Inside, the rooms held the quiet warmth of evening. Pilar slipped off their shoes and felt the smooth wooden floor under their socks. Papa set the spare string on the table, and Pilar carried a cup of water to the low chair by the window. They both sat for a little while without turning on the brighter lamps. Pilar rested both palms on their knees and let the stillness settle into their shoulders. Outside, the harbor sounds had grown distant and kind. Papa smiled across the small space, and Pilar smiled back, content that the tags and parcels had found their proper places without any fuss or hurry.

Later Pilar climbed into bed while Papa drew the curtain halfway. The lantern by the window glowed softly. A faint ribbon of cooler night air slipped past the glass and touched Pilar’s forehead, and the room smelled faintly of clean cotton sheets. Papa sat on the edge of the mattress and brushed a stray hair from Pilar’s cheek. “Sleep well,” he whispered. Pilar closed their eyes and listened to the far-off water and the slow settling of the house. Their hands remembered the paper tags and the shared work, and the memory felt as gentle as the light. In a few minutes Pilar’s breathing slowed, and the quiet of the room wrapped around them like a favorite blanket.
