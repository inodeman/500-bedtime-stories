Vera heard a tiny click from the floor near the wardrobe and turned their head on the pillow to notice it. The room held a mild evening chill that brushed Vera’s nose, and the blue night-light cast a calm circle on the ceiling. Vera pulled the blanket higher and let their mind drift back to the sunny afternoon at the meadow gate where the day’s games always began. They could almost feel the grass again and see Mr. Ade standing nearby with his quiet smile. Vera remembered how the wooden see-saw sat right beside the open gate, one end pressed firmly to the ground because the other seat waited empty. The memory felt soft and safe, like the rest of the house around them.

Outside the gate that afternoon the air had smelled faintly of distant cocoa from the thermos Mr. Ade kept for snack time. Vera stood on the warm boards of the see-saw’s low side and looked toward Arun, who hovered just beyond the fun with hands tucked in pockets. The boards felt smooth under Vera’s sneakers. Mr. Ade watched without speaking, letting the children figure things out themselves. Vera waved and called, “Arun, the see-saw is stuck down on this side because nobody is sitting over there.” Arun took one step closer and answered, “I can see it, Vera. It looks lonely like that.” Vera nodded and felt the gentle heat of the sun on their arms while they thought.

Vera climbed down and walked the short path to Arun, the dry meadow grass whispering against their ankles. They wanted Arun to have a real place in the game, not something handed out like a prize. Vera said, “If we both sit, it will balance, and we can go as slow as we like.” Arun’s face brightened in the clear light. Together they returned to the see-saw. Vera settled on the raised end while Arun carefully sat on the lowered one. The board lifted with a slow wooden sigh until both children hung even in the middle air. Mr. Ade’s wool sleeve brushed the gate post as he leaned to watch, the soft knit catching for a second on the rough wood.

The balanced see-saw felt steady under Vera’s legs. They pushed off the ground with one foot so the ride began at a gentle pace. Up went Arun, down came Vera, then the other way around, never fast. The leaves at the edge of the meadow made a faint rustle in the light breeze. Arun laughed once and said, “This is better than standing outside, Vera.” Vera answered, “We can keep this slow rhythm as long as we want.” Mr. Ade stayed silent but his eyes shone with quiet pride that the children had solved the empty seat themselves. Vera felt the cool metal handle under their palms and smiled across at their friend.

After several calm rides Vera suggested they change the seating so each could try the other side. They both stepped off, the see-saw settling flat for a moment in the soft afternoon glow. Arun climbed onto the end that had been Vera’s, and Vera took the opposite seat. The board tilted smoothly again. Vera said, “Now we match each other perfectly.” The sweet leftover scent of the earlier cocoa still lingered near Mr. Ade’s bag, mixing with the fresh meadow air. Vera pushed gently once more, and the see-saw rose and fell like a quiet boat on still water. Arun’s shoes scuffed the dirt lightly each time they neared the ground.

They kept the pace easy so no one felt rushed. Vera watched the sky turn a warmer gold and listened to the distant birds. Mr. Ade finally spoke only to offer water from a bottle that felt cool against Vera’s fingers. “You two found a fine way,” he said simply, then stepped back again. Vera and Arun continued, sometimes pausing at the level middle just to talk. The wooden plank stayed warm from the sun, and Vera’s hands rested easily on its edges. They decided the new rule would be that anyone near the gate could join by simply sitting down, no announcements needed. Arun grinned and nodded.

When the shadows grew longer Vera and Arun let the see-saw rest with both feet on the earth. They climbed off and stood side by side near the meadow gate. The air had cooled a little, and Vera rubbed their arms while looking at the empty seats that no longer seemed stuck. Mr. Ade gathered the thermos and the small bag. Vera walked with Arun toward the path home, the grass now cooler underfoot. They spoke softly about coming back tomorrow to try the same balanced rides. The day felt complete and friendly without anyone saying so out loud.

Later that evening Vera sat in the low chair by their bed and rocked once, then let the chair stop by itself. The house was still except for the far-off settling of walls. Vera’s pajamas felt cozy against their skin as they thought again of the see-saw that had waited for two. They remembered Arun’s happy face and Mr. Ade’s patient waiting. The memory brought a peaceful heaviness to Vera’s eyelids. They stretched once and slid under the covers, the pillow cool at first then warming quickly.

Vera curled on their side and listened to the quiet house settle around them. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside a soft wind moved the curtains just enough to make a hush of fabric, and Vera closed their eyes, breathing the calm night air that carried no hurry at all.
