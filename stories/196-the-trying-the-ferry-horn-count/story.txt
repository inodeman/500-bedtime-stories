The soft evening breeze brushed the windows of the small school room with a low stage edge, letting a gentle coolness drift across the floorboards and settle on bare arms. Niko stepped inside and felt that mild chill touch their wrists while pale gold light from the low sun made quiet patterns near the short wooden platform. They looked at the bright ferry horn resting on a stool by the stage edge and pressed their lips together, feeling shy about counting with the horn even if they might lose the number. Mama waited a few steps away in her familiar coat, and the room held a calm hush broken only by distant leaves. Niko’s shoes made soft taps on the boards as they moved closer, the cool air still resting lightly against their face.

Mama crouched so her eyes met Niko’s and spoke in a low, steady voice. “You can try the smallest bit if you like, Niko. No rush and no big show.” Niko nodded once and smelled the faint wet wool of Mama’s coat drying from the walk over, a clean damp scent that felt ordinary and safe. They reached toward the ferry horn and ran one finger along its smooth curve, noticing how the late light warmed the metal just enough to feel pleasant. Walid sat cross-legged near the edge of the low stage and offered a quiet smile without moving closer. The air stayed cool around Niko’s ankles while they breathed in and out once, holding the horn loosely.

Niko lifted the ferry horn a little and whispered, “I might lose the number, but I can start with one.” Mama answered softly, “One is brave enough, Niko. I am right here.” The tin clock on the teacher’s shelf gave a single clear tick that marked the quiet second, and Niko heard it like a friendly reminder that time moved slowly here. They pressed the horn once and made a gentle low toot that floated only as far as the first row of chairs. Walid clapped two fingers together so the sound stayed tiny. Warm light from the window rested on Niko’s hair while they stood on the low stage edge and felt the solid wood under their feet.

Niko tried again and said aloud, “Two,” before giving the horn another soft toot that stayed easy to hear yet never loud. Mama kept her hands folded and did not ask for more, her kind face steady in the gentle light that filled the small school room. Niko felt the paper edge of a counting sheet someone had left on the stool, smooth and thin under their free hand, and used it only to keep place without worry. They counted “three” in a voice that reached Mama and Walid clearly, then tooted once more. The cool evening air still moved a little near the open window, brushing Niko’s cheek like a calm hand while they stayed on the low stage edge.

Walid spoke gently from his seat. “I like how steady that sounds, Niko.” Niko answered, “Thank you. I am still counting even if the number might slip.” Mama smiled and said nothing else, simply staying close enough that Niko could see her without turning far. They pressed the horn for four and five in the same small brave way, the toots clear in the quiet room yet never pushed into a performance. Soft shadows stretched longer across the floor as the light outside grew softer and warmer against the glass. Niko’s shoulders stayed loose while they kept their feet planted on the low stage edge and felt the wood’s mild grain through their socks.

Niko paused, breathed once more, and tried six and seven with the ferry horn, each number spoken so Mama and Walid could hear it without strain. The smell of wet wool had already faded into the background, and now only the ordinary scent of polished wood remained. They felt shy again for a short moment when the count wobbled, yet they simply began again at the last clear number and kept going. Mama’s presence stayed kind and still, never urging a longer turn or a louder sound. Cool air continued to drift near Niko’s knees while the late light turned the edges of the chairs a quiet amber.

Walid held up eight fingers slowly so Niko could see them without pressure. Niko smiled a little, said “eight,” and gave the horn one more gentle toot that filled only the space between them. They finished with nine and ten in the same careful voice, letting the numbers land softly even when one almost slipped away. Mama nodded once and kept her calm seat on the nearby chair, her kindness like a steady lantern of its own. The temperature in the room felt pleasantly mild now, neither cold nor warm, wrapping Niko like a light blanket while they stood on the low stage edge.

Niko set the ferry horn back on the stool and stepped down, the boards cool under their feet again. “I did the smallest brave version,” they told Mama. Mama replied, “Yes, Niko, and I heard every number you chose to share.” Walid stood and walked beside them toward the door without hurry. The three of them paused near the window where the last of the outdoor light mixed with the indoor glow, and Niko felt the quiet settle around their shoulders like soft cloth. No one asked for another round; the counting with the horn had been enough just as it was.

Niko leaned against Mama for a moment and watched the room grow stiller while the mild air rested on their arms. They thought of the clear toots and the numbers spoken out loud, and the shyness felt smaller than before. Mama’s hand rested lightly on Niko’s shoulder, warm and steady. Walid waved a quiet goodbye and slipped out first. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Niko took Mama’s other hand and walked with her into the gentle evening, the cool boards giving way to the softer path outside while the small school room kept its calm light behind them.
