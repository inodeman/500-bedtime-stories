A soft tick came from somewhere near the shelves, steady and gentle like a fingertip tapping glass. Tamsin heard it first and turned their head on the cushioned library window seat, where afternoon light lay warm across their knees. Outside the tall glass the garden path shone pale gold, and inside the air felt cool against Tamsin’s cheeks. They wriggled a little deeper into the seat and noticed the tin clock on the low table, its face bright and its tiny sound keeping company with the hush of turning pages farther away. Tamsin smiled at the quiet rhythm and waited, curious, as soft footsteps approached along the carpet.

The ferryman arrived with a folded paper and a kind, weathered face, and he settled carefully on the edge of the window seat so Tamsin would not feel crowded. He smelled faintly of river water and of something green from the kitchen garden, and when he set a small cloth bundle down, a mild scent of basil rose and drifted past Tamsin’s nose. Tamsin looked from the paper to the ferryman’s calm eyes and felt the smooth wood of the seat under their palms. “May I sit with you a while, Tamsin?” the ferryman asked in a low voice. Tamsin nodded, glad of the company, and the sunlight warmed the side of their neck while the library stayed peaceful around them both.

The ferryman unfolded the paper slowly and began to tell Tamsin about directions to a step that was easy to miss. He spoke of a landing near the old footbridge where the boards met stone, and how a single shallow rise hid in the shadow of a potted fern. Tamsin felt a bubble of bigger news rise in their chest and nearly spoke, yet the ferryman’s steady tone held them still. The window glass was cool when Tamsin rested their forehead near it for a moment, listening. Outside a sparrow chirped once and then fell quiet, and inside the ferryman’s words moved gently, describing how the missed step waited for careful feet and patient eyes.

Tamsin wanted very much to interrupt with the larger thing they had carried all day, but they kept their lips closed and watched the ferryman’s hands smooth the paper flat. The directions named three turns after the reading chairs and one soft count of four before the fern’s shadow. Tamsin pressed a polished pebble the ferryman had brought as a quiet weight for the corner of the page; the stone felt cool and glassy under Tamsin’s thumb, and turning it helped them stay quiet. Warm light striped the cushion, and Tamsin’s breathing slowed to match the unhurried story. They decided, without saying so, to hear every word before offering any of their own.

The ferryman explained that the easy-to-miss step was not tall or tricky, only shy, tucked where people looked ahead instead of down. He told Tamsin how boat passengers sometimes hurried and stepped over it without knowing, yet those who paused found it kind and steady underfoot. Tamsin smelled the faint basil again when the ferryman shifted the cloth, and the library’s quiet seemed deeper. Tamsin’s shoulders relaxed against the pillow. They still held their bigger news, bright and waiting, but they let the directions finish unfolding like a path drawn in soft pencil.

He described the last part carefully, saying the step sat just before the little balcony that overlooked the water garden, and that noticing it made the whole walk feel friendlier. Tamsin’s fingers traced the seam of the cushion, feeling its nubby weave, while they listened all the way. A cart rolled somewhere in another aisle with a muted squeak, then silence returned. Tamsin almost spoke twice, yet each time they swallowed the words and stayed with the story. The ferryman’s voice remained even, and Tamsin felt proud of their own stillness, though no one named it.

“And that is the whole of it, Tamsin,” the ferryman said at last. “If you count four after the fern, the shy step will meet your foot like a friend.” Tamsin waited until the final sentence truly ended, then asked one real question. “Does the step feel the same in the evening light as it does in the morning?” Tamsin said, their voice soft. The ferryman considered, and the window seat held them both in a pool of gentle brightness. “It feels a little cooler after sunset,” he answered, “but just as sure.” Tamsin nodded, satisfied, and the bigger news settled into something smaller and easier to share.

Only then did Tamsin offer a small piece of their own day. “I found a bookmark shaped like a leaf under the atlas,” Tamsin said, “and I left it for the next reader.” The ferryman smiled and the air near the window stayed pleasantly mild on Tamsin’s wrists. Together they smoothed the directions once more and set the polished paper where it would not curl. Tamsin leaned against the cushion and listened to the distant hush of the library, content that their news had waited its turn. The ferryman folded his cloth with care, and Tamsin watched the simple motion, feeling peaceful and heard.

Later the room dimmed to a honey color as clouds drifted past the sun, and Tamsin stayed a little longer on the window seat with the directions resting nearby. The ferryman rose without hurry and thanked Tamsin for listening so completely. Tamsin thanked him back for the careful map to the shy step, and their shared quiet felt like a blanket. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Tamsin curled their bare toes against the warm fabric of the cushion, breathed the last faint garden sweetness in the air, and let their eyes grow heavy while the tin clock kept its gentle company and the library held them safe until it was time to go home.
