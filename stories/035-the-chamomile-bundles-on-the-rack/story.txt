Auntie Luz sat already at the quiet craft corner, her sleeves rolled, pressing a clean sheet of paper flat against the wooden table while the lamp threw a honey-colored pool of light across the scissors and the little pot of paste. The air in the room felt pleasantly cool on Una’s cheeks as they stepped closer, and Una watched the way Auntie Luz’s fingers moved without hurry. Outside the open window the evening had settled soft and blue, and Una could hear the distant hush of the street mixed with something greener. Auntie Luz hummed a tiny tune that sounded like water dripping into a bowl. Una stood very near, smelling the clean paper and the faint sweetness that always lived in this corner, and they felt the smooth edge of the table under their palm.

Una’s eyes went straight to the wooden rack that stood beside the paste jars. On its thin bars rested several chamomile bundles, still wrapped in soft cloth so the flowers could finish drying in peace. Una wanted very much to lift one corner of the cloth and peek, because the pale blossoms looked almost ready and Una loved their tiny round centers. “May I look just once, Auntie Luz?” Una asked, their voice quiet as a moth. Auntie Luz kept smoothing the paper and answered without turning, “Not yet, Una. The bundles need their own time under the cloth.” The lamp light warmed the side of Una’s face while they considered the covered rack, and the paste pot gave off a mild clean scent that mixed with the cooler air drifting in.

Una reached one careful finger toward the nearest bundle, then stopped. Auntie Luz set down her paste brush and looked at Una with kind eyes. “The flowers are still giving their last bit of green water to the air,” Auntie Luz said. “If we uncover them too soon the petals stay soft instead of crisp.” Una nodded slowly. The wooden rack felt sturdy when Una rested a hand on its side, and the grain of the wood was slightly rough under their skin. From the open window came the sound of wind in the fig leaves, a gentle shushing that rose and fell like slow breathing. Una listened and let their shoulders drop.

“What if we make little paper pockets while we wait?” Auntie Luz offered. Una thought about it, then smiled. “I can cut the squares,” Una said. They pulled a chair close so their knees fit under the table edge. The paper felt dry and whispery between Una’s fingers as they snipped along the pencil lines Auntie Luz had drawn. Each snip made a tiny crisp sound. Auntie Luz dabbed paste onto the edges, and together they folded the pockets that would later hold dried petals. The lamp cast a steady glow that made the paste shine like wet moonlight for a second before it turned clear.

Una still glanced at the covered chamomile bundles every few moments. The cloth looked smooth and pale. Auntie Luz noticed and spoke softly. “They will tell us when they are ready by the way they smell,” she said. “Until then we can keep our hands busy with quiet things.” Una decided to leave the cloth exactly where it was. They set down the scissors and wandered the two steps to the low brick ledge beneath the window, where a small brown snail had begun a patient journey across one whole brick. Una crouched so their nose nearly touched the cool brick surface and watched the snail’s silvery trail catch the last light. The brick felt solid and slightly dusty under Una’s fingertips.

While Una watched, they noticed a fallen fig leaf resting near the snail’s path. Una lifted it gently and turned it over. The leaf’s soft underside brushed their thumb like the finest velvet, cool and almost fuzzy. Una held the leaf for a long breath, then set it back so the snail would not be bothered. Behind them Auntie Luz kept folding paper pockets, the paste brush making soft wet clicks against the jar rim. Una stayed crouched, feeling the evening air move across their bare arms, cooler now, and counting how many tiny waves the snail’s body made as it crossed the red brick from one end to the other without stopping once.

When the snail reached the far edge of the brick, Una stood and returned to the table. Auntie Luz had finished three neat pockets and was lining them up like little boats. “The bundles still need a while,” Auntie Luz said, “but you waited so patiently, Una.” Una felt proud in a quiet way and picked up a fourth square of paper. They folded it themselves this time, pressing the crease with the side of their thumb until it lay flat. The paper made a tiny sighing sound. A faint smell of beeswax drifted from the drawer where Auntie Luz kept the sealing sticks for the finished pockets, warm and golden and calm. Una breathed it in and smiled.

Time passed the way honey drips—slow and sweet. Una and Auntie Luz talked about nothing important: the way the paste turned clear, the number of pockets they would need, the color of the fig leaves outside. Una’s hands stayed busy with folding and pressing. Every so often they looked at the wooden rack but did not touch the cloth. The lamp light grew a little warmer as the sky outside deepened. Una’s eyelids felt pleasantly heavy, yet they kept working until Auntie Luz finally rose and walked to the rack. She lifted one corner of the cloth and sniffed. “Now they are ready, Una,” she said. “Come feel how light they have become.”

Una stepped close and touched a dry bundle with two careful fingers. The flowers felt crisp and feather-light, exactly right. Together they unwrapped the cloth and laid the chamomile onto clean paper so the last bits of fragrance could settle into the new pockets. Una helped tuck a few blossoms into each pocket, then pressed the edges closed. The craft corner smelled gently of dried summer. Auntie Luz brushed a stray petal from Una’s sleeve. Una leaned against Auntie Luz’s side and watched the finished pockets rest in a neat row. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the fig leaves had gone still, and the room felt perfectly quiet and safe for sleep.
