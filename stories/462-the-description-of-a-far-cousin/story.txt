The folded paper rested open on the library window seat, its cream surface catching a thin stripe of late sun so that every careful loop of ink shone like a tiny river. Nim bent close enough to see the small sketches along the margin, a cousin drawn with freckles and a wide easy smile, and the description of a cousin they had not met seemed almost to breathe under Nim’s fingertips. The cushion beneath the page felt plump and cool against Nim’s knees, and the quiet of the tall room wrapped around them like a soft blanket. Dust motes drifted in the light, and Nim stayed still, reading the first lines again without rushing.

The ferryman sat opposite Nim on the same wide seat, his coat smelling faintly of river water and his hat resting on the floorboards. He had carried the paper all the way from the landing, he said, because the library window seat was the best place for slow stories. Warm air rose from the heating vent near their shoes, and the ferryman cleared his throat with a gentle hum. Brigid, who had come only to keep them company, curled nearby with a book of her own, her presence calm as a still pond. Nim glanced at the ferryman’s kind face and waited.

“This cousin of yours lives where the willows lean over the ferry path,” the ferryman began, his voice low enough not to disturb the shelves. “They keep a pocket full of smooth stones and laugh when the ducks steal bread.” Nim felt a quick flutter of bigger news inside their chest, something they had wanted to shout the moment they sat down, yet the soft light on the paper held them quiet. The description of a cousin they had not met continued in the ferryman’s careful words, and Nim pressed their palms flat on the cushion to stay steady. Outside, a single leaf tapped the glass like a polite knuckle.

Nim’s shoulders tensed once, wanting to leap in with their own news, but they chose instead to listen all the way through. The ferryman spoke of how the cousin liked to name the clouds after animals and once spent a whole morning teaching a frog to sit still. A cool draft slipped under the window sash and brushed Nim’s ankles, reminding them that the afternoon was easing toward evening. Brigid turned a page with a soft rustle, and Nim kept their eyes on the ink. The urge to interrupt rose and then settled again, like a bird deciding to stay on its branch.

“They write letters on the backs of ferry tickets,” the ferryman went on, smiling. “And they say the river smells different every season.” Nim breathed in and noticed, quite suddenly, the clean bright scent of mint leaves drifting from a small pot on the nearby reading table, a scent that made the whole corner feel fresher. The description of a cousin they had not met grew richer with each sentence, and Nim tucked their bigger news carefully aside for later. The window seat fabric was worn smooth under their hands, and the ferryman’s voice never hurried.

Somewhere down the aisle a cat’s collar bell gave one tiny silver note as the library cat wandered past the history shelves. Nim listened harder, following every word about the cousin’s favorite blue scarf and the way they hummed while polishing the ferry rail. The light through the glass turned a deeper gold, and the temperature near the window cooled just enough that Nim pulled their sweater sleeves over their wrists. Brigid glanced up once and offered a quiet nod, as if she too understood the value of waiting. Nim stayed with the story until the very last phrase settled into the air.

“And that,” the ferryman finished softly, “is how the cousin always ends their letters, with a drawing of the moon over the water.” Nim waited a full breath after the final sentence, then asked one real question. “Do they ever visit the library window seat the way we are now?” The ferryman nodded, pleased. “They hope to, one day soon, and they would like to meet you properly.” The cushion dipped gently as Nim shifted, and the paper crinkled with a friendly sound under the changing light.

Only then did Nim share something small of their own. “I found a glass bead this morning by the garden path,” they said, holding out their palm so the ferryman and Brigid could see the cool round texture resting there. “It looks like a drop of river caught and frozen.” The ferryman touched the bead with one careful finger and smiled wider. Nim felt the quiet pride of having listened first, and the bigger news they had carried now seemed content to wait for another afternoon. Warmth from the low sun still lingered on the glass, and the three of them sat without hurry.

The library grew gentler as the day thinned, and Nim folded the description of a cousin they had not met with slow care before slipping it into the ferryman’s waiting hand. Brigid closed her book and stretched, the air around them smelling faintly of old paper and the earlier mint. Nim leaned back against the window frame, shoulders loose, and watched the garden shadows lengthen across the lawn. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
