On the damp brick the snail's silver map lay open like a shining ribbon of wet silk, every thin curve catching the pale light that filtered through the clouds after the gentle rain. The trail twisted in slow loops and sudden turns, as if the snail had drawn a whole countryside no wider than a hand. Cool moisture beaded along the brick's rough edge, and each droplet held a soft gleam. Chioma leaned so near that the silver lines filled their whole sight, and the air rising from the wet stone felt chilly against their cheeks. They stayed very still, watching the map glitter without a single sound except the quiet drip of leftover rain from a nearby leaf.

Chioma rocked back on their heels and looked along the garden path where the bricks still shone dark and slick. The wish to pocket the silver map or poke its delicate lines rose for a moment, bright and quick, yet Chioma only breathed in the fresh damp scent of the path and kept both hands safely on their own knees. A leather lace hung from the edge of Chioma's jacket pocket, smooth and slightly stiff under their thumb as they touched it without thinking. They whispered to the empty air, “Hello, little map,” and the words floated soft as mist. Farther down the path a patch of moss glowed greener than before, and the whole garden seemed hushed and listening.

Joss came walking along the path with careful steps so the wet bricks would not splash. Chioma waved them closer and pointed. “Come see, but only with your eyes,” Chioma said. Joss knelt beside them, and together they peered at the silver curves that wound across the brick like a treasure chart for someone very small. The light slanted lower now, turning the trail even brighter against the dark clay. Chioma showed Joss how to lean in slowly, how to tilt their head so the map caught every bit of glow, and how to keep elbows lifted high above the brick. Joss smiled and breathed out a quiet “Oh.”

They stayed that way for a long gentle stretch of time, shoulders almost touching. Chioma explained in a low voice, “If we leave it alone the snail can find its way again when it wants.” Joss nodded and answered, “It looks like roads and rivers all mixed up.” The cool air brushed their necks while a single bird called once from the fence and then fell silent. Chioma felt proud of the careful looking, and the silver map seemed to thank them by shining a little more. Tiny grains of sand stuck to the brick edges looked like mountains beside the trail.

Mama’s footsteps sounded soft on the wet path as she came to join them. She carried a small basket that smelled faintly of soapy wool from the scarf tucked inside. “What have you found, Chioma?” Mama asked, kneeling so her knees touched the damp ground. Chioma pointed once more and said, “A snail made this silver map, and we are looking without touching.” Mama’s eyes widened with the same quiet wonder, and she rested a hand lightly on Chioma’s shoulder. The three of them formed a small circle of stillness around the brick while the last raindrops slid from the rose leaves overhead.

Mama spoke gently, “It belongs right here on its brick.” Chioma agreed at once, and Joss added, “We can remember every turn.” They all studied the looping silver lines one last time, noticing how one branch of the trail curled toward a crack where a bit of green waited. The temperature of the air had grown milder, almost warm against their faces now that the clouds had thinned. Chioma reached out only far enough to feel the smooth wet surface of a neighboring brick, never the map itself, and then drew the hand back. A cat’s collar bell tinkled once from the porch steps as the family cat wandered near and then settled to watch.

Chioma stood up slowly so nothing would shake. They took Joss by the hand and Mama by the other, and the three of them stepped backward along the path, still gazing at the silver map until distance made it look like a single bright stroke of paint. The garden smelled clean and new, and the light had turned the color of fresh butter. Chioma felt the leather lace again for a second and then let it fall free. No one hurried. They simply walked the short way toward the house door, leaving the brick and its shining trail exactly as they had found them under the open sky.

Inside the kitchen the floorboards felt warm under Chioma’s socks after the cool path. Mama set the basket down while Chioma and Joss stood by the window that looked out over the garden. They could still see the spot where the brick waited, though the silver map itself had become a soft sparkle too far to read. Chioma said, “Tomorrow it might be longer or shorter.” Joss answered, “Or the snail might add a new road.” The room held a peaceful hush broken only by the quiet clink of a cup Mama set on the counter. Chioma pressed their palms to the cool glass for a moment and then turned away with a small satisfied sigh.

Later Chioma sat on the low bench near the window while Mama brushed their hair with long slow strokes. Joss had gone home with a wave, and the house settled into its evening quiet. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Chioma thought once more of the silver map resting safe on its brick, of the careful looking they had shared, and of the gentle rain that had made everything shine. Their eyelids grew heavy in the calm golden light, and the last thing they noticed was the faint warmth rising from the floorboards beneath their feet.
