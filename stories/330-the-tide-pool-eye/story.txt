The shrimp drifted sideways through a circle of seawater no bigger than a plate, its pale body catching the late afternoon light that slipped between the meadow fence boards. Tiny legs flicked once and sent a slow ripple across the still surface. Ximena knelt close enough to see the faint sand grains at the bottom and the single clear whisker that trailed behind the shrimp. Beside the fence stood the wooden bucket Auntie Luz had carried from the shore path that morning, its water cool against the warm air, and the plate-sized pool rested carefully in a shallow dish braced against the bucket so the shrimp could rest in shade. Ximena’s breath made a soft cloud on the water’s edge for a second. They stayed very still, elbows on the grass, watching the light dance in thin silver lines.

Ximena felt the wish rise sharp and real, the wish to cup the whole little pool in both hands and pocket it or to reach in with one careful finger and poke the shrimp just to see it dart. The green smell of tomato vines drifted over the fence from the garden rows beyond, rich and sun-warmed. Ximena’s fingers curled into the grass instead, feeling the cool damp earth. They let the wish sit and then settle, because the shrimp looked peaceful in its small world. A soft scuff of sneakers on the path told Ximena that Orla was coming.

Orla dropped to the grass beside Ximena and peered at the plate of water. “Is that a real shrimp?” Orla asked in a hushed voice. Ximena nodded and kept their own voice quiet. “Yes. We look with our eyes only. No touching the water or the shrimp.” The late light slanted lower and painted the fence posts a gentle orange while a warm breeze brushed Ximena’s cheek. Orla leaned in slowly, hands flat on their own knees, and the two of them watched the shrimp glide in one unhurried circle. Ximena pointed with a finger held far above the rim so nothing disturbed the surface.

“See how its legs move like little feathers?” Ximena said. Orla smiled and whispered back, “I see the tiny shadow it makes on the sand.” The water stayed glass-smooth under the soft glow. Ximena felt proud of the careful way they both held still. Auntie Luz’s footsteps approached along the meadow edge, steady and calm, and the air around them stayed mild and kind. Ximena kept their gaze on the shrimp so Orla would too.

Auntie Luz settled on the low wooden bench that rested against the fence posts and looked down with them. “May I see as well?” Auntie Luz asked gently. Ximena answered, “Yes, Auntie Luz. We are only looking.” The three of them formed a quiet circle of attention. Ximena noticed a single grain of the bench under their own palm, smooth and worn from many summers, and rested their hand there while the shrimp paused near a pebble no bigger than a seed. Cool shade from the fence boards kept the little pool comfortable. Orla shifted closer without speaking so Auntie Luz could see better.

The shrimp flicked once more and settled again. Ximena said, “It belongs right here until Auntie Luz takes the water back tomorrow.” Auntie Luz nodded and spoke softly. “You are right, Ximena. Looking is enough.” A faint jingle sounded from the garden path as a cat’s collar bell rang once and then faded. The evening air cooled a little against Ximena’s arms. They all stayed quiet for another long moment, simply watching the pale shrimp drift in its plate-sized home beside the bucket and the fence.

Ximena breathed out slowly and felt the wish to pocket or poke melt away into the grass. Orla’s shoulder touched theirs lightly in agreement. The light had turned the color of warm honey across the meadow. Auntie Luz stood first and offered a hand. Ximena and Orla rose together, still glancing once more at the still water. They left the tide pool exactly where it rested against the bucket, safe and undisturbed under the open sky. The wooden fence held its quiet posts while a soft cricket began somewhere far off in the grass.

They walked the short path toward the house as the sky deepened. Ximena listened hard for the far clock in the village tower instead of the nearer kitchen timer, catching only the distant soft chime that floated over the fields. The air felt cooler now against their skin and smelled clean after the day. Orla skipped once then matched Ximena’s slower steps. Auntie Luz walked beside them, humming a low tune that matched their pace. Inside the open doorway the rooms waited, calm and ready for night.

Ximena washed their hands in lukewarm water that smelled faintly of soap and dried them on the soft towel. They climbed into bed while Orla waved good night from the hall and Auntie Luz smoothed the blanket. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Ximena closed their eyes and pictured the tiny shrimp still gliding in its quiet circle of water beside the meadow fence, safe under the darkening sky, and the cool sheets settled around them like a gentle tide of their own.
