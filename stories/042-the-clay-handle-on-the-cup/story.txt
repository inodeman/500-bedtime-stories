The cup handle that had just been joined rested on its wooden tray in the center of the cottage windowsill, the clay still cool and slightly damp along the fresh seam where the curve met the cup’s side. Soft late light spilled across the join and made the wet edge gleam like a narrow ribbon of silver. Golda leaned so near that they could see every tiny fingerprint pressed into the soft clay and almost taste the clean earthy smell rising from it. Around the tray stood jars of pale glaze, shallow trays holding other small clay pieces, and loops of drying wool that hung in gentle arcs and brushed one another with a dry whisper whenever the air moved. Golda’s breath fogged the cool surface for a moment, and they longed to tap the handle just once to feel whether the join had already set.

Golda wanted very much to lift the soft cloth and check the cup handle before it was ready, yet the ferryman sat only a few steps away on a low stool, his river-worn boots resting on the warm floorboards. Steam curled from the mug he held, carrying a quiet heat into the room. “The clay likes its own slow time,” the ferryman said in his deep, even voice, the words soft as water against a wooden hull. Golda’s fingers hovered above the tray while cooler air from the open pane brushed their wrists. Jars winked in the light, trays held steady, and the drying wool gave off a faint lanolin scent that mixed with the clay. Golda drew their hands back and nodded, though the wish to peek still tickled inside them.

Eshe sat cross-legged on a cushion near the sill, sorting buttons into a shallow dish, and the soft clink of one glass bead rolling against another reached Golda’s ears like a tiny bell of its own. Golda settled beside Eshe and felt the smooth, cool roundness of that single glass bead under their thumb when they helped. “We can leave the cloth over the handle,” Golda said quietly. “It will finish when it finishes.” The ferryman smiled and set his mug down with a gentle wooden tap. Warm afternoon light shifted across the drying wool and turned its fibers golden at the tips. Golda rubbed the glass bead once more, noticing how perfectly round and slick it felt against their skin, then placed it carefully back with the others.

A small gray cat padded across the floorboards, and the bright sound of a cat’s collar bell rang once, twice, three times before the cat settled in a patch of sun. Golda listened to that clear, friendly chime and felt the last of the hurry drain from their shoulders. The ferryman reached over and rested one broad hand lightly on Golda’s back, the fabric of their shirt warm from the cottage air. “Shall we find a quiet task while the clay rests?” he asked. Golda breathed in and out and answered, “Yes. I will name three smells I notice right now.” They closed their eyes for a moment and let the room speak to them through scent alone.

First Golda named the sharp green smell of mint leaves that someone had crushed earlier and left in a little dish near the jars. Second came the clean, soapy smell of the drying wool that had been washed that morning and now hung in soft loops. Third was the mild steam from the ferryman’s tea, which smelled of honey and ordinary black leaves. Eshe smiled and whispered, “Those are good ones.” The cool sill under Golda’s elbows kept them company while the cat’s collar bell gave one more soft ring as the cat shifted. No one lifted the cloth. The cup handle stayed covered and safe, doing its quiet work in the dimness beneath the cloth.

Golda and Eshe began folding the smallest lengths of drying wool into neat coils, feeling the springy, slightly oily texture of each strand between their fingers. The light through the window had grown gentler and more golden, laying long bars of warmth across the trays and jars. “The river was very still today,” the ferryman remarked, his voice low so as not to startle the resting clay. Golda answered, “I like still days. They give everything room to finish.” They kept folding, matching Eshe’s slow rhythm, and the cottage filled with the small sounds of wool brushing wool and the distant tick of a wall clock. Cool air still drifted from the pane, balancing the warmth of the floor.

After a while Golda fetched a clean cloth and wiped the rims of the glaze jars, noticing how the glass felt smooth and almost cold under the damp rag. Eshe hummed a wordless tune that matched the slow swing of the drying wool above them. The ferryman rose and stretched, his joints making a quiet creak like an old boat, then sat again. “You are doing a fine job of waiting,” he told Golda. Golda felt a small glow of calm settle in their chest, warmer than the sun stripes on the floor. They did not peek. The covered tray simply sat among its neighbors of jars and wool, patient as a stone in a quiet stream.

When the light had softened even more, Golda finally lifted one corner of the cloth with careful fingers and found the cup handle firm and matte, the join sealed and ready for whatever came next. The clay no longer felt cool and damp; it held the mild temperature of the room itself. Eshe clapped once, softly, and the cat’s collar bell answered from under the table. “It finished,” Golda said, wonder bright in their voice. The ferryman nodded and held out his broad palm so Golda could set the finished cup carefully into it for a moment of shared looking. The drying wool stirred once more in the breeze, and the jars caught the last clear light of day.

Golda replaced the cup on its tray and leaned their elbows on the sill while the cottage grew quieter still. Eshe stacked the button dish, the ferryman finished his cooled tea, and the gray cat curled tighter in its warm spot. Outside, the river kept its steady hush, and inside every jar and tray seemed to rest. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Golda breathed the lingering green of mint leaves one last time, felt the smooth memory of the glass bead on their thumb, and listened for any final chime of the collar bell, content to let the evening arrive in its own gentle time.
