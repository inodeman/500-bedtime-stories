The smell of rain on dust rose warm and soft from the courtyard stones, and Milo’s hands were already busy sliding smooth plates along the long wooden table beneath the fig tree. They stacked the little cups in a careful row and pressed a napkin flat beside each one while the last of the daylight turned the leaves a gentle green-gold. The air still held that clean damp scent from the earlier sprinkle, and Milo breathed it in as they straightened a fork that had gone crooked. Grandpa Sol hummed somewhere near the kitchen doorway, and the table felt solid and familiar under Milo’s palms. They liked the way the wood grain curved like tiny rivers. A moth drifted past the low branches, and Milo paused only long enough to watch it before returning to the quiet work of making everything ready for supper.

Warm light spilled from the open doors and mixed with the cooler evening air that brushed Milo’s cheeks and the backs of their hands. Grandpa Sol carried out a wide platter of roasted vegetables that still held a little steam, and Milo helped settle it in the middle of the long table so everyone could reach. The fig tree above them rustled with a dry, friendly whisper whenever a small breeze moved through. Milo checked the seats again and smoothed the cloth where it had bunched near the corner. They could hear the soft clink of serving spoons and the distant call of a bird settling for the night. Everything smelled good in a plain, comforting way, and Milo felt the quiet happiness of a meal that was almost ready.

A gate latch clicked once from the far side of the courtyard wall, and Kira stepped through with a small satchel and a bright smile. Milo waved both hands and hurried around the end of the table to meet them. “You came just in time,” Milo said, and Kira answered, “I brought the picture book about the river boats.” The satchel brushed Milo’s sleeve as they walked back together, and the evening air felt cooler now that the sun had slipped lower. Grandpa Sol nodded a welcome and set out an extra plate without fuss. Milo showed Kira the seat nearest the thickest shade of the fig leaves, where the light was softest on the eyes. They poured water into three cups and listened to the gentle pour, which sounded like a tiny stream.

Near the center of the table sat a lamp with a swiveling shade that Grandpa Sol had brought out earlier, explaining it could be turned so two pages were lit at once if anyone wanted to read after the meal. Milo touched the cool metal base and watched how the glow could stretch in two directions across open books. The lamp’s light was steady and golden, never harsh, and it made the fig leaves cast gentle moving shadows on the cloth. Milo imagined the river-boat pictures shining side by side. They turned the shade a little to test it, and a soft click sounded as it settled. The idea of sharing the light felt right already, and Milo smiled at the warm circle it made on the wood.

When Grandpa Sol began to serve the first helpings, Milo noticed the largest soft roll resting alone on the bread board and felt a small tug of thought. If only one person took the first and biggest share, the lamp that could brighten two pages would not feel fair later either, because supper and story belonged together under the same tree. Milo looked at Kira’s empty plate and at their own, then at Grandpa Sol’s kind face. The vegetables smelled earthy and sweet, and the night air cooled another degree against Milo’s neck. They decided without hurry that the good things should travel evenly from the start.

Milo tore the large roll carefully into three smaller, even pieces and set one on Kira’s plate, one on Grandpa Sol’s, and one on their own. “This way we all begin the same,” Milo said quietly. Kira’s eyes brightened, and they replied, “Thank you, Milo. That feels friendly.” Grandpa Sol simply nodded once, a calm smile on his mouth, and let the choice stand without a speech of any kind. He passed the vegetables next in the same even spirit. Milo felt the soft give of the bread between their fingers and the pleasant warmth still held inside it. The fig tree above them held still for a moment, as if listening.

After the plates were filled and the first quiet bites were taken, Milo reached for the lamp again and turned its shade so the light fell equally across the open picture book Kira had brought. “We can look at the river boats together when we finish,” Milo said. Kira leaned closer and answered, “The pages will both be bright that way.” The glow rested on the paper like morning sun on water, and Milo could already see the painted boats and the blue lines of the current. Grandpa Sol buttered his small piece of roll and watched the two children with easy contentment. The courtyard held a hush broken only by the occasional soft scrape of a chair leg and the distant settling of leaves.

When the meal slowed and the cups were nearly empty, Milo noticed a satin ribbon marking Kira’s favorite page and ran one careful finger along its smooth, cool length before opening the book fully under the shared lamp light. The ribbon slipped like quiet water against their skin. Together they turned the pages while Grandpa Sol cleared the nearer dishes with slow, kind movements. The lamp kept both sides of each spread gently lit, and Milo pointed to a little painted dock where two children sat side by side. No one rushed. The air grew cooler still, and a faint mist of breath showed for a second when Milo laughed at a funny boat with too many flags. They traced a small circle of fog on the side of a water pitcher with one dry finger, then wiped it away so the glass shone clear again.

Later, when the courtyard had grown fully blue with evening and the fig leaves were only dark shapes overhead, Milo helped carry the last cups inside and paused by the low window that looked back toward the quiet table. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Milo leaned their shoulder against the wooden frame and felt the settled calm of the whole supper still resting in their chest. Outside, the long table waited under the tree for morning, and the lamp’s memory of shared pages stayed warm in Milo’s mind. Grandpa Sol rested a steady hand on Milo’s shoulder for a moment, and Kira yawned a happy yawn near the doorway. Milo breathed the cool night air one more time, already half dreaming of river boats and even pieces of bread, and the house wrapped them in its familiar gentle hush as they turned toward bed.
