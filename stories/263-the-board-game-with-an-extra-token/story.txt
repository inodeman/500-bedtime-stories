The clean smell of lemon soap rose warm and bright from the damp cloth as Bodhi’s small hands moved already busy in slow circles across the smooth wooden table set right beside the meadow gate where games always began. Soft late-afternoon light pooled on the boards and made the wet path of the cloth shine. Bodhi pressed harder in one spot and felt the grain of the wood, then lifted the cloth and folded it neatly against a floured towel that still held the gentle powdery drag of baking flour from earlier bread-making. Beyond the gate the grass stretched green and open, and a light breeze drifted past.

Bodhi set the board game box on the cleaned table with a soft thump and lifted the lid. Inside lay the familiar cardboard paths and little cardboard houses, plus one extra token no one had expected, a small smooth wooden disk painted sunny yellow that nestled among the usual pieces as if it had always belonged. Bodhi turned the yellow token between their fingers and felt its cool edges. Mr. Ade stood a few steps away under the fig tree, smiling without speaking, and the leaves above him whispered. Nim waited just outside the gate posts, shoes scuffing the dirt path, watching the open box with quiet interest but staying still.

“I brought the game out here so the meadow could watch us play,” Mr. Ade said in his easy low voice, and the words floated gently across the table. Bodhi looked at Nim and then at the empty chairs pulled close to the boards. The air carried the faint sweet green of crushed grass underfoot. Bodhi’s own chair scraped softly as they stood and shifted it half a step sideways, making a wider space without saying why. Nim’s shoulders relaxed a little at the new gap. Bodhi placed the yellow token near the edge of the board where anyone could reach it easily.

Wind in the fig leaves made a dry soft rustle that rose and fell like quiet breathing while Bodhi arranged the game pieces in a slower circle than usual. “We can take turns whenever we feel ready,” Bodhi said, keeping their voice calm and even so the pace itself became an invitation. Nim stepped through the gate and settled into the opened space. The wooden seat felt warm from the sun. Mr. Ade only nodded once and stayed back, letting the children decide the rest. Bodhi slid the yellow token toward the middle so it could join any path without special announcement.

“May I move the blue house first?” Nim asked, and the question hung lightly in the lemon-scented air still rising from the cloth. Bodhi answered, “Yes, and then the yellow token can travel beside it if you like.” Their fingers brushed the floured towel again as they wiped a stray crumb, feeling the soft dusty weave under their palm. The game pieces clicked gently against one another. Afternoon light slanted longer across the meadow gate and painted gold edges on every token. Bodhi watched Nim’s face brighten as the blue house and the extra yellow token traveled the cardboard road together.

They played through three quiet rounds while the breeze kept the fig leaves murmuring overhead. Bodhi changed the seating once more by pulling their own chair even closer so elbows nearly touched, and the shared warmth of the wood felt friendly. “Your turn can come right after mine, or we can go together,” Bodhi offered, and Nim chose the together way. Mr. Ade folded his arms and smiled from the shade, never interrupting. The yellow token rested under Nim’s fingertip for a long moment, smooth and solid, before sliding forward along the path. Bodhi breathed in the clean lemon soap smell that still clung to their sleeves and felt glad the table was ready for everyone.

Shadows of the gate posts stretched longer across the grass as Bodhi slowed the whole game even further so no one hurried. “Look how the yellow one fits every square,” Nim said softly, and Bodhi replied, “It does, just like any of us.” The floured towel lay folded nearby, its powdery texture catching a last bit of sun. Pieces moved with tiny friendly sounds. Bodhi kept one hand resting lightly on the table edge so the space stayed open and kind. Mr. Ade remained quiet, trusting the children to keep the circle complete.

When the cardboard path finally looped back to its start, Bodhi gathered the tokens into a gentle pile that included the extra yellow one right in the middle. Nim helped stack the little houses. The meadow grass cooled under the fading light, and the fig leaves stirred once more with their dry soft voice. Bodhi wiped the table a final slow circle with the lemon-soap cloth, then set the floured towel beside the closed box. Everyone’s hands rested easy. Mr. Ade stepped closer only to watch the neat ending.

Bodhi and Nim sat side by side a little longer while the last warmth of the day settled into the wooden boards. The meadow gate stood open and peaceful behind them. Bodhi felt the quiet satisfaction of the shared game still humming in their chest. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
