The soft tick of a tin clock filled the quiet bakery as afternoon light slanted through the front windows. Tamsin stood at the bakery counter facing the street and noticed the gentle ticking right away. It sounded like a tiny heart keeping time beside the shelves of fresh loaves. Warm air from the ovens brushed Tamsin's cheeks while they held a smooth wooden recorder in both hands. Outside, a few people walked past the glass, their footsteps muffled on the stones. Tamsin shifted from one foot to the other and listened again to that steady tick. The counter felt cool under their elbows. They had come to visit Pilar and brought the recorder because the day felt open and kind, yet a small flutter of shyness sat lightly in their chest. Golden crumbs sparkled on the wooden boards nearby.

Tamsin looked down at the recorder and thought about the very first note waiting inside the hollow wood. They had practiced the finger places at home on the quiet rug but had never blown a sound that other people might hear. Through the wide window the street glowed with soft golden light from the lowering sun, and the glass held a faint warmth where the rays touched it. Pilar, who helped bake and tidy, wiped a clear circle on the pane and smiled across at Tamsin. "Would you like to help fold some napkins while you wait?" Pilar asked in a gentle voice that blended with the kitchen hush. Tamsin nodded and stepped nearer a neat stack of cloth napkins edged with little stitched pears. The fabric felt smooth and clean against their palms as they began.

As Tamsin folded the first napkin so the stitched pear faced neatly upward, the door gave a soft chime and the ferryman stepped inside, carrying a breath of cool river air on his coat. He wore his familiar cap and held a small woven basket over one arm. "Good day, Tamsin and Pilar," the ferryman said with a warm smile that reached his eyes. "I stopped for some bread before the evening crossings on the water." His voice stayed steady and kind, easy as the calm stretches of river he knew so well. Tamsin felt the cool round shape of a polished pebble they kept in a pocket and rubbed it slowly with one thumb for quiet comfort. The pebble was perfectly smooth from many gentle touches. They kept folding so each stitched pear pointed the right way, just as Pilar preferred, while the ferryman waited without hurry.

Pilar handed the ferryman a warm roll wrapped in soft brown paper that still held oven heat. The bakery smelled gently of basil from the herb bread cooling on a wire rack near the side wall. Tamsin breathed in the fresh green scent and felt their chest ease a little. They set the recorder carefully on the counter beside the growing pile of napkins. "I brought this today," Tamsin said, nodding toward the simple wooden instrument. Their voice came out quiet yet clear enough to travel across the short space between them. The ferryman nodded once without reaching for the recorder or asking them to play. He simply rested his hands on the counter facing the street and watched a small brown bird hop along the sunny sidewalk outside the glass.

Tamsin continued folding the napkins one after another, turning each so the stitched pear sat upright and proud on the finished square. The cloth stayed soft and cool against their palms even as more warm air drifted from the back ovens. Soft shadows from the striped awning outside drifted slowly across the counter top when a thin cloud crossed the sun. They thought for a moment about leaving the recorder untouched and pretending it was only something pretty to look at, yet that choice did not sit well inside them. Instead Tamsin decided they would try the smallest brave version possible. Quiet kitchen sounds rose around them now, a wooden spoon tapping a bowl and the whisper of flour being sifted into a bin.

The ferryman leaned his elbows on the smooth cool marble of the counter and spoke in the same gentle tone. "Whatever you choose is fine with me, Tamsin. No need for any show at all." His words drifted like easy mist above the bread baskets. Tamsin picked up the recorder again. The polished wood felt familiar and steady under their fingertips. They set their fingers carefully over the holes for the very first and simplest note. Warmth from the nearby ovens reached comfortably across their back and shoulders. Outside the street had grown quieter as evening drew nearer and fewer feet passed the window. Tamsin drew a small calm breath and lifted the mouthpiece.

Then Tamsin blew the softest first note on the recorder. The sound came out clear and round, a single gentle tone that floated through the bakery air for a short sweet moment. It stayed quiet enough not to startle the bird outside yet strong enough that both the ferryman and Pilar heard it plainly over the low kitchen hum. Tamsin felt their shoulders loosen as the note faded into the warm space. The counter under their free hand remained smooth and solid and reassuring. Pilar brought her hands together in one soft clap. "That was a beautiful first note, Tamsin," Pilar said with a bright quiet smile. The ferryman simply smiled his kind smile and did not ask for another sound or any longer piece.

Tamsin set the recorder down with care and finished the last napkin, folding the cloth so the stitched pear faced up perfectly in the center. A mild cool draft from the street door brushed their ankles when an earlier customer stepped out with a bag of rolls. The ferryman settled his bread safely into the basket and thanked them both with a small nod. "I will think of that note while I row this evening," he said in his easy river voice. Tamsin felt happy and settled inside. They helped Pilar stack the finished napkins into a tidy even pile on the side board. The light inside the bakery turned softer and more golden as the sun slipped lower beyond the street-facing windows, wrapping every loaf and jar in a peaceful glow.

Later, as Tamsin prepared to walk home with the recorder tucked gently under one arm, they glanced once more around the quiet bakery. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Its gentle light mixed with the last amber of day and made the polished counter shine like still water. Tamsin touched the cool glass of the big street window and listened to the fading town sounds beyond it. They felt the friendly light weight of the recorder against their side. Pilar waved from beside the basil bread rack, and Tamsin waved back before stepping out into the mild evening air that carried a clean hint of the nearby river. The ferryman was already strolling toward the dock with his basket, and Tamsin walked the other way knowing the single clear note had been exactly enough for the soft close of the day.
