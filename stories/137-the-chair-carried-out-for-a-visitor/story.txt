The warm smell of honey drifted from open jars while Femi’s busy hands wiped each sticky rim with a soft cloth, turning the glass bright in the late afternoon light. Baskets lined both sides of the market lane, and flower pots held sleepy blooms that brushed Femi’s sleeves as they worked. Cool air moved past Femi’s cheeks. Grandma Nia hummed nearby, stacking empty baskets with careful thumps. Femi kept polishing, feeling the cloth grow damp and smooth under their fingers. “These jars shine like little suns,” Femi said, and Grandma Nia smiled without stopping her work. The lane felt calm and close, full of quiet footsteps and the gentle clink of lids.

Femi set the last jar down and ran one finger along a woven basket handle that felt rough and dry against their skin. Sunlight lay in long golden strips across the stones of the market lane, warming Femi’s bare ankles. Jars of preserves stood in neat rows beside clay pots that held trailing green leaves. Femi watched a bee hover and then drift away. Grandma Nia straightened a flower pot and brushed dirt from her apron. “A visitor is coming before supper,” Grandma Nia said, her voice easy and low. Femi nodded, already thinking of the extra chair still upstairs that always waited by the wall. The air held a mild heat that made the afternoon feel slow and kind.

Femi followed Grandma Nia toward the open doorway that faced the market lane, feeling the cooler shade slip over their shoulders like soft water. Inside the house the light turned softer, and the wooden floorboards felt smooth under Femi’s feet. Grandma Nia looked toward the stairs and sighed once, not unhappily but as if the climb already pressed on her knees. “That extra chair still upstairs would help our guest sit comfortably among the pots,” Grandma Nia said. Femi heard the words and understood at once. The chair was awkward for one person to manage alone down the narrow steps. Femi stayed quiet, simply standing near and listening to the distant murmur of the lane.

Grandma Nia started up the stairs with slow care, her hand sliding along the rail that felt cool and solid. Femi watched from below, noticing how Grandma Nia paused halfway and adjusted her grip on the banister. The extra chair waited in the upstairs room, plain wood with a worn seat, too wide for easy carrying alone. Femi’s ears caught the small click of a gate latch somewhere along the market lane, a tidy sound that marked a neighbor returning home. Warm light from the high window fell across the stairs in a quiet rectangle. Femi decided then, without speaking first, that they would take a useful half of the job.

Femi climbed after Grandma Nia and found them already beside the chair, testing its weight with both hands. The room smelled faintly of clean laundry and old wood. Femi placed their own hands on the opposite side of the chair seat, feeling the grain under their palms. “I can hold this end,” Femi said simply. Grandma Nia looked over and nodded once, her eyes soft. Together they lifted, and the chair rose between them, steady and shared. The wood felt solid, and Femi kept their steps matched to Grandma Nia’s slower ones, careful on every stair. Cooler air from the lower floor rose to meet them as they descended.

Halfway down, Grandma Nia’s free hand brushed Femi’s, and Femi felt a chalky hand texture left from the white dust that had settled on the chair rails upstairs. The soft powder rubbed gently between their fingers before fading. They kept moving, one step and then another, the chair balanced so neither person strained. Soft shadows stretched along the wall beside them. “You found the exact right moment,” Grandma Nia said, her voice warm as honey tea. Femi only smiled and kept holding firm, feeling the quiet pride of work shared without any need for extra words. The last steps came easily, and the chair touched the ground floor with a small friendly thump.

They carried the chair straight out into the market lane, where the light had turned peach-colored and gentle across the baskets and jars. Femi guided their end past a row of flower pots whose leaves brushed cool against Femi’s arm. They set the chair down near a low table already holding two cups and a small plate. The wood of the chair now caught the outdoor air and felt smooth again under Femi’s resting palm. A neighbor waved from farther along the lane. “The chair looks happy right there,” Femi said, and Grandma Nia laughed a quiet laugh that sounded like water over stones. Femi stepped back, satisfied that the useful half of the job was done.

Grandma Nia poured a little water into a flower pot while Femi stood nearby, feeling the mild evening breeze lift the edge of their shirt. They waited together through a single chime that floated from a distant clock tower, a pure soft note that hung in the air and then faded. The market lane grew quieter as sellers began to cover their baskets with cloth. Femi’s fingers rested on the rim of a clay pot that held the day’s leftover warmth. “Shall we go inside now?” Grandma Nia asked. Femi nodded, and they walked back through the doorway side by side, the chair ready behind them for whoever would sit. Inside, the house held the quiet scent of wood and the fading sweetness from the jars.

Femi washed their hands in cool water that trickled over their wrists, then dried them on a towel that felt thick and kind. Grandma Nia settled a blanket across the small sofa near the window while Femi climbed up and leaned against the cushions. Outside, the market lane had nearly emptied, leaving only the soft shapes of covered baskets and still flower pots under the first blue of evening. Femi’s eyelids grew heavy as Grandma Nia’s hand rested lightly on their shoulder. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Femi breathed in the peaceful room and let sleep come, warm and slow, while the quiet house kept its gentle watch.
