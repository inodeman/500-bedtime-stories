A faint tick reached Opal across the ceramic studio, soft and steady, and they turned their head toward the shelf where a tin clock kept gentle time between jars of glaze. Warm afternoon light lay in long stripes over the long table, and Opal felt the mild heat of it on their shoulders while they sat on the low stool. Under damp cloths the clay slip rested thick and quiet in open dishes, and new handles waited without leaning so they could set firm. Opal’s bare toes curled against the smooth cool floorboards. The air smelled of clean earth and water. Nana Ruth moved somewhere behind them with slow steps, and Opal listened to the clock a moment longer before looking back at the covered square of paper near the center of the table.

Opal knew the name card lay under that paper, and they remembered the glossy gel letter they had pressed carefully onto it earlier. The gel still needed time to harden, just as the slip and the handles needed time. Opal’s fingers rested on the edge of the table and felt the faint grit that always stayed after washing. They wanted to lift the paper right then and see if the letter shone dry and bright. The light on the table shifted a little as a cloud passed outside, turning the stripes softer. Opal leaned forward until their nose nearly touched the paper edge. The studio stayed hushed except for the clock and the quiet rustle of Nana Ruth’s apron.

“May I check the name card now, Nana Ruth?” Opal asked in a small voice. Nana Ruth came closer, and the warm scent of chamomile rose from the mug she carried. She set the mug on a clear corner of the table and smiled down at Opal. “Not quite yet, Opal,” Nana Ruth said. “The glossy gel letter still needs to finish setting, the same way the slip and the handles must stay still a while longer.” Opal looked at the covered card and then at Nana Ruth’s kind face. The steam from the mug curled upward in the golden light, and Opal felt the soft heat of it near their cheek. They kept their hands folded in their lap so they would not lift the paper too soon.

Opal decided they would leave the name card covered. They slid their stool back a little so the paper square stayed safe in the middle of the table beside the resting clay. The floor felt smooth and cool under their feet again as they stood. Nana Ruth nodded as if she understood the choice without any extra words. Opal walked a few steps to the side bench where a warm heap of clean laundry waited in a basket. The cloth felt soft and still faintly heated from the dryer, and Opal pressed their palms into it for a moment. They began to match two socks from the warm heap, finding the pair that belonged together by touch and color. The quiet task filled their hands while the gel letter and the slip kept working in their own slow way.

“These two match, Nana Ruth,” Opal said, holding up the socks so the light caught the soft knit. Nana Ruth glanced over and answered, “They do, Opal. You have a careful eye.” Opal smiled and set the pair aside, then reached for another sock in the heap. The fabric held a gentle warmth that faded slowly against their fingers. Across the room the clay tools cast short shadows, and the covered name card stayed still. Opal sorted one more pair, pressing the heels together until they lay flat. The studio air felt calm around them, neither too warm nor cool, and the work of matching kept Opal’s thoughts easy while time moved forward.

Opal carried the matched socks to a small shelf and laid them neatly. When they returned to the table they stood beside Nana Ruth and watched the cloths over the slip without touching them. A shallow tin container sat near the glaze jars, and Opal rested one hand on its tin lid, feeling the smooth cool metal under their palm. The lid made a quiet little sound when their finger tapped it once, then they left it alone. Nana Ruth poured a little more warm water into her mug, and the light from the windows grew softer as the afternoon leaned toward evening. Opal’s shoulders relaxed. They thought of the glossy gel letter waiting under its paper and felt no hurry now.

“Will the letter be ready soon?” Opal asked after a stretch of quiet. Nana Ruth looked at the covered name card and at the resting handles. “It will finish in its own time, Opal,” she said. “You chose well to leave it covered.” Opal nodded and leaned their elbows lightly on the table edge, careful not to shake anything. The clay smell mixed with the last of the tea steam. They watched a thin ribbon of light travel across the floorboards toward the wall. Somewhere a drawer closed with a soft wooden sound, and Opal stayed close to Nana Ruth, content to wait while their hands rested still.

Opal returned once more to the warm heap and finished matching the last two socks, pressing them together until the cuffs lined up. Then they washed their hands at the low sink, feeling the cool water and the smooth soap, and dried them on a soft towel. Back at the table Opal stood very quiet and looked at the paper square. Nana Ruth reached out and gently lifted one corner of the covering. The glossy gel letter on the name card had set clear and bright, no longer wet. Opal’s eyes widened with gladness. The slip in the dishes looked firmer too, and the handles held their shapes. Nana Ruth laid the paper aside, and Opal touched only the dry edge of the name card with one careful fingertip.

Opal held the finished name card in both hands and felt the slight weight of it while Nana Ruth smiled. The clay work around them had settled into readiness in the same unhurried stretch of time. Opal carried the card to a safe dry shelf and set it where the last light could touch the glossy letter. Outside the windows the sky turned the color of soft blue cloth. Nana Ruth gathered the empty mug and the matched socks, and Opal walked beside her toward the quieter corner of the room. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Opal stood near it for a moment longer, feeling the gentle warmth on their face, and knew the gel letter and the clay had both finished well.
