The small card with Milo’s spoken part rested on the low stage edge, its corners catching the warm yellow light from the school room lamps, and the thin black letters looked almost too quiet for a lantern walk. Milo touched the paper edge with one careful finger and felt how smooth and dry it was, like a leaf that had never been outside. Mama sat nearby on a wooden chair that smelled faintly of chalk dust, and the room held a soft hush broken only by the shuffle of little shoes. Milo kept their eyes on the card and whispered the words once under their breath, tasting the shy shape of them. Outside the tall windows the afternoon light turned honey-colored and settled on the floorboards in long gentle stripes.

Milo felt the shyness sit in their chest like a warm mitten that was a little too thick, and they curled their toes inside their socks while Mama smiled without saying hurry. The air in the small school room carried a mild scent of wet wool from the coats hanging by the door after the damp walk from home. Yara sat cross-legged on the rug and waved a tiny wave, the kind that meant no rush at all. Milo looked at Mama and then at the low stage edge again, hearing their own breath move in and out. “I can try just a little,” Milo said, the words soft but clear enough to reach the first row of chairs.

Mama nodded and kept her voice kind and even. “That sounds just right, Milo,” she answered, and the quiet approval felt like a blanket laid across Milo’s shoulders. The tin clock on the teacher’s desk gave a single bright tick that marked the minute without startling anyone. Milo stood up slowly, the soles of their shoes cool against the smooth floor, and stepped onto the low stage edge where the wood felt steady underfoot. They held the card in both hands so the light could fall across the short lines they had practiced at home. Yara clapped once very gently, more like a soft pat than a noise, and the room stayed peaceful.

Milo drew a slow breath that tasted of the clean air near the open window crack and decided not to pretend the shyness was gone. Instead they chose the smallest brave version, the kind that still let the words be heard by Mama and Yara and the empty chairs that waited like friendly listeners. “My lantern shines for all of us tonight,” Milo said, voice steady enough to carry across the quiet space yet soft as a bedtime page. The sound of their own speaking settled into the room like warm tea steam, and Milo felt their shoulders loosen a little. Mama’s eyes shone with ordinary happiness, the kind that needed no bigger show.

After the first try Milo stepped down and sat on the edge of the stage where the wood was smooth and cool under their palms. Yara scooted closer and offered a shared smile that needed no words for a moment. “You said it so we could hear,” Yara whispered, and the whisper floated like a feather. Milo nodded and touched the card again, feeling proud of the quiet choice they had made. The lamps overhead hummed a tiny steady note that mixed with the far-off sound of a car rolling past the school. Mama reached over and smoothed Milo’s hair with a warm hand that smelled faintly of the soap from the kitchen sink at home.

They tried the part one more time because Milo wanted to, not because anyone asked. Milo stood on the low stage edge once more, the light from the window now softer and pinker as afternoon leaned toward evening, and spoke the short line again with the same small bravery. “My lantern shines for all of us tonight,” Milo repeated, and this time the words felt familiar like a favorite sock. Mama stayed right where she was, kind and unhurried, offering no push toward anything larger. The temperature in the room stayed gently warm, the kind that made eyelids feel pleasantly heavy in a good way.

Yara stood too and practiced a quiet step of her own beside Milo, their two pairs of feet making almost no sound on the stage wood. “We can walk slowly with the lanterns later,” Yara said, and Milo answered, “Yes, slow is good.” The simple plan settled between them like a shared pillow. Milo folded the card carefully so the paper would stay neat in their pocket. Mama watched the two friends with the calm look that said everything was already enough. A soft draft from the hallway carried the distant scent of crayons and clean erasers, wrapping the moment in ordinary school comfort.

When practice felt finished Milo and Mama and Yara sat together on the rug for a short quiet stretch, the kind where no one needed to talk unless they wanted. Milo leaned against Mama’s side and felt the steady rise and fall of her breathing, warm and even through her sweater. The lamps glowed lower now, casting round pools of light that made the low stage edge look like the shore of a tiny calm lake. Milo thought about the lantern walk that would come later and how their small spoken part would fit inside it without needing to be loud. They breathed in and out once more, matching the peaceful pace of the room, and felt the shyness loosen into something softer and lighter.

Mama gathered the coats while Yara found Milo’s backpack, and the three of them moved toward the door in an easy line. Milo kept the folded card safe in their pocket where it rested against their side like a friendly secret. Outside the windows the sky had turned the gentle blue that comes before true night, and the school room lights reflected in the glass like quiet stars. “Thank you for listening,” Milo told Mama, and Mama answered, “I loved hearing you.” They stepped into the hallway where the floor tiles felt cool underfoot and the air smelled clean and ready for home. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
