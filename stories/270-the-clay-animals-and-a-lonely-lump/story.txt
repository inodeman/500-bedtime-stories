A tiny click sounded near the pillows, like a wooden bead settling, and Ines lifted their head from the warm blanket to listen. Soft afternoon light filtered through the curtains and laid gold stripes across the bedroom rug of cushions, puppets, and paper. Ines scooted closer on their knees, feeling the thick weave of the rug against their palms, and saw how the paper animals lay scattered as if waiting for someone to finish the play. The air felt gentle and still, and Ines breathed in the quiet while their fingers brushed a folded paper wing. “Papa, the little fox puppet fell over again,” Ines said, and Papa smiled from the doorway without stepping in to fix it. Ines set the puppet upright and patted the nearest cushion, already planning where everyone might sit once Nia came fully into the circle.

Ines remembered that Nia stood just outside the ring of cushions, close enough to watch but not quite inside the fun, and a smooth lump of clay rested near Nia’s shoes like a quiet friend that had not yet been shared. The clay felt cool when Ines rolled it once between their hands, and the scent of tomato vines drifted in from the open window where Papa had set a small pot earlier. Ines looked at Nia’s careful face and decided the seating needed a change so the circle would open naturally. They slid two cushions sideways and made a wider nest of paper paths between the puppets. “Nia, the clay can live right here with the paper river,” Ines said in a soft voice, and Nia’s shoulders eased. Papa stayed back and only nodded once, letting the children arrange the space themselves.

Ines slowed the pace of the game so that rolling and pressing the clay became part of the story instead of a prize handed out with fanfare. They pressed their thumb into the lump and shaped a round sheep while the paper cows waited nearby, and the room felt pleasantly cool near the floor. Nia settled onto the cushion Ines had moved, and together they pressed tiny ears and tails. “We can make the sheep walk along the paper path first,” Ines whispered, and Nia answered, “Then the puppets can greet them.” The grain of the bench under the window felt faintly rough when Ines rested one elbow there for a moment while thinking of the next shape. No one announced any gift; the clay simply became shared work under their quiet hands.

Ines kept the rules gentle by inviting turns without counting them aloud, and they patted a place for Nia’s knees so the friend sat level with the puppets. Warm light touched the clay as it warmed in their palms, and Ines shaped a second animal that looked like a sturdy little horse. The cushions smelled faintly of clean cotton when Nia leaned against one, and the paper rustled under careful fingers. Papa watched from his chair across the room and stayed quiet, trusting the children to find the right rhythm. Ines handed Nia half the clay without ceremony so both could roll and press at the same time, and the bedroom felt full of soft concentration rather than waiting.

Ines listened for a moment to the far clock ticking beyond the hallway instead of the nearer one on the shelf, and the slower sound helped them keep the play unhurried. They flattened a piece of paper into a bridge and set the clay horse upon it while Nia added tiny clay footprints beside it. Cool air moved across Ines’s ankles from the open window, and they smiled when Nia’s clay sheep joined the procession. “The bridge is strong enough for both,” Ines said, and Nia nodded as they pressed a little more clay into place. The puppets leaned in as if watching, and the cushions held everyone steady without any need to call attention to who sat where.

Ines changed the seating once more by tucking an extra cushion behind Nia so the friend could lean back while still reaching the paper and the clay. Soft shadows stretched longer across the rug, and the texture of the clay grew smoother under repeated rolling. Ines and Nia made a third animal together, a small dog with careful legs, and set it beside the others so the parade felt complete. Papa remained in his place and only offered a quiet smile when Ines glanced up. The children kept their voices low and kind, and the room filled with the small sounds of paper sliding and clay being shaped into calm companions.

Ines noticed how Nia’s hands moved more freely now that the circle held a true open space, and they slowed again to let Nia decide where the clay dog should rest for the night. Warmth rose from the floor lamp in the corner and touched Ines’s cheek while they waited. “We can leave the animals sleeping on the cushions until morning,” Ines suggested, and Nia replied, “They will like the soft places.” The cat’s collar bell chimed once from the hallway as the cat wandered past, a bright tiny sound that made both children pause and smile. Ines felt glad that the lump of clay had become shared shapes without any announcement, and Papa’s quiet presence kept the whole afternoon gentle.

Ines gathered the leftover scraps of paper into a neat pile and brushed a few clay crumbs into a small dish, feeling the smooth rim under their fingers. Cool evening air slipped farther into the room, and the cushions held the shapes of where they had sat. Nia helped tuck the puppets into a loose row so they faced the clay animals like friendly neighbors. Ines spoke once more, saying, “Tomorrow we can add a paper barn if we want,” and Nia agreed with a soft yes. Papa rose only to dim the brighter light, still leaving the solving to the children who had already found their places.

Ines and Nia sat side by side on the rearranged cushions while the clay animals rested in the middle of the paper paths, and the bedroom grew quieter as night settled. Ines felt the last warmth of the day fade from the rug under their legs and listened to Nia’s calm breathing nearby. Papa stepped closer only long enough to smooth a blanket over both children without disturbing the little parade they had made. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Ines closed their eyes with the scent of the evening air still gentle around them and the shared clay shapes safe in the circle they had opened together.
