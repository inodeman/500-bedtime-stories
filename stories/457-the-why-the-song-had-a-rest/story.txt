The warm smell of honey drifted from a sticky jar someone had left open near the cushions as Remy’s hands already busied themselves smoothing the frayed edge of a blue blanket on the library window seat. Late afternoon light poured through the tall glass in gentle gold stripes that warmed Remy’s knees and made the dust motes dance slowly. Remy pressed their palms flat against the soft fabric again and again, feeling how the weave held the day’s leftover heat. Outside, bare trees stood still against a pale sky, and the whole room felt hushed and safe. Remy wriggled a little deeper into the seat, still working the blanket with careful fingers, while the honey scent mixed with the quiet paper smell of a hundred waiting books.

A tall figure in a worn coat settled on the far end of the same wide seat, and Remy looked up to see the ferryman resting a small folded paper on one knee. The ferryman’s coat carried the cool damp of river air, and the wooden floor creaked once under his boots as he shifted. Remy kept one hand on the blanket and watched the way the light caught the silver buttons on the coat. “I brought something for you today,” the ferryman said in a low voice that matched the soft room. Remy nodded but felt a quick bubble of bigger news rise in their chest, news about a drawing they had made that morning. The ferryman smiled and waited, and the window glass felt cool when Remy leaned a shoulder against it.

The ferryman unfolded the paper so Remy could see neat black notes drawn across five thin lines. “This song needed a rest right in the middle,” he began, tapping the empty space with one careful finger. Remy’s fingers stilled on the blanket. The light through the window turned warmer and lower, painting orange shapes across the paper. Remy wanted to blurt out the news about the drawing right then, yet the ferryman’s voice stayed calm and steady, so Remy only breathed in and kept listening. The seat cushion held a gentle spring under both of them, and the air felt still enough to hear pages turning somewhere deeper in the library.

“A rest is not an ending,” the ferryman continued. “It is a quiet place where the notes can catch their breath so the next part can sound clearer.” Remy watched his chalky hand move slowly across the paper, the dry powdery texture leaving the faintest white mark on the edge of one line. The explanation unrolled like a short river path: the song had climbed high, then needed that open pause so the low notes afterward could feel round and kind instead of rushed. Remy’s own hands stayed folded now, still wanting to speak, yet staying quiet all the way through each sentence. The window seat fabric brushed softly against Remy’s wrists with every small shift.

Outside, the faint click of a gate latch drifted up from the garden path far below, a single clear sound that came and went without hurry. The ferryman kept talking in the same even tone, showing how the rest sat between two phrases like a soft bridge. Remy listened to the last full sentence about how the rest let the singer smile before finishing the tune. The bigger news still pressed behind Remy’s teeth, but they held it carefully, the way one holds a cup without spilling. Warm light slid higher on the wall, and the library air tasted faintly sweet from the open honey jar still sitting nearby.

When the ferryman finished and set the paper down, Remy waited one more breath and then asked the one real question that had grown while they listened. “Does the rest feel lonely while it waits?” Remy’s voice stayed small and curious. The ferryman shook his head gently. “No,” he answered. “The rest knows the song is still holding it, the way this seat holds both of us right now.” Remy felt the truth of that settle in their chest like a warm stone. The window glass had cooled further, and Remy’s bare toes curled against the cushion’s edge.

Only then did Remy share something small of their own. “I drew a boat with yellow sails this morning,” they said, voice soft as the fading light. The ferryman’s eyes crinkled. “I would like to see that boat someday,” he replied. Remy smiled and smoothed the blanket one last time, feeling how the fibers had grown friendlier under their busy hands. The two of them sat side by side while the library grew quieter still, and the honey scent had nearly faded into the paper smell of the shelves.

Remy leaned their head against the cool glass and watched the sky deepen to soft blue. The ferryman folded the paper again with slow care and tucked it into his coat. No one needed to hurry. Remy’s earlier wish to interrupt had melted into a quiet gladness that they had heard every word first. The seat held them both without complaint, and the last stripe of sunlight slipped across the floorboards like a friendly cat finding its evening spot.

The lantern by the window glowed softly. Remy closed their eyes for a moment and felt the gentle weight of the blanket, the memory of the chalky hand on the paper, and the calm that came after listening all the way through. The ferryman stayed nearby without speaking, and the library window seat kept its quiet promise of rest until morning light would return.
