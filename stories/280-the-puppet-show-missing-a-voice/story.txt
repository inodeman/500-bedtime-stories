The puppet that had no voice yet rested on a curl of paper at the rim of the bedroom rug, its button eyes dull in the low lamp light and its cloth mouth sealed in a calm stitched line. Isla bent so close that the cool evening air from the cracked window brushed their cheek, and they traced the springy yarn hair with one careful finger while the rest of the room waited in hush. Soft carpet fibers pressed warm against their bare knees. The puppet felt lighter than a mitten and still somehow ready, as if a story sat inside it without a sound to carry it out.

Papa settled on a fat cushion with a linen napkin folded neat across his lap and began stacking paper hats beside a row of other puppets already waiting. Isla hummed under their breath and slid more cushions into a loose half-circle that left one clear space near the paper pile. Zev stood just beyond the edge of the rug in sock feet, watching the bright scraps and the silent puppet without crossing onto the soft island of play. A faint sweet smell of orange peel drifted from the little bowl on the dresser where Papa had left the peels after snack.

“We could start with the fox puppet first,” Isla said, holding up a different one whose fuzzy ears caught the lamp glow. Papa only smiled and kept folding, the quiet tick of a tin clock drifting from the hallway shelf like a patient heartbeat. Zev shifted from foot to foot and let one hand rest on the doorframe, close enough to see everything yet still outside the circle of cushions. Isla noticed the way the paper rustled when the heater breathed warm air across the floor and decided the seating needed a small change rather than any big announcement.

Isla scooted two cushions sideways so a new nest opened right beside the quiet puppet, then patted the spot once with an open palm. “This place is for whoever wants to sit and listen first,” they said in a low voice meant only for the room. Zev took one slow step onto the rug and sank onto that cushion, the fabric cool at first then warming under his legs. Papa stayed quiet on his own cushion and let the children arrange the paper and the puppets however they liked, the lamp light pooling gold across the floor like spilled honey.

Isla placed the puppet that had no voice yet gently into Zev’s open hands so he could feel the soft cotton body and the loose thread at its neck. “Maybe it just needs us to take turns giving it words,” Isla whispered, and Zev nodded while the paper under the cushions made tiny crinkling sounds whenever anyone moved. They agreed without any prize or loud cheer that Zev would speak first for the quiet puppet while Isla moved the other ones in slow arcs above the paper stage. The air smelled clean and the temperature stayed gentle, perfect for small voices.

“Once upon a time the quiet one wanted to say good evening to the moon,” Zev offered, lifting the puppet so its yarn hair brushed the warm lamp light. Isla answered with the fox puppet in a soft matching tone, “And the moon answered with silver light across every cushion.” Papa watched from his place and only reached out once to steady a tipping paper hat, his smile quiet and sure. The children kept the pace slow on purpose, pausing between each spoken line so no one felt hurried and so every word had room to settle like dust motes in the glow.

Isla changed the simple rule again by whispering that anyone could pass the quiet puppet after two lines, and Zev passed it back with careful hands when his turn felt finished. “Your turn to find the next words,” Zev said, and Isla took the puppet and let it bow toward the paper river they had drawn earlier. The cushions held them both in a snug half-ring while Papa remained nearby yet uninvolved in the fixing, trusting the new seating and the gentle pace to do the work. Soft carpet texture met bare toes whenever Isla stretched a foot, and the whole room felt wrapped in ordinary kindness.

They played three short scenes that way, voices rising only as high as a bedtime murmur, until the quiet puppet had borrowed enough words to feel complete for the night. Isla set it upright against a cushion so it could “watch” the last paper boat float across the rug, and Zev leaned a shoulder companionably against Isla’s without needing any extra words. Papa collected the unused hats into a neat stack and smoothed the linen napkin one final time, the house around them settling deeper into evening stillness. Everything smelled faintly sweet still, and the lamp threw long kind shadows that made the puppets look almost ready to rest.

Isla and Zev tucked the last scraps of paper into a shallow box while Papa dimmed the main light until only the small glow remained. They sat a moment longer on the cushions, breathing the calm air and listening to the distant hush of the house, then Isla rested a hand lightly on the puppet that had found its borrowed voice. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
