The soft scrape of a chair leg against wooden floorboards whispered through the quiet room first. Tala heard that small sound and sat up under the blanket, feeling the cool evening air drift across their bare arms while soft golden lamp light pooled on the walls. Papa stood near the door with his coat already on, and the faint tick of something metallic waited farther off in the kitchen. Tala rubbed sleep from their eyes and watched dust motes float in the warm glow. Outside the window the sky had turned the color of deep blue cloth. Papa smiled when he saw Tala awake and ready.

“The parcels need changing hands down at the harbor step tonight,” Papa said in a low voice that matched the hush of the house. Tala slid from the bed and felt the smooth boards under their feet, still holding the leftover warmth of the day. They pulled on soft socks while Papa waited patiently by the open door. A gentle breeze carried the distant call of a gull through the hallway. Tala nodded and took Papa’s hand, the two of them stepping into the mild dusk together. Street lamps threw long gentle circles of light ahead of their path.

They walked the short path that smelled faintly of wet rope and evening dampness, and soon the harbor step appeared below them like a wide stone bench beside the water. Lanterns hung from posts and cast steady yellow light across the boards where people passed wrapped packages from one set of hands to another. Tala stood close to Papa and watched the careful exchange. The air felt cooler here near the water, and small waves made a soft rhythmic lap against the pilings. Papa set down a wooden box of parcels and lifted the family’s own lantern so he could see the labels clearly.

The lantern glass was smudged with many fingerprints, and the light inside looked cloudy and dim. Papa tried to hold three parcels against his chest while wiping the glass with his free sleeve, but the glass stayed streaked and awkward. Tala saw the way Papa’s shoulders shifted and how he kept pausing to peer through the blurred spots. A kind-faced child named Dae stood nearby waiting for a small bundle and gave Tala a quiet wave. Warm light from other lanterns flickered across Dae’s freckled cheeks. Papa muttered softly to himself as he juggled the load.

“I can hold these while you look at the names,” Tala said, reaching for two of the lighter parcels so Papa’s hands were freer. Papa glanced down and nodded with a grateful look that needed no extra words. Tala settled the packages carefully against their own side and felt the solid weight press gently through their sweater. The stone step underfoot still held a little daytime heat. Dae stepped closer and received one finished parcel with both hands cupped ready. Water lapped again in a calm repeating pattern that matched Tala’s breathing.

Papa still struggled a little with the remaining smudge, so Tala noticed the linen napkin tucked in Papa’s coat pocket and drew it out without a word. The linen napkin felt dry and lightly woven against Tala’s fingers as they polished the glass in slow careful circles until the fingerprints faded and the light shone clear again. Papa watched for a moment and then returned to reading the labels with ease. “That helps more than you know,” Papa said quietly while passing the next parcel to a waiting boatman. Tala simply kept holding the rest of the stack steady and smiled at the clean bright circle of light now resting on the step.

After the last package changed hands, Papa peeled a bright orange and the sweet sharp smell of orange peel rose into the cool harbor air around them. Tala and Papa shared the sections while sitting side by side on the wide stone, the lantern glowing steadily at their feet. Dae waved goodbye and walked home along the pier under the soft string of lights. Tala listened to the gentle creak of ropes and felt content. Papa packed the empty box and the clean lantern, and together they started the short walk back through streets that smelled clean after the day.

Back inside the quiet house Tala washed their hands at the kitchen sink and, for a calm stretch of minutes, counted twelve drips from the tap without peeking, just listening to each soft drop land. The familiar tick of the tin clock marked the slow peaceful seconds while Papa hung their coats. Warm steam rose faintly from the kettle Papa set to heat for bedtime tea. Tala dried their hands on a towel and followed Papa down the short hall, the floorboards cool once more under socked feet. Soft lamp light waited in Tala’s room like a friend who had stayed awake.

Tala climbed into bed and felt the blankets settle in a gentle weight across their shoulders while Papa sat nearby for a short while. Outside the window the harbor lights had grown small and distant. Papa rested a hand lightly on the quilt and spoke one last quiet sentence. “Rest well, Tala,” Papa said. Tala answered, “Good night, Papa,” already feeling their eyes grow heavy in the safe dimness. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
