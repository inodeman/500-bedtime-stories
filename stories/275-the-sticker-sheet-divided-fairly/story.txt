The warm smell of beeswax rose from the little polished tin of crayons resting near Jorie’s busy hands as they pressed a green paper square flat against the soft bedroom rug of cushions, puppets, and paper. Afternoon light slanted through the open window and made the crayons gleam like tiny honey drops while Jorie’s fingers smoothed every edge with careful strokes. The rug felt cool and slightly springy under their knees, and the paper whispered each time they moved it. Puppets with button eyes sat in a loose circle, waiting. Jorie hummed a quiet tune under their breath, already arranging more paper shapes beside a bright sticker sheet that lay untouched in the middle of everything.

Papa sat nearby on a low cushion, the fabric warm from the sun, and watched without speaking while the room stayed peaceful and bright. Jorie glanced at the stickers—rows of stars, moons, and tiny leaves printed on shiny backing—and then noticed Ines standing just beyond the edge of the rug, close enough to see every color yet not quite inside the circle. The air carried a gentle coolness from the open window. Jorie’s bare feet brushed a cushion that felt plush and yielding. “Ines, the rug has room,” Jorie said softly, voice steady as they kept their hands moving. Ines shifted from one foot to the other, eyes on the sticker sheet that could be divided before anyone asked.

Jorie scooted two cushions sideways with a slow sliding sound against the floorboards, making a clear open space that faced the paper and the puppets directly. The light on the wall grew softer as a cloud drifted past. “You can sit right here beside me,” Jorie told Ines, patting the new spot so the invitation felt ordinary and true. Ines stepped onto the rug and settled down; the cushion sighed under them like a quiet breath. Jorie kept arranging paper without pause, changing the pace so everything simply opened wider. Papa remained still on his own cushion, letting the children find their way, the warmth of the room steady around them all.

Together they looked at the sticker sheet while a faint breeze stirred the curtains and brought the sound of wind in the fig leaves outside the window. Jorie peeled one corner of the backing with a soft rip and held the sheet so both could see. “We can choose the ones we like and put them on our papers,” Jorie said. Ines nodded and reached for a silver moon. The stickers lifted easily, their edges cool and smooth under fingertips. Jorie placed a yellow star on their own green square, pressing firmly so it stayed. The room smelled clean and calm, and the paper felt dry and ready beneath their palms.

They worked side by side, adding stickers one by one while the puppets watched with stitched smiles. Jorie handed Ines a small leaf-shaped sticker and said, “This one matches the green.” Ines smiled and pressed it down. A real leaf from the fig tree had drifted in earlier and lay near the edge of the rug; Jorie picked it up gently and turned it over so both could feel a leaf’s soft underside, velvety and cool against their skin. The texture made them both pause for a moment of quiet wonder. Papa’s quiet presence stayed nearby like a steady lamp, never interrupting as the children decided everything themselves.

Jorie moved one puppet closer to Ines so the cloth figure sat between them, its yarn hair soft to the touch. The light in the room turned golden and slow. They took turns making the puppet bow and speak in tiny voices that stayed gentle. “Hello, friend,” the puppet seemed to say through Jorie’s careful hands. Ines answered for another puppet, “Hello back.” The game stretched without hurry, and the sticker sheet emptied fairly between their two papers until each held bright shapes that sparkled when turned toward the window. The floorboards felt smooth and solid under their seated legs.

Outside, the wind in the fig leaves continued its soft rustle, a steady sound that filled the quiet pauses between their words. Jorie gathered the leftover backing paper and folded it neatly, the material crinkling like dry grass. Ines helped stack the crayons back into their beeswax-scented tin. The temperature in the bedroom stayed comfortably warm, like a held blanket. They leaned the finished papers against a cushion so the stickers caught the last slanted light and glowed. Papa smiled once but still said nothing, trusting the calm space the children had made.

As the day eased toward evening, Jorie and Ines rested on their cushions and watched a small snail that had climbed onto the windowsill brick, moving so slowly it seemed to draw a silver path across one whole brick while they breathed in time with it. The snail’s shell looked pale and ridged in the fading light. Jorie’s shoulder touched Ines’s lightly, fabric soft against fabric. They stayed still together, listening to the distant leaves and feeling the rug’s gentle give beneath them. No one rushed. The puppets rested in a friendly heap, and the papers with their shared stickers lay safe nearby.

Later Papa helped them tuck the cushions into a neat pile while the room cooled just enough to feel restful. Jorie climbed into bed with the paper creation placed carefully on the nearby table, and Ines waved goodnight from the doorway before heading home. The sheets felt smooth and cool against Jorie’s arms. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Outside the fig leaves whispered one last time, and Jorie closed their eyes with the quiet weight of the day settled kindly around them.
