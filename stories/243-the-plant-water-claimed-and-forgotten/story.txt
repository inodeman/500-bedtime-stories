The clean smell of lemon soap still clung to Kavi’s busy fingers as they turned the little clay pot this way and that on the wide library table by the round window. Soft gold light spilled across the wood and warmed the backs of Kavi’s hands while they tried to tuck a stray leaf behind another. The plant’s soil felt dry and powdery under one fingertip, and Kavi’s stomach gave a quiet flutter because they had told everyone earlier that the watering was already done. Outside the round glass the afternoon stayed gentle, and Kavi kept their shoulders rounded over the pot as if that might hide the thirsty stems.

Kavi heard the soft scrape of a chair leg and knew Miss Dalca was nearby, so they slid the pot a little closer to the edge of a tall stack of picture books. The leaves brushed Kavi’s wrist with a papery tickle, and for one long breath Kavi almost pushed the whole plant behind the books completely. Warm air drifted in through the slightly open window, carrying the distant sound of wind in the fig leaves that grew just beyond the glass. Kavi froze with both palms still on the pot and listened to those leaves whisper against one another, then slowly drew the plant back into the open circle of light on the table.

Miss Dalca stepped closer, her shoes quiet on the carpet, and the lemon-bright scent of the soap still rose faintly whenever Kavi moved. “Kavi, is that the plant from the reading corner?” she asked in her calm low voice. Kavi nodded once and kept their eyes on the dry soil. A thin ribbon of cooler air touched Kavi’s cheek from the window, and the plant’s top leaves drooped as if they too were waiting. Kavi’s fingers curled around the rim of the pot, feeling the rough clay grit, and the wish to hide the truth sat heavy in their chest for another heartbeat.

Then Kavi lifted their chin and spoke the plain words. “I claimed I watered it this morning, Miss Dalca, but I forgot,” Kavi said. The sentence came out steady even though Kavi’s ears felt hot. Miss Dalca did not laugh or frown; she simply pulled another chair close so she sat level with Kavi at the library table by the round window. “Thank you for telling me straight away,” Miss Dalca answered. “We can mend what needs mending together.” The light through the round window stayed soft and golden, and Kavi felt the tightness in their shoulders loosen like a knot slipping free.

Miss Dalca reached into her canvas bag and drew out a clean floured towel, its surface still dusted with the fine white powder from the morning’s bread baking. She spread the towel across one corner of the table so any spilled drops would stay neat. Kavi touched the cloth and felt the soft dusty texture under their palm, cool and slightly grainy. Together they carried the pot to the low sink at the side of the room, and Kavi held the pot steady while Miss Dalca poured a slow stream of cool water until the soil turned dark and rich. The plant’s leaves seemed to lift a little already, and Kavi watched a single bead of water roll down one green edge and catch the light.

Back at the table they set the pot on the floured towel to drain. “Would you like to wipe the wood while we wait?” Miss Dalca asked. Kavi nodded and took a damp cloth, moving it in slow circles across the smooth table surface. The circles left faint gleaming paths that smelled faintly of the same lemon soap Kavi had used earlier, and the motion felt quiet and good. Outside, the wind in the fig leaves rose and fell like a distant lullaby, and Kavi’s hands kept making the gentle circles until every water spot disappeared. Miss Dalca smiled and said, “The plant will drink slowly now, and tomorrow the leaves will stand taller.”

Kavi pressed one careful fingertip to a leaf and felt its cooler, smoother surface. “I almost hid it behind the books,” Kavi admitted. Miss Dalca rested her elbow on the table and answered, “You chose to show it and tell the truth instead, and that lets us help.” The round window framed a slice of soft sky, and the library around them stayed hushed except for the far-off rustle of pages turning in another corner. Kavi breathed in the clean air and felt the last of the worried flutter leave their chest. They straightened the pot so it sat exactly in the center of the towel, then brushed a few grains of flour from the wood with the side of their hand.

Miss Dalca brought two small cups of warm milk from the staff nook and set them down without a clink. Kavi wrapped both hands around the cup and felt the gentle heat seep into their palms while they sipped. The milk tasted mild and comforting, and the light through the round window had begun to turn the color of honey. “Next time I will water it when I say I will,” Kavi said quietly. Miss Dalca nodded and replied, “I know you will, and I am glad we fixed this side by side.” They sat without hurry, watching the plant’s soil settle and darken evenly, and the whole library table by the round window felt peaceful again.

Kavi stacked the picture books into a tidy tower, smoothed the floured towel one last time, and rested both elbows on the clean wood while the sky outside deepened. The plant stood between them like a small green promise, its leaves no longer dusty or limp. Miss Dalca turned the lamp near the window to its lowest glow, and the room filled with a hush that matched the slowing of Kavi’s own breathing. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Kavi’s eyes grew heavy as they listened to the quiet settling of the library and the far gentle movement of leaves, and they knew the plant and the truth and the calm table would all still be there when morning came.
