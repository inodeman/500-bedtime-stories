Soft evening mist brushed the library window seat with a cool damp breath that made the glass beads of rain glisten like quiet pearls. Sora curled sideways on the deep cushion, knees tucked, and felt the mild chill kiss their bare ankles while the last pale light of day settled across their hands. The river beyond the garden still murmured under the mist, and Sora knew the ferryman would soon climb the stone steps and join them, just as he had promised that morning. Sora pressed one palm flat to the warm wood of the seat frame and watched a single drop race another down the pane. The library smelled of old paper and beeswax, and far away a page turned with a soft hush. Sora waited without fidgeting, eyes on the path that led from the water.

The ferryman arrived with his coat still holding the cool river air, and he settled opposite Sora on the wide window seat as if it were the most ordinary place in the world for a man who spent his days poling a wooden boat. He carried no oars tonight, only a small cloth bag that clinked once when he set it down. Sora felt the seat dip a little under his weight and heard the faint creak of old springs. Warm yellow light from a nearby lamp fell across the ferryman’s weathered knuckles as he untied the bag. Inside lay two small brass bells, one with a thin silver rim and one plain. He held them out so Sora could see the soft shine. “These came from the boathouse shelf,” he said, voice low and steady. “I brought them so we could talk about how to hear the difference between two bells.”

Sora’s chest filled with a sudden bright bubble of bigger news they had carried all day, something about a drawing they had finished and a promise from Lumen, yet they pressed their lips together and only nodded. The ferryman lifted the first bell and gave it the gentlest shake. A clear high note rang out and faded into the quiet shelves. He waited, then shook the second. The tone was almost the same, yet a shade lower and softer around the edges, like a voice speaking from farther away. Sora listened hard, feeling the cool mist still seeping through the cracked window and smelling, just once, a faint green trace of basil that must have drifted in from the kitchen herb pots downstairs. The two notes hung in the air side by side, and Sora kept still so they would not miss the last trembling of either sound.

“The difference hides in the space after the ring,” the ferryman continued, turning the bells slowly in his hands so the lamp light slid across them. “One keeps singing a little longer in your ear. The other drops away clean, like a pebble settling on river sand.” He set both bells on the cushion between them. Sora watched the brass catch tiny reflections of rain and felt a polished pebble the ferryman had also brought, smooth and cool, roll gently against their wrist when the bag tipped. They wanted to speak right then about the news, yet they only curled their fingers around the pebble’s even surface and waited. The library around them stayed hushed except for the distant soft tread of someone shelving books.

He picked up the higher bell again and rang it once more, letting Sora lean close. “Close your eyes if you like,” he said. “Feel how the sound sits in the bones near your ear. The silver-rimmed one leaves a bright thread. The plain one leaves a rounder hum.” Sora closed their eyes and heard the two notes carefully, one after the other. Between rings the quiet room held only the slow tick of a tin clock on the librarian’s desk, a sound small and steady as rain on a roof. Sora kept their eyes shut until both tones had vanished completely, then opened them again to the soft lamp glow and the ferryman’s patient face. The bigger news still pressed behind their teeth, yet they stayed silent and ready for whatever came next.

The ferryman rang each bell a final time, slower now, and spoke the last sentence of his explanation in the same calm tone he used when guiding the boat through morning fog. “When you can tell which voice belongs to which bell without looking, you will know you have listened all the way through.” He placed both bells back into the cloth bag and drew the string closed. Sora felt the warm blanket still draped over their legs and the cooler air near the glass. They had not interrupted once. The whole telling had unfolded like a quiet current, and now it rested. Sora sat up a little straighter on the cushion, the polished wood of the seat firm beneath them, and looked at the ferryman with clear eyes.

“Why does the longer note feel brighter even though both bells are almost the same size?” Sora asked, the one real question that had grown while they listened. The ferryman smiled and answered without hurry. “Because the silver rim lets the metal keep moving a breath longer after the clapper strikes. That extra breath is what your ear remembers as brightness.” Sora nodded, turning the cool pebble once more between their fingers, and felt the gentle weight of understanding settle. Outside the mist had thinned, and a softer evening light now touched the wet leaves of the garden. The library air smelled clean and paper-sweet again.

Only then did Sora share the small thing of their own. “I finished the river drawing today,” they said, voice soft. “Lumen helped me mix the blue so it matched the water under the bridge.” The ferryman listened as carefully as Sora had listened to him, nodding once. “That sounds like good work,” he replied. Sora smiled and set the pebble back beside the bag. They both sat quiet for a moment, hearing only the distant tick fade and the faint brush of rain that had almost stopped. The window seat held them safely above the garden, and the last cool air of dusk slipped in like a friendly sigh.

Sora leaned their head against the wooden frame and watched the ferryman gather his bag. The library had grown dimmer, yet the cushions remained warm under them and the blanket held the mild heat of their own body. The ferryman rose slowly, patted the seat once in farewell, and stepped toward the stairs that led back to the river path. Sora stayed where they were, breathing the quiet room and feeling the smooth wood under their palm. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
