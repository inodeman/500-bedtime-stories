In the quiet workshop a soft tick rose from the little shelf clock, and Pilar heard it first, before any other sound, and paused with one hand still on the door latch while the late afternoon light lay warm across the floorboards. The air inside felt gently heated from the day, and tools hung ready on a low peg within easy reach of small hands. Pilar stepped farther in and smelled the faint green tang of tomato vines drifting through the open side window from the pots Papa kept just outside. They looked around for Papa and found him already near the wide front window, wiping the sill clear so that nothing blocked the long view down the path to the corner. Pilar watched the careful motion and understood, without a word yet, that Papa had taken care of the goodbye that needed someone staying at the window until the walker turned that far corner.

Papa set the cloth aside and smiled when he saw Pilar fully inside. “I thought you might come before supper,” he said, his voice steady and low in the warm room. Pilar walked closer and ran one fingertip along the grain of the bench, feeling the tiny raised lines of the wood under their skin. Outside, a cat’s collar bell gave one clear chime as the cat crossed the yard, then fell silent again. Pilar looked from the clean window glass to the cleared sill and to the open stretch of path beyond, and a small gladness rose in them because the arrangement was already finished and kind. Papa had made the place ready so that whoever stood there could watch all the way to the corner without leaning or squinting past clutter.

“You moved the jars and the spare handles,” Pilar said, noticing how the low peg still held the hammers and chisels in neat order while the window stayed free. Papa nodded and rested a palm on the bench. The light through the glass had turned a softer gold, and it touched the tops of their heads like a thin warm scarf. Pilar thought a quick thank-you would feel too thin for something so thoughtful, the sort of care that made leaving feel gentle instead of sudden. They wanted to answer the kindness with something useful of their own, something Papa had not asked for at all. Their eyes moved along the low peg and the tidy tools, searching for a small task that would help.

Near the bench sat a shallow tray of wood scraps and a coil of soft twine that had begun to unwind across the floor. Pilar knelt and gathered the twine, winding it slowly back into a neat loop so it would not catch a shoe later. The floorboards felt cool under their knees after the warmer air higher up. Papa watched without interrupting, and the workshop held only the quiet of two people sharing the same calm space. When the twine sat tidy in the tray, Pilar stood and reached for the soft brush that hung from the end of the low peg. They swept the stray shavings from the bench into a little pile, careful not to raise dust into the golden light.

“I did not ask you to clear that,” Papa said, though his voice held only surprise and quiet pleasure. Pilar kept brushing until the surface shone dull and clean in the slanted light. They could hear, if they listened past the nearer tick of the shelf clock, the farther clock in the house hallway marking the slower minutes, and they chose that distant sound for a moment because it made the room feel even more peaceful. The brush made a faint dry whisper against the wood. Pilar set the brush back on its peg and turned to face Papa fully, ready at last to speak the plain words they had been holding.

“I noticed what you did at the window,” Pilar said. “You made it ready so someone can stay and watch until the corner.” Papa’s eyes softened, and he reached out to rest a hand lightly on Pilar’s shoulder. The touch felt steady and familiar through the thin cloth of their shirt. Outside the side window the tomato leaves shifted once in a small breeze, but inside the air stayed still and kind. Pilar felt the gladness settle deeper, no longer only a quick thought but something solid they could stand on. They had done one useful thing without being asked, and they had said, plainly, that they had seen the care.

Papa glanced toward the path that bent away past the garden fence. “When you walk home in a little while, I will stand right here,” he said. “I will stay at the window until you turn the corner, just as we like.” Pilar nodded, and the promise felt as warm as the remaining light on the floor. They imagined the walk already—the short stretch, the wave, the moment of turning—and knew the window would hold Papa’s shape the whole way. No hurry pressed them yet. They stood together a little longer, letting the workshop hold them both, the tools quiet on the low peg and the bench clear between them.

Oona’s footsteps sounded lightly on the stone outside, and then Oona’s face appeared at the side window with a friendly wave. “I came to walk back with you when you are ready,” Oona called softly through the open pane. Pilar lifted a hand in answer, and the cooler evening air that slipped in brushed their cheeks. Papa smiled at Oona too, then looked once more at the front window he had prepared. Everything needed for the gentle goodbye was already in place. Pilar felt the day folding itself neatly toward rest, the way a well-used cloth folds after work is done.

Pilar took a last slow breath of the workshop’s warm quiet and stepped toward the door with Oona waiting just beyond. Papa moved to the front window as promised, one hand resting on the clean sill. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Pilar walked the path with Oona beside them, and when they reached the corner they both turned to look back; Papa was still there, a steady shape in the gentle light, and they raised their hands once more before the corner hid the workshop from view and the evening settled around them like a calm blanket.
