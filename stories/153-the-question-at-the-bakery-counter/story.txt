"Would anyone like to share a quiet question they have been carrying today?" the storyteller asked, and her voice floated across the cushions like a soft ribbon. Yelena sat in the library story circle with their knees tucked close and felt the gentle heat rising from the floor vent that warmed their toes through thick socks. Auntie June sat right beside Yelena, her presence steady as a favorite blanket, while pale afternoon light poured through the high windows and painted long rectangles on the rug. Cosima sat across the way and offered Yelena a small friendly smile that made the circle feel less huge. Yelena listened to the hush of turning pages somewhere behind them and held the edges of their cushion with careful fingers as the other children settled with soft sighs.

The storyteller opened a wide picture book filled with drawings of a busy bakery counter where bakers in tall hats answered every kind of curious voice. Yelena watched the bright illustrations and remembered that very morning at the real bakery, where bags had rustled and people had spoken over one another in a cheerful rush. Yelena had stood near Auntie June and wanted to ask a special question, but the counter had felt so full of motion that the words stayed tucked away. Now, in the library story circle, the memory returned with the warm glow of the reading lamp beside the storyteller's chair. Auntie June noticed Yelena's thoughtful face and simply stayed near, kind and unhurried, never poking or prodding, while the lamp light made the book pages shine like butter.

When the storyteller held up a prop from a cloth bag, Yelena saw a folded baker's garment, and later, when it was passed gently around, Yelena felt the soft weave of a cotton apron under their fingertips, worn smooth in places and dotted with tiny pale marks that might have been old flour. The texture surprised Yelena with its friendliness, as if the apron itself invited ordinary questions. The storyteller read on about customers who asked for seed rolls and honey twists, and Yelena's own unasked question stirred again. Cosima leaned forward at a picture of glazed buns, and cool air drifted from a cracked window to brush Yelena's cheek. Auntie June caught Yelena's eye and gave the smallest nod that said she understood both waiting and choosing.

After the last page, the storyteller opened a round tin and shared crackers that filled the air with the smell of toasted sesame, rich and nutty and almost like the bakery itself had peeked into the library. Yelena took a cracker and breathed the scent deeply before tasting the delicate crunch. Auntie June broke her own cracker in half and passed a piece so they could nibble together, and the shared smell made Yelena feel braver in a quiet way. Children chewed softly, and the little sounds mixed with the faraway murmur of librarians stamping books. The light in the room had grown honey-colored as the sun lowered outside the glass, and Yelena wiped a sesame crumb from their lap while glancing at the open middle of the circle.

From the courtyard beyond the story room, Yelena heard a gate latch close with a clear metallic click that settled the afternoon into an even deeper calm. That small sound seemed to say everything outside was safely in place and everything inside could move at a gentle pace. The storyteller invited the children to bring any question they liked into the open air of the circle, the way someone might speak at a counter when ready. Cosima asked whether the bakery in the book stayed open after dark, and the storyteller answered with a smile. Yelena felt the old shyness rise like a slow tide, the shyness about the question at a busy bakery counter that still waited inside them. Auntie June rested her hand on the cushion between them, palm open, a silent promise that no performance was required, while blue shadows lengthened on the rug.

Yelena could have pretended not to care. They could have looked at the rug patterns and let the invitation drift past like a leaf on a stream. Instead Yelena tried the smallest brave version, gathering the words the way one gathers buttons into a pocket. They straightened the cushion beneath them with both hands, setting it square the way a person might set a chair square to a table, making a neat little place for themselves in the circle. The temperature near Auntie June felt pleasantly warm, and Yelena drew on that warmth. Their voice would not need to fill the whole library; it only needed to be heard by the kind ears already listening as Yelena lifted their chin just enough to meet the storyteller's patient gaze.

"I have a question I wanted to ask at the bakery counter this morning," Yelena said, and the sentence came out steady enough to travel across the soft rug. Everyone waited with friendly faces. "I wanted to ask if the baker could please save one sesame seed bun for Auntie June for tomorrow, because those are the best ones." Auntie June blinked with happy surprise. "Oh, Yelena, that is such a thoughtful question," Auntie June said, her tone full of ordinary delight and no extra spotlight. The storyteller added, "Bakers love knowing what people hope for." Cosima nodded hard and said, "I hope they save it." Yelena felt a quiet glow settle under their ribs, lighter than any worry, while the reading lamp shone on the closed book.

The circle spent a few more minutes trading gentle comments about favorite breads and favorite books, and the overhead lights dimmed so the reading lamp became the main star. Yelena leaned their shoulder against Auntie June and listened to the low, contented hum of voices winding down. The polished floor under the edge of the rug held a leftover coolness that felt good against Yelena's heel. Someone laughed softly at a joke about a very tall cake, and the laughter sounded like tiny bells. Auntie June smoothed Yelena's hair with a careful hand and whispered that they could visit the bakery again in the morning if Yelena still wished to ask. Yelena nodded, already knowing the words would come more easily the second time in that peaceful library air.

When story circle ended, families rose in a rustle of coats and backpacks, and Yelena stood with Auntie June near the tall window that faced the quiet street. They talked in low voices about sesame buns and about how speaking once in a safe circle made the next speaking feel possible. Outside, the sky softened toward evening blue, and inside the library the last of the honey light clung to the shelves. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Yelena took Auntie June's hand and felt ready for the walk home, for supper, and for the slow drift toward sleep, while the cool evening air at the door brushed their face like a goodnight touch and the memory of the circle stayed warm inside their chest all the way down the steps.
