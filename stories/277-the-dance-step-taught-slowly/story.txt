"Will you teach the dance step one more time, Grandma Nia?" Tamsin asked, their words soft in the hush that filled the library. Warm afternoon light spilled through the round window and rested like a golden blanket on the thick library rug where Tamsin sat. The air felt pleasantly cool against their arms. Grandma Nia nodded and stepped onto the rug with careful feet. Lior lingered just outside the circle of soft wool, watching with quiet eyes. Tamsin patted an empty spot beside them and smiled. The woven fibers pressed gently under Tamsin's palms as they waited. Somewhere a page turned with a dry whisper. Tamsin stayed close to the center so Lior could see every small movement.

Grandma Nia lifted one foot and set it down again, moving no faster than a drifting leaf. "This step belongs to the newest feet," she said, her voice calm as still water. Tamsin watched closely and copied the slow slide and soft stamp. The light from the round window shifted across the rug and warmed the tops of Tamsin's bare toes. Lior took one tiny step nearer yet stayed off the wool. Tamsin noticed and kept their own motions open and easy so there would be room. The library smelled faintly of old paper and wood. Tamsin breathed in and out, matching the unhurried pace. Grandma Nia only watched and let the children find their way.

Tamsin turned toward Lior and spoke again. "We can make the circle bigger so everyone fits," Tamsin said. They scooted backward a little, sliding across the rug until a clear space opened right beside them. The temperature near the floor felt cooler than the sunlit air higher up. Lior's shoulders loosened. Tamsin reached out a hand, and when their fingers met, Tamsin felt the dry chalky hand of their friend, leftover powder from drawings made earlier on the big sidewalk board. The touch was dusty and familiar. Lior stepped fully onto the rug at last. Grandma Nia smiled but said nothing more, trusting the children to arrange the space themselves.

Tamsin began the dance step once more, sliding one foot and then the other at the exact speed of the newest feet. "Follow me as slowly as you need," Tamsin whispered. Lior copied the motion, toes brushing the soft loops of wool. A faint sweet smell of honey drifted from the small tin of tea cakes Grandma Nia had set on a nearby low shelf. The scent mixed gently with the quiet room. Light continued to pour through the round window and painted pale circles on the rug. Tamsin kept the rhythm steady and kind so no one had to rush. Lior's face brightened with each careful stamp.

Outside the tall library walls a gate latch clicked once and then fell silent again. Tamsin heard the small metal sound yet stayed focused on the shared step. They guided Lior's elbows with light fingertips and showed how the heels could settle softly. The rug felt springy under every landing. Grandma Nia rocked gently on her heels at the edge, still letting Tamsin and Lior decide the shape of the practice. Tamsin spoke once more. "We do not need any prizes or turns announced," Tamsin said. "We just keep the step going together." Lior nodded and matched the pace exactly.

They practiced the slide and the quiet stamp again and again until the motion felt smooth as poured cream. Tamsin felt the warm light on their back and the cooler shadow where the window's edge fell. Lior began to hum a tiny wordless tune that fit the slow beat. Tamsin joined in under their breath. The library rug held them safely, its texture thick and kind beneath their moving feet. Grandma Nia folded her hands and watched with soft eyes. No one hurried. The dance step stayed patient for every newest foot that wanted to learn it.

Tamsin changed the seating once more by inviting Lior to stand right in the brightest pool of window light. "Here the floor feels nicest," Tamsin said. Lior stepped into the glow and smiled. The air around them held a gentle warmth from the sun through glass. They repeated the step side by side, shoulders almost touching. Tamsin could sense the easy friendship growing in the shared motion without needing to name it. Grandma Nia remained quiet, pleased that the children had solved the circle themselves. The whole room seemed to breathe with them.

After many slow repetitions Tamsin suggested they pause and simply stand together on the rug. A single chime sounded from the old library clock, and they waited through that one clear note before moving again. The sound faded into the peaceful air. Lior's breathing matched Tamsin's. The round window light had grown softer and more amber. Tamsin felt grateful for the open space they had made and for the unhurried teaching of the dance step. Grandma Nia finally stepped closer and rested a kind hand on each child's shoulder.

Later, when the practice ended, Tamsin and Lior sat cross-legged once more on the library rug under the round window while Grandma Nia packed the honey cakes away. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Its mild light mixed with the last daylight and warmed the quiet faces of the children. Tamsin leaned against Lior for a moment, still feeling the memory of the chalky hand and the gentle pace they had chosen together. The air stayed cool and calm. Outside, evening settled without hurry. Tamsin closed their eyes and pictured the slow slide and soft stamp one last time before the library grew ready for sleep.
