The poster project rested in the middle of the stone circle, its wide sheet of white paper catching the mild golden light of late afternoon so that every painted cloud seemed to glow from within. Two brushes lay crossed near the bottom edge, their soft bristles still dark with leftover blue and green, and a third friend waited just outside the fun as if the paper itself had left a quiet empty space. Milo crouched low enough to trace one careful finger along a paper edge that felt thin and slightly rough under the pad of their fingertip, noticing how the light made the paint shine like still water. The low swing hung nearby with its wooden seat cool and smooth, and the whole schoolyard smelled faintly of damp earth after the morning sprinkle. Cousin Pio stood a little behind Milo, hands in pockets, watching without hurry.

Milo glanced up and saw Yara standing just beyond the ring of stones, her shoes scuffing softly against the packed dirt while she held her own hands together. The air carried a gentle warmth that made Milo’s cheeks feel pleasant, and they could hear the distant tin clock ticking from the open classroom window in slow even beats. “Pio, the poster needs more stars along the top,” Milo said in a voice that stayed calm and clear. Cousin Pio only nodded once and stepped back two paces so the children could decide. Milo patted the stone beside them and kept their eyes on the paper rather than calling out any prize.

Yara took one small step closer when Milo shifted the two brushes apart and left a clear open place right in the middle of the circle. The stones under Milo’s knees felt solid and a little cool through their trousers, and a soft breeze brought the clean smell of wet wool from the sweater someone had left draped over the swing chain. “You can sit right here with us, Yara,” Milo said, moving their own knees sideways so the space felt natural and ready. Yara sat down without rush, her shoulder almost touching Milo’s, and the three of them looked at the half-finished poster together. Cousin Pio stayed quiet near the low swing, letting the children find their own pace.

Milo handed one brush to Yara and kept the other, then showed how the third friend could hold the paper steady by pressing gently at the corners. The paint on the bristles smelled fresh and a little sweet, and the sunlight warmed the backs of their necks in a steady glow. “We can take turns with the blue first,” Milo told them both, speaking softly so the words fit the quiet yard. Yara dipped the brush and made a careful arc that looked like a bird’s wing, while Milo added tiny dots beside it. The low swing swayed once with a gentle creak that sounded almost like a hello.

They worked without hurry, Milo changing the seating by sliding a flat stone under Yara’s elbow so her arm stayed comfortable the whole time. The texture of the stone was dry and slightly dusty, and Milo’s own hands felt the same dust when they brushed it away. “Pio, does the sky need more green near the bottom?” Milo asked, glancing over their shoulder. Cousin Pio smiled and shrugged, still leaving the choice to them. Yara spoke next in a bright quiet voice. “I think a little green would look like soft grass,” she said, and Milo nodded right away so the idea belonged to all three.

Milo slowed the pace on purpose, dipping the brush only after Yara had finished each stroke, so no one rushed and every color had room to dry a little in the open air. The afternoon light turned the painted stars silver at the edges, and Milo could feel the mild heat rising from the stones into their palms. They wiped a tiny drip with the side of a finger and laughed under their breath when the drop made a funny shape. Yara laughed too, a small sound that matched the soft tick of the distant tin clock still marking time from the window. Cousin Pio rocked the low swing once with one hand, making it move like a slow pendulum while the children painted.

The circle of stones held them all now, Milo in the middle, Yara on one side, and the poster bright between their knees. Milo changed the simple rule they had started with by saying the brushes could be shared whenever someone wanted a new color, and Yara’s place felt as real as the paper itself. The air cooled just enough that Milo pulled the wet-wool sweater closer around their shoulders for a moment before setting it back. “This star looks like it could fly,” Milo said, pointing with the clean end of a brush. Yara answered, “Then we should give it two more wings,” and together they added the lines while the swing ropes whispered against each other.

When the poster was nearly full of color, Milo stood and stretched their arms, feeling the pleasant looseness in their shoulders after sitting so long. They invited Yara to walk with them once around the stone circle, shoes making soft scuffs on the ground, while Cousin Pio kept a gentle hand on the swing so it would not sway too far. The light had grown softer and more golden, wrapping everything in a calm haze that smelled of grass and drying paint. Milo breathed in slowly and out again, matching the quiet rhythm of the yard itself. They all three carried the poster carefully toward the school wall where a wide sill waited.

Inside the doorway the air felt cooler and still, and Milo set the finished poster against the wall so the colors could be seen from the yard. Yara stood close on one side and Cousin Pio on the other, all of them looking at the bright shapes they had made together. Milo rested a hand on the sill and felt its smooth painted wood under their fingers while the last of the daylight slipped across the floor. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
