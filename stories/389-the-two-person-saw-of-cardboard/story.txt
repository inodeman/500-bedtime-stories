"Hold your end steady, Yuri, and we will count the baskets as we pretend to saw," said Farah, resting their fingers on the cardboard two-person saw that Uncle Idris had shaped that morning so the children could help beside the quiet harbor dock where baskets were passed up.

Farah felt the faint grit of dried salt on the planks under their shoes while the cardboard saw made a soft shushing scrape each time they and Yuri drew it carefully back and forth, never hurrying, because the counting of the woven baskets still had to match the real ones climbing from boat to dock. The air carried a mild cinnamon scent from the spice sacks stacked near the railing, and Farah breathed it in while watching the rope handles rise one after another. Uncle Idris stood a few steps away with his sleeves rolled, offering only a calm number when asked and never grabbing the saw himself. "Basket seven is on its way," he said in his even voice, and Farah repeated the number aloud so Yuri could hear it clearly across the cardboard blade. The light slanted golden across the water and warmed Farah's cheeks as they kept the rhythm slow and even.

Farah noticed that if either of them pulled too fast the pretend saw would bend and the numbers would tumble out of order, so they and Yuri decided right then to split the work into two slow jobs that fitted together like gentle waves. Farah would watch the baskets coming up and call the next number only when the cardboard teeth lined up just so, while Yuri would keep the far end steady and whisper the same number back. The wooden dock felt cool beneath Farah's palms whenever they knelt to check a basket mark, and the small breeze carried the distant click of a gate latch from the shed at the far end of the pier. "We go slower than the gulls," Farah told Yuri with a quiet smile, and Yuri nodded, matching the next pull to Farah's pace. Uncle Idris only named the knot they might need later, saying "a simple loop will do," and left the children to their careful counting.

Farah kept one hand light on the cardboard handle and felt how the pretend saw flexed just enough to remind them both not to rush, while the next basket rose with a soft thump of straw against the rail. The temperature of the afternoon air stayed pleasantly warm against Farah's neck as they and Yuri drew the saw again, counting "eight" together in voices barely louder than the lapping water. Farah glanced at Uncle Idris, who simply held up eight fingers and then returned to steadying the rope, and Farah felt glad he trusted them to finish the match themselves. "Your turn to say nine when it comes," Farah whispered to Yuri, and Yuri answered, "I will wait until the saw sits straight." The cardboard edges had been rubbed smooth enough that they never scratched, and Farah liked the way the whole dock seemed to listen to their unhurried work.

Farah and Yuri settled into the two slow jobs so thoroughly that the baskets kept arriving in perfect step with their pretend cuts, each number spoken once by Farah and echoed once by Yuri before the cardboard blade moved again. A cool shadow from the boat mast slid across Farah's bare arm and made the sunlight feel even kinder when it returned. Uncle Idris murmured the name of another knot, "the bowline for later," but he never hurried them or took the saw. Farah smelled the leftover cinnamon still drifting from the nearby sacks and smiled because it made the dock feel almost like a kitchen at home. "Ten and resting," Farah said softly when the tenth basket settled, and both children paused with the saw balanced between them so the count could stay true.

Farah felt the walnut-smooth spoon that Uncle Idris had left in the picnic basket earlier when they reached for a drink of water, the handle cool and polished under their fingers before they set it down again and returned to the cardboard saw. The light had grown softer and more peach-colored across the harbor, painting gentle stripes on the water that Farah watched while counting the next basket. Yuri kept the far end level, and together they pulled only as fast as a sleepy turtle might walk. "Eleven matches," Yuri said, and Farah answered, "Eleven stays," keeping every word unhurried. Uncle Idris offered nothing more than a quiet nod and the soft reminder of the loop knot's name if they needed it for the last ropes, and Farah felt the teamwork of the three of them settling around the dock like a warm blanket.

Farah and Yuri continued their split jobs until the final baskets rose, each one counted twice and checked against the cardboard saw's slow journey back and forth so nothing could slip out of place. The planks still held a trace of daytime heat under Farah's knees when they knelt to straighten a handle, and the sound of the water against the pilings stayed low and friendly. "Last one, twelve," Farah called, waiting for Yuri's echo before the pretend saw finished its final gentle stroke. Uncle Idris spoke the number once more only to confirm, then named the simple knot again so they could tie the last rope themselves if they wished. Farah hummed four soft notes and then left a rest of silence, listening to the quiet dock breathe around them while the cardboard saw rested across their laps.

Farah helped Yuri lean the cardboard saw against the railing where it would stay dry, and both children watched Uncle Idris lift the final basket into place with the same calm care they had shown all afternoon. The evening air cooled against Farah's wrists as they walked the short path from the dock toward the little shed that held their jackets. Inside, the wooden floor felt smooth under their socks once shoes were off, and Farah sat for a moment on the low bench while Yuri folded the spare rope. Uncle Idris thanked them by name for keeping every number true, and Farah answered that the slow jobs had made the counting easy. Outside the small window the sky turned the color of milk tea, and Farah felt ready for home.

Farah carried the memory of the matched baskets and the steady cardboard saw all the way back to the quiet house, where supper smelled gentle and the chairs waited. After teeth were brushed and pajamas felt soft against Farah's skin, they climbed into bed with Yuri's borrowed storybook nearby and Uncle Idris humming somewhere down the hall. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Farah closed their eyes to the familiar hush of the room and let the day's slow pulls and careful numbers drift into sleep like boats settling for the night.
