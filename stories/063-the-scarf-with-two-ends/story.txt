The warm smell of lemon soap rose from Miss Dalca’s clean hands as Bodhi sat on the ferry bench and worked their fingers already busy along the soft edge of the folded blanket. The wood under Bodhi felt cool through their trousers, and the blanket’s wool gave off a gentle, sleepy heat that made Bodhi’s palms feel safe. Across the quiet deck the water shone in long silver streaks under the late light, and Bodhi listened to the soft slap of the ferry against small waves while their hands kept smoothing and folding. Miss Dalca sat close enough that Bodhi could feel the calm of her coat sleeve. “The air is kind tonight,” Miss Dalca said in a low voice, and Bodhi nodded without looking up, still busy with the blanket corner. A thin breeze brushed Bodhi’s cheek like a feather, cool but friendly, and the lemon soap smell stayed near like a quiet promise.

Bodhi noticed the long scarf resting half under the blanket, its two ends trailing down toward the floorboards and its middle looped in a loose neck that looked thinner and colder than the rest. The scarf’s yarn felt slightly rough yet kind under Bodhi’s exploring fingertips, and the colder neck seemed to wait for someone to notice it. Nim sat on the other side of the bench, knees tucked, watching the scarf with quiet interest. “It looks longer than it is warm,” Nim said softly, and Bodhi turned the cooler neck between both hands to feel the difference. The ferry lights glowed pale gold along the rail, and Bodhi could see dust motes drifting in that light like tiny slow stars. Bodhi’s shoes pressed the warm deck boards, and the whole bench felt steady under them.

Bodhi thought about how the scarf had two ends that could cover two people, yet only one neck, and how it would not feel fair if only one person received the warm middle first while someone else waited with colder hands. The idea settled gently inside Bodhi like a small stone dropping into still water. “We could make smaller even shares,” Bodhi said, looking from the scarf to Nim and then to Miss Dalca. Miss Dalca’s eyes were calm and bright in the deck light, and she simply waited without rushing anyone. The folded blanket under Bodhi smelled faintly of sun and river air, warm against their legs. Somewhere beyond the rail the wind in the fig leaves made a dry, whispering hush that sounded like pages turning very slowly.

Bodhi lifted the scarf carefully and began to arrange both ends so that the cooler neck sat in the middle like a shared bridge. The yarn slipped through Bodhi’s fingers with a quiet friction, and Bodhi felt the difference between the thicker ends and the thinner neck as clearly as the difference between shade and sunlight. Nim leaned closer, and Bodhi could feel the small warmth of Nim’s shoulder almost touching theirs. “If we each take one end and leave the neck between us, then nobody waits,” Bodhi said, and their voice stayed soft so it would not hurry the moment. Miss Dalca rested one hand on the blanket edge and left the choice alone, offering no speech at all. The ferry hummed low under the bench, a steady vibration Bodhi felt through the soles of their feet, and the evening air cooled the tips of Bodhi’s ears in a pleasant way.

Bodhi looped one scarf end around Nim’s shoulders first, then brought the other end across their own front so the colder neck rested evenly between them like a polite guest. The yarn lay light and friendly against Bodhi’s collarbone, and Bodhi could feel Nim’s quiet breath near the shared middle. Miss Dalca watched with a small smile that stayed in her eyes more than on her mouth. “It sits just right,” Nim whispered, and Bodhi answered, “It does,” while smoothing the cooler neck so it would not pull toward only one side. The folded blanket under them stayed thick and kind, and Bodhi pressed a palm into its wool to feel the springy give. Across the water a distant buoy light blinked once, soft as a firefly, and the deck smelled clean and open.

Bodhi noticed a floured towel resting near Miss Dalca’s bag, left from an earlier snack of soft rolls, and the dry dusty texture of the cloth brushed Bodhi’s wrist when they reached to straighten the blanket corner. The flour felt powdery and cool, almost like fine sand that had forgotten the beach, and Bodhi brushed it away with slow care so nothing would mark the scarf. The ferry bench creaked gently when Bodhi shifted, a wooden sound that felt like a quiet friend clearing its throat. Nim’s end of the scarf rose and fell with each calm breath, and Bodhi matched that rhythm without trying. Miss Dalca’s coat gave off a faint trace of the lemon soap again whenever she moved her arm, and Bodhi felt the whole scene settle into a peaceful shape.

Bodhi and Nim sat side by side under the shared scarf while the ferry carried them slowly forward, and Bodhi kept one hand lightly on the colder neck to make sure it stayed in the middle. The night air grew cooler on Bodhi’s uncovered cheek, yet the scarf ends held a gentle heat that traveled from yarn into skin. “We can keep it this way until we reach the dock,” Bodhi said, and Nim nodded with a sleepy smile that made their eyes half-close. Miss Dalca folded her hands in her lap and let the choice stand exactly as Bodhi had made it, without adding any words that might tip the balance. The blanket beneath them stayed folded and warm, its weight steady against Bodhi’s legs like a soft anchor. Light from the ferry windows painted pale rectangles on the deck boards, and Bodhi watched those shapes rock gently with the motion of the water.

Bodhi’s eyelids felt heavier as the minutes passed, and they rested their head against the back of the bench while still holding the scarf neck even between them and Nim. The wool of the blanket smelled faintly sweet from the day’s sun, and Bodhi breathed that smell in slow counts. Miss Dalca reached over once to tuck a loose blanket corner nearer to Bodhi’s knee, her movement quiet as a leaf settling. “Everything feels fair now,” Nim murmured, almost asleep, and Bodhi answered only with a soft hum that vibrated in their own chest. The ferry’s engine kept its low lullaby under the bench, a vibration Bodhi felt in their spine like a distant cat purring. Cool air from the water brushed Bodhi’s forehead, clean and kind, and the shared scarf stayed balanced without any tug toward only one child.

When the ferry lights dimmed a little for the last quiet stretch of the crossing, Bodhi’s hands finally grew still on the scarf and the blanket, and they felt the even warmth travel through both ends into their shoulders and Nim’s. Miss Dalca sat nearby like a gentle lamp that needed no extra glow, and the choice Bodhi had made remained exactly as it was. Bodhi’s breathing slowed to match the soft rock of the boat, and the cooler neck of the scarf no longer felt cold at all because two friends shared it. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
