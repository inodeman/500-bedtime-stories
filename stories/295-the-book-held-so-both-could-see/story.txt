"Come sit closer, Jorie, the pictures look better from here," Ines said softly as the warm lamp light pooled across the bedroom rug of cushions, puppets, and paper. Papa settled on the edge of the circle with a quiet smile and let the children arrange themselves. The air carried a faint sweet smell of beeswax from the little candle on the shelf. Ines patted a blue cushion beside them and waited while Jorie stepped nearer, socks whispering on the floorboards. Outside the open window the wind in the fig leaves made a gentle rushing sound that mixed with the hush of turning pages. Ines held the book in the middle so both pairs of eyes could reach the same bright drawings without stretching or leaning too far. Puppets with yarn hair rested against a pile of soft paper boats, and the whole rug felt like a small island of quiet fun. Ines kept their voice low and kind so the evening stayed peaceful. Papa watched without steering, hands folded, simply glad to be near.

Ines noticed that Jorie still hovered at the outer edge of the cushions, close enough to hear every word yet not quite inside the cozy ring. The book held in the middle glowed under the lamp, its thick paper warm to the touch. Ines shifted two cushions sideways with careful hands, making a wider nest that left a clear open space right beside them. They did not call it a special seat or make a big announcement; they simply moved the soft fabric and patted it once. Jorie slid into the new spot, knees brushing the paper boats, and the cool evening air drifted across their cheeks. Ines turned a page slowly so the colored fish on the paper seemed to swim under the light. Papa stayed quiet on his cushion, letting the children find their own rhythm. The room felt pleasantly warm, not stuffy, and the lamp made little golden circles on the walls.

Jorie reached toward a puppet fox whose fuzzy ears caught the light, then pulled the hand back as if unsure. Ines saw the hesitation and slowed the whole pace of the story time. They set the book held in the middle down for a moment on a flat cushion and invited Jorie to choose which puppet might sit on the corner of the page. "You can put the fox right here if you want," Ines whispered, and Jorie did, settling the soft toy so its nose pointed at the next picture. The texture of a leaf's soft underside came from a real fig leaf Papa had brought in earlier and left on the windowsill; Ines brushed it once with a fingertip while thinking, then left it alone. The lamp light stayed steady and kind. Outside, the evening cooled a little, but inside the rug stayed snug. Papa nodded once, still letting the children solve the small puzzle of space and turns.

Ines began reading again, holding the book so the middle spine rested evenly between their lap and Jorie’s. The pages smelled faintly of clean paper under the stronger beeswax note still floating in the room. Jorie’s shoulder touched Ines’s shoulder lightly, and neither pulled away. Puppets leaned together like quiet friends, and scraps of colored paper curled at the edges in the warm air. Ines pointed to a drawing of a boat and asked, "Shall we sail this one next?" Jorie answered, "Yes, let’s go slowly," and Ines smiled at the shared plan. Papa’s breathing was calm and even, a soft sound beneath the story words. The cushions gave a little under their weight, soft and welcoming. Light from the lamp painted gentle shadows that moved only when someone turned a page.

They took turns naming the colors they saw: sea blue, shell pink, leaf green. Ines made sure Jorie spoke first each time, simply by waiting with an open face instead of jumping in. The book held in the middle never tipped toward one child more than the other. A paper crane near Ines’s knee caught a puff of cooler air from the window and rocked once. Ines felt the pleasant weight of the evening settling around them like a blanket. Jorie’s fingers traced the edge of a cushion, finding the seam, while Ines kept the pages steady. Papa remained a quiet lighthouse at the edge, ready if needed yet content to watch friendship unfold without any fuss. The room stayed the perfect temperature for sitting still and listening.

Ines decided the rules of their little reading game could stretch. Instead of racing through every page, they agreed without many words to linger on the ones Jorie liked best. "We can stay on this picture as long as we want," Ines said, and Jorie replied, "I like the tall trees most." The wind in the fig leaves brushed the screen again, a soft outdoor hush that made the indoor quiet feel even safer. Ines held the book firmly in the middle so the tall trees stayed centered for both. Puppets guarded the corners of the rug like sleepy sentries. Paper scraps formed tiny paths between the cushions. Papa’s eyes crinkled with quiet happiness. The lamp’s glow felt warmer as true dusk gathered outside.

Jorie picked up a small paper boat and set it carefully on the open page as if it belonged in the drawn river. Ines left it there and kept reading around it, never brushing it aside. The two children leaned in together, shoulders aligned, and the book held in the middle became a shared bridge. Ines felt the smooth cover under their palms and the softer paper of the inside sheets. A faint cool draft touched their ankles, pleasant after the earlier warmth. They spoke in turns about what the boat might see, inventing gentle journeys that never hurried. Papa stretched his legs once, then settled again, still leaving the solving to them. Every cushion seemed to hold a little pocket of calm.

Time moved slowly on the rug of cushions, puppets, and paper. Ines turned only when both were ready, watching Jorie’s face for the small nod that meant go ahead. The beeswax scent had faded to a bare whisper, replaced by the clean smell of cotton cushions. Light lay in soft bars across the floor. Ines and Jorie rested the book between them so neither wrist grew tired. "Papa, look how the boat sits on the river," Ines said, and Papa answered, "I see it floating nicely." Jorie added, "We put it there together," and the three of them shared a quiet laugh that did not break the peace. The leaf on the sill stayed still. Everything on the rug felt exactly right, no one left outside the circle anymore.

Ines closed the book at last with a soft sound and left it resting in the middle of the cushions where both could still see the cover. Jorie curled a little closer, content. Papa rose only to dim the brighter lamp, leaving a gentler light. The children straightened one puppet’s yarn hair and stacked two paper boats side by side. Cool night air drifted in, and the cushions kept their legs warm. Ines felt glad all the way through without needing to say why. They sat a minute longer in the easy silence, listening to ordinary house sounds. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
