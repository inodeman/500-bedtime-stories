"Anil, the rain is ticking softly on our porch roof and we have all the time we need," Nana Ruth said as she patted the empty seat beside her.

Anil settled close to Nana Ruth on the wide porch while the rain ticked against the overhang and nobody was late for supper or bed or any other thing. Cool air drifted across their bare arms and made the little hairs stand up in a pleasant way. Nana Ruth held a creased sheet of paper that smelled faintly of the garden soil still clinging to its edges. Anil felt a bigger piece of news rising in their chest, news about Nils and the shiny stone they had found together near the fence, yet the quiet weight of the paper made Anil keep the words inside for now. They watched the slow drip of water from the porch edge and listened to the steady ticking that sounded like a clock made of leaves. Nana Ruth smoothed the paper with her palm and began to speak in her low evening voice that matched the rain.

Nana Ruth told Anil that the paper held instructions for a fern that did not like being moved from its old shady corner to a brighter pot near the steps. She described how the fern had sulked at first, its fronds drooping as if the change of light felt too sudden and sharp. Anil pictured the plant standing stubbornly with its roots still dreaming of the darker soil. The porch boards held a gentle warmth left over from the afternoon sun, and Anil rested their hands on that warmth while Nana Ruth kept reading the careful notes aloud. Every few words Nana Ruth paused so the rain could fill the quiet spaces. Anil wanted to jump in with the story of Nils and the stone, yet they stayed still and let the fern instructions unfold one slow sentence after another.

The instructions explained that the fern needed water poured only at the base and never over the curly new leaves, and that it preferred the same side of the pot always facing the morning light. Nana Ruth’s finger traced each line so Anil could follow. A soft breeze carried the clean smell of pear skins from the kitchen bowl just inside the door, and Anil breathed it in while they listened. The fern, Nana Ruth said, had slowly uncurled again once the notes were followed exactly, as if the plant understood it was being treated with patience. Anil kept their own bigger news folded away and simply nodded, feeling the cool rain-washed air slip between the porch posts. Nana Ruth’s voice stayed even and kind, never hurrying past any detail about soil or shade or the right day to turn the pot a quarter turn.

Anil watched a single drop slide down a porch post and listened as Nana Ruth reached the part about speaking gently near the fern so its leaves would not startle. The light from the open doorway fell in a pale rectangle across Anil’s knees and made the paper glow a little. Nana Ruth said the fern had once been moved too quickly by someone who meant well but forgot the instructions, and that was why the notes had been written down so carefully afterward. Anil’s toes found the corner of the small porch rug and straightened it with a quiet push so the edge lay flat again. They still held the bigger news inside like a warm secret, yet the ticking rain and Nana Ruth’s calm reading made waiting feel easy rather than hard. The evening stayed peaceful and nobody called them inside.

Nana Ruth continued until she reached the final line of the instructions, which simply said to check the soil with one fingertip each morning and to trust the fern’s own slow pace. She read that last sentence twice so Anil would hear every word clearly. Wind moved through the fig leaves beyond the porch rail and made a dry whispering sound that mixed with the rain ticks. Anil sat very still, hands folded, and let the whole story of the fern settle inside them from the first word to the last. Only when Nana Ruth folded the paper again and looked over with a small smile did Anil know it was finally their turn. The bigger news still waited, but first came the question that felt real and needed asking.

“Nana Ruth, did the fern ever seem glad after it settled into the new pot?” Anil asked, their voice soft so it would not rush past the quiet of the porch.

Nana Ruth nodded and said the fern had grown two bright new fronds within a week and that those fronds had caught the morning light like green feathers. Anil felt the answer land gently and then, only then, reached into their pocket and drew out a warm coin they had found earlier near the garden path while walking with Nils. The metal felt smooth and still held the heat of Anil’s hand. They turned the coin once between their fingers and shared the small thing they had chosen instead of the bigger news: that the coin had been resting in a patch of clover and that Nils had said it looked like a tiny moon. Nana Ruth listened with the same care she had given the fern instructions, and Anil felt the porch air grow even softer around them both.

The rain kept ticking in its steady rhythm while Anil leaned against Nana Ruth’s side and watched the last pale light fade from the yard. The warm coin rested in their palm like a quiet thank-you for waiting. Nana Ruth slipped the folded instructions into her pocket for safekeeping and rested one hand lightly on Anil’s shoulder. No one hurried them toward bed or chores. The fig leaves had grown still again, and the pear-skin smell had faded into the broader scent of wet wood and evening. Anil breathed slowly and felt the calm stretch of the porch hold them both. The lantern by the window glowed softly.
