Soft evening air drifted through the open window of the ceramic studio and brushed the long wooden table where bowls rested under damp cloths. The light from outside was mild and gold, and it made the wet slip shine like quiet milk. Sable walked in beside Nana Ruth and laid one careful hand on the edge of the table, feeling how the wood held a faint coolness from the clay. A thank-you note sat in the middle under a thin scrap of paper, its ink still wet and dark. Sable leaned close and smelled the gentle scent of chamomile rising from the mug Nana Ruth had set nearby. “I want to peek just once,” Sable whispered, eyes on the covered note.

Nana Ruth smiled and kept her voice low so the clay would not be disturbed. “The handles still need time to set, and so does the ink,” she said. Sable nodded but stayed near the note, listening to a tin clock ticking steadily on the shelf above the drying rack. The sound was soft and regular, like a slow heartbeat for the whole room. Sable’s fingers hovered, then pulled back. The air felt warm near the kiln vent and cooler by the window, and Sable noticed both temperatures at once. They took a small step away from the note and looked instead at the rows of cups waiting for their future handles.

Hester, who had come to help glaze later, sat on a stool and watched with quiet interest. “We can wait together,” Hester said. Sable answered, “I will leave it covered.” The three of them stood for a moment in the calm light, and Sable felt the smooth side of a biscuit tin that held spare sponges. The tin lid was cool and slightly ridged under Sable’s palm. Nana Ruth moved a cloth over a tall mug so the slip would not dry too fast. Sable copied the gentle motion on a smaller pot, pressing the cloth lightly without lifting it. The studio smelled of damp earth and warm tea, and everything seemed to settle into the same unhurried rhythm.

Sable wanted very much to check the thank-you note again, yet they remembered the wet ink and the soft paper cover. Instead of lifting the scrap, Sable turned toward a basket of clean laundry Nana Ruth had brought in earlier. The socks inside were still warm from the dryer, and Sable lifted two of them and held them to their cheek. The fabric felt soft and slightly fuzzy. Nana Ruth nodded approval without speaking at first. Sable matched the pair carefully, heel to heel, then found another pair and did the same. The quiet task filled the waiting minutes while the ink dried in its own time and the clay handles grew firm under their cloths.

“These two are the same blue,” Sable said, holding up the socks so Hester could see. Hester grinned and replied, “They look like twin birds.” Nana Ruth poured a little more warm water into her mug, and steam curled upward in the golden light. Sable kept matching socks, placing each finished pair in a neat stack on the bench. The table remained still. No one touched the thank-you note. Sable listened again to the steady room and felt proud of the small pile growing beside them. The coolness of the ceramic table edge under one hand and the warmth of the socks in the other made a pleasant balance.

After the socks were done, Sable walked once around the table without rushing. They looked at the covered note from a short distance and saw that the scrap of paper still protected it. Nana Ruth wiped a drip of slip from the rim of a bowl with a sponge that squeaked very softly. Sable copied the careful wipe on another piece and felt the sponge give under their fingers. Hester stacked empty glaze cups so they would be ready later. The light outside slipped lower and turned the window glass the color of honey. Sable’s shoulders dropped into an easy rest. The note would be ready when it was ready, and the clay would be ready too.

Nana Ruth invited Sable to sit on the low stool beside her. “Tell me what you wrote,” she said gently. Sable answered, “I wrote thank you for the yellow cup you made last week.” The words felt good to speak aloud while the ink finished drying. Hester listened and smiled. Sable described the cup’s wide handle and the little flower painted near the foot. While they talked, the tin clock kept its quiet count and the covered note rested untouched. Sable rubbed one thumb along the grain of the wooden stool and noticed how smooth it had become from many years of use. The studio felt peaceful, full of unfinished things that were finishing themselves.

When enough time had passed, Nana Ruth lifted the scrap of paper with two careful fingers. The ink on the thank-you note was dry and clear. Sable leaned in and read the words again, happy that every letter stayed sharp. “It is ready now,” Sable said. Nana Ruth folded the note once and handed it to Sable to keep safe until morning. Hester clapped once, softly, so the clay would not jump. Sable held the note and felt the paper’s dry surface, no longer sticky. The handles on the table had set as well; Nana Ruth tested one with a light touch and nodded. Everything had waited and then become complete without any hurry.

Sable placed the finished note on the windowsill beside a small plant and matched one last sock that had hidden under the basket rim. The evening light faded to a deeper gold, and the ceramic studio grew quieter still. Nana Ruth turned down the lamp over the table. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Sable stood close to Nana Ruth and Hester, breathing the calm air, ready for bed whenever the walk home began.
