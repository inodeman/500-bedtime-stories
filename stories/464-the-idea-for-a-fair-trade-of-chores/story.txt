The soft tick of a tin clock drifted from the kitchen doorway onto the porch, and Farah noticed it right away while the rain ticked gently on the roof boards above. Farah sat close beside Nana Ruth on the wide wooden bench, feeling the cool damp air brush their bare arms and watching the soft yellow light from the house windows stretch across the floorboards. Nobody was late for anything, and the evening felt slow and safe. A warm heap of clean laundry rested in a basket near their feet, still holding the heat from the dryer. Farah rested one hand on the smooth railing and listened to the steady rain while the clock kept its quiet beat inside.

Nana Ruth poured tea from a small pot and the steam carried the smell of chamomile across the porch, wrapping Farah in its mild sweet scent. Farah breathed it in and felt their shoulders settle lower against the bench back. Nana Ruth smiled and set the pot down with care. Farah wanted to jump in with bigger news about the bright blue feather they had found near the garden gate that afternoon, yet they held the words still and waited. The rain kept ticking, and the porch light made tiny bright dots on every wet leaf beyond the railing. Farah pressed their toes against the warm wood and stayed quiet.

“Farah, I have been thinking of an idea for trading chores that had three parts,” Nana Ruth said in her calm low voice. Farah turned toward her and felt the cool evening temperature settle deeper into their sweater sleeves. Nana Ruth held up one finger. “The first part is that whoever waters the herbs also folds the tea towels afterward.” Farah nodded slowly and watched a drop of rain slide down a post. The air stayed soft and the light from the kitchen stayed steady and gold. Farah kept their bigger news tucked away like a smooth pebble in a pocket.

Nana Ruth lifted a second finger and the porch boards felt solid and cool under Farah’s shoes. “The second part is that the person who sweeps the steps also sets out the supper plates.” Farah heard the rain tick a little faster for a moment and then settle again. They wanted to speak about the feather and how Juno had said it looked like a scrap of sky, yet Farah stayed still and listened hard. Nana Ruth’s voice moved gently, and Farah watched the way her hands rested open on her knees. The warm laundry heap nearby sent up a faint clean scent that mixed with the damp air.

“The third part,” Nana Ruth continued, “is that whoever feeds the little birds also waters the porch ferns the next morning.” Farah felt the smooth cool texture of a tin lid as they absently touched the tea tin resting on the small table between them. The metal felt round and quiet under their fingertips. Farah kept listening all the way through every word, even though the bigger news pressed at the front of their mind like a bright balloon. Rain ticked on the roof in a soft even pattern, and the porch stayed peaceful with no one rushing anywhere.

Farah waited until Nana Ruth’s last sentence faded into the sound of the rain. Then Farah asked one real question in a careful voice. “Nana Ruth, if both of us want to water the ferns on the same morning, how do we choose?” Nana Ruth’s eyes crinkled kindly in the porch light. Farah felt the cool air move across their face and waited without hurry. The tin clock still ticked faintly from inside, though Farah no longer needed to notice it first. Everything on the porch felt steady and kind.

“We can take turns by the days of the week, Farah,” Nana Ruth answered. “Monday for you and Tuesday for me, and so on.” Farah smiled and let the answer settle. Only then did Farah share something small of their own. “I found a blue feather by the gate today,” Farah said. “It felt light when I held it.” Nana Ruth nodded as if the feather mattered just as much as the chore idea. Farah reached into the warm laundry heap and matched two soft socks, pairing them carefully while the rain continued its quiet ticking.

The matched socks rested in Farah’s lap like two small clouds, and the porch air stayed pleasantly cool against their wrists. Farah leaned a little closer to Nana Ruth and listened to the rain once more. Nana Ruth poured a little more tea and the chamomile scent had already drifted away into the evening, leaving only the clean damp smell of the wet boards. Farah felt glad they had waited. The light from the house windows painted long gentle shapes across the floor, and nobody needed to go anywhere yet.

Farah and Nana Ruth sat together while the rain ticked on and the matched socks stayed warm in Farah’s hands. The evening grew a shade softer and the porch felt like a quiet pocket of the world. Farah breathed slowly and watched a single raindrop catch the house light as it fell from the roof edge. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Farah rested their head for a moment against Nana Ruth’s shoulder and listened to the steady rain until their eyes grew heavy with calm.
