A soft tick-tick came from the shelf by the door, so small that Nils almost missed it at first. Nils tilted their head and noticed the gentle sound at once, then stood quietly while cool air drifted in from the open courtyard and brushed the backs of their hands. The light in the room was low and honey-colored, and Nils could hear the kettle beginning its quiet tick as steam rose in a thin ribbon. Nils breathed in slowly with that soft kettle sound and felt their shoulders loosen. Grandpa Sol called from outside in a warm voice, and Nils padded across the smooth floorboards toward the long table waiting under the fig tree.

In the courtyard the evening air smelled clean and leafy, and the long wooden table stretched beneath the fig tree whose broad leaves made soft green shadows on the cloth. Nils ran one hand along the edge of a bench and felt the wood still holding a little daytime warmth. Veda was already there, sitting with knees tucked up, and Grandpa Sol moved slowly between plates and cups. Lantern light began to pool in small circles on the tabletop. Nils sat down near the middle so they could see both ends of the table and the low branches overhead. A light breeze stirred the fig leaves with a dry whisper that Nils liked.

Grandpa Sol set down a wide basket right in the center. Under a stripey cloth the basket held warm rolls, and a faint puff of yeast and butter rose when the cloth shifted. Nils leaned closer and felt the heat coming through the cloth against their palms. The rolls looked round and golden, each one nestled beside the next. Veda’s eyes grew round too. “Those look soft,” Veda said. Nils nodded and kept both hands resting lightly on the stripey cloth so the warmth stayed. The stones under the table still held a gentle chill that rose up through Nils’s socks.

Nils looked down the long table and saw empty plates waiting at every place. The thought came quietly that the basket of warm rolls under the stripey cloth would not feel fair if only one person was served first. Nils wanted Veda to have some at the same moment everyone else did. Grandpa Sol stood nearby with a calm face and did not reach for the cloth yet. Nils smelled a quick note of wet wool from the damp sweater hanging on the chair back where someone had left it after watering the plants. The scent was mild and clean, like rain on a coat, and it mixed with the bread smell without spoiling it.

Nils lifted the stripey cloth just enough to see inside, careful not to let the heat escape too fast. The rolls sat in a neat mound. Nils decided to make a smaller, even share so Veda would be included right away. With slow fingers Nils took one roll and broke it into tidy pieces, then another, setting the pieces on a clean plate in little groups that matched. “We can pass these around together,” Nils said. Veda watched and smiled. The paper edge of a folded napkin brushed Nils’s wrist as they worked, thin and slightly rough, and Nils smoothed it flat without looking away from the bread.

Grandpa Sol watched the small piles form and simply nodded once. He did not make a speech. He let the choice stand. Nils felt glad inside and kept breaking the rolls until every place had the same modest share. “Here is one for you, Veda,” Nils said, sliding a plate closer. “And one for Grandpa Sol, and one for me.” The tin clock on the side table gave a single clear tick that carried across the quiet courtyard, marking the moment without hurry. Soft lantern light caught the steam still rising from the pieces. Nils’s fingers felt the last bit of warmth leave the crust as they set the final share down.

Everyone began to eat the small even pieces. The rolls tasted mild and comforting, and Nils chewed slowly so the flavor lasted. Veda said, “Thank you for making them the same.” Nils answered, “I wanted us all to start together.” Grandpa Sol only smiled and took his share, the fig leaves above them rustling once in a warmer puff of air. Nils noticed how the temperature under the tree stayed gentle, never too cool, and how the long table felt steady beneath their elbows. Between bites Nils glanced at the basket, now lighter, the stripey cloth folded back like a quiet flag.

After the rolls were gone Nils helped gather the empty plates, stacking them with care so they made almost no clink. The courtyard stones had grown smoother underfoot as night settled. Grandpa Sol carried the basket inside while Veda and Nils stayed a moment longer under the fig tree. “The leaves look like little hands,” Veda whispered. Nils looked up and agreed in a soft voice, “They do.” A moth fluttered past a lantern and the light wavered once, warm and yellow on Nils’s cheek. Everything felt settled and kind, the long table empty but still friendly in the dusk.

Later Nils sat on the low step near the doorway with a cup of warm milk that steamed against their upper lip. The lantern by the window glowed softly. Grandpa Sol rested in the chair nearby, and Veda had already gone inside to wash. Nils listened to the last small night sounds and felt the milk heat travel down slowly. Their eyelids grew heavy in the kindest way. Nils set the cup aside, stretched once, and let the quiet courtyard hold them a little longer before sleep.
